In the past, I held onto a lot of fixed views. For example, as a straight man, I used to think I was a bigoted male chauvinist, believing I should take on all the responsibilities and always be the one to step forward. I remember when I was in a relationship, my fixed views often clashed with my girlfriend's. I didn't realize this until we were together. Suddenly, I realized I was actually quite conservative and held some pretty traditional views. I used to think I wasn't a bigoted male chauvinist, but it turned out I was. Now I think that's wrong. The reason is simple: I believed in what I understood, and my perspective was more from a male point of view, which led to a different understanding of women. The process of getting along also exposed some problems. The reason I clung to these views was because I was stuck in my own cognitive trap. I didn't listen to others' opinions, or even if I did, I still followed my own thoughts. So, thinking about it, I'm pretty unreliable!

As for why I didn't act on the views I believed in, it's actually quite interesting. If they're correct and I believe in them, why didn't I act? To be honest, it's just laziness. I used to think I was still capable, but the word "still capable" is really scary. Every time I say "still capable," it means I'm just getting by, being careless. If I rephrase it, what kind of things would I stick with and act on? For example, Li Xiaolai's words gave me a big shock. I realized how unreliable I really am. I don't believe I can't do it. It feels like a bit of a provocation, but mainly, it gave me a significant meaning. This is too important to me. For example, why do I want to join the practice group? It's for financial freedom, to be with excellent people, and to get rich quietly. It seems I'm still greedy, with an instinctive desire. More importantly, I assigned great significance to this, paying for seven years, which is a lifetime. I might have completely changed, at least I have a long-term mindset, can keep up with regular investments, and focus more on the future. There are more people walking with me!

Here are some common logical errors that ordinary people often make but rarely realize:

First, the straw man fallacy. This is when you distort someone's view to make it easier to attack them, making your argument seem more reasonable. For example, Xiao Ming says the government should increase the budget for education. Xiao Hong replies, "You're not patriotic at all, wanting to reduce defense spending so that foreign powers can take advantage." Xiao Hong has committed the straw man fallacy.

Second, appeal to emotion. This is when you use emotional resonance instead of factual evidence. For instance, Xiao Hong sees Xiao Ming eating dog meat at a restaurant and confronts him, saying, "How can you eat dog meat? Little dogs are so cute, like little kids. Can you bear to hurt little kids?" Xiao Hong should have provided evidence that eating dog meat is wrong, but instead, she used human feelings for children as her evidence. That's a logical fallacy.

Third, the slippery slope fallacy. This is when event A happening leads to the conclusion that extreme event B will happen, thus proving A shouldn't happen and affecting the objectivity of discussions about A. For example, Xiao Hong opposes same-sex marriage because she believes if we allow same-sex marriage, people might want to marry tables and chairs. This is a slippery slope fallacy. It seems like a reasonable analogy at first glance, but in reality, there's no connection between the two. There's no evidence that same-sex marriage would lead to people wanting to marry tables and chairs.

Fourth, the loaded question fallacy. This is when a question is phrased in a way that leads the respondent to answer in a specific way. For example, Xiao Hong suspects her husband is having an affair and asks him, "Does Xiao Li have a birthmark on her bottom?" This is a loaded question.

Fifth, the Texas sharpshooter fallacy. This is when you fire a shot and then draw a target around the bullet hole to prove you're a sharpshooter. For example, a public charity is questioned about fraud. To prove their sincerity, they publicize how much money they've donated and how many Hope Primary Schools they've built, but they never mention their extravagant spending on public funds. This charity has committed the Texas sharpshooter fallacy. This fallacy involves deciding your stance first and then finding evidence that supports it, while ignoring evidence that contradicts it.

Sixth, the middle ground fallacy. This is when you assume that the middle ground between two extreme views is correct. For example, Xiao Hong believes vaccines cause autism in children, Xiao Ming believes they don't, and Xiao Li believes the middle ground—that vaccines cause autism in some children but not all. This is the middle ground fallacy. Many people make this logical error when discussing issues like genetically modified organisms. In reality, the middle ground between truth and lies is still a lie.

" How to make a good mistake," "How to correctly make a mistake," and "How to make a correct mistake." Are errors still divided into correct and incorrect ones? A "correct mistake" is one that has a clear, simple, and effective algorithm for filtering and eliminating errors.

When you make this kind of mistake, it first means you were once "right." It's a bit like the World Cup knockout stage: if you're eliminated in the semifinals, it means you won in the quarterfinals. Many people's cognition gets stuck at a certain stage because they no longer make mistakes. Or, after a certain point, they no longer make "mistakes that count"—mistakes that have a simple, clear, impartial, and measurable algorithm for elimination. Only by filtering out the wrong can the right move forward.

There's a saying: "A ship without a direction has no wind that isn't against it." This can also be reversed: "A sail without a direction has no wind that isn't favorable." A correct wind is a meaningless correctness, and its opposite is a meaningless error. Cognition is the process of sinking errors to the bottom, ultimately resulting in a correct answer, with countless errors beneath it.
