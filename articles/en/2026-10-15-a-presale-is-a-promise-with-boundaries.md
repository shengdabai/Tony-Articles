# A Presale Is a Promise with Boundaries

> Advance publication date: 2026-10-15 · 2026-10-15 advance edition · Material current through 2026-10-14 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-15-预售是一份有边界的承诺.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-15-a-presale-is-a-promise-with-boundaries.md)

---

In 2017, the email product Superhuman asked people who had recently used it how disappointed they would be if they could no longer have it. At first, 22% said “very disappointed.” Founder Rahul Vohra later wrote that the team identified the people who cared most about the product and kept improving it. Three quarters later, the share had risen to 58%. [Those figures come from Vohra's account](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/). They do not prove that a survey can guarantee a successful business. They remind me of a gap that is easy to miss: getting someone to try a product is a long way from knowing what you owe that person.

An AI summary of a recent startup interview raised the idea of selling before building: get a paying order, then let the product grow out of a real need. I see the attraction. It gets us out of our rooms and away from imaginary customers. But I would add the sentence that tends to go missing: **the customer must be able to understand what they are buying at the point of sale; whatever remains uncertain is the seller's learning task.** Payment can make a conversation more serious. It cannot quietly move all the uncertainty onto the person paying.

## Payment makes a promise specific

Imagine someone who wants to build an AI tool for organizing a creator's reader emails. The tool does not exist yet. But the seller can already organize one week's messages by hand for one creator, sort them into agreed categories, flag the ones requiring a personal reply, and deliver an editable list by an agreed date. They can sell that manual service first, then watch which steps are worth turning into software. The customer pays for a visible service, not for a smart assistant that might exist once the seller has figured everything out.

Those descriptions sound close. Their consequences are far apart. With the first, both parties can name the deliverable, the deadline, the material the seller may use, and who will fix an error. With the second, the seller's development schedule becomes the customer's risk. If the idea changes, the seller can say they learned something while the customer still lacks what they believed they bought. Learning matters to a founder. The customer is not obliged to pay the whole tuition bill.

In [“Do Things that Don't Scale,”](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) Paul Graham describes founders personally recruiting early users and sometimes doing by hand what they hope to automate later. Stripe's founders would set up willing users on the spot. Viaweb's team built online stores for merchants itself. Graham's 2013 essay draws on selected early companies; it is not a measured success rate for every industry. It supports a simple move: do the work yourself while you still can, because that is how you see exactly where a customer gets stuck.

The same essay contains a warning. Once founders charge specifically for extra attention, customers reasonably treat that attention as something they have purchased and expect it to be delivered. That matters even more now. AI can speed up a manual service, and delivering the service may teach you what to automate. Once you have taken payment, “I'm experimenting” cannot stand in for “Here is what I promised.”

A presale therefore has two ledgers. The customer ledger says what I will deliver this time, when I will deliver it, and what counts as failing to do so. The learning ledger says which need, process, or judgment I hope to understand better through the work. The ledgers can inform each other, but they cannot be merged. The customer-facing promise should still hold if the next version of the product is never built.

## A paying customer can still be buying something other than your idea

Payment tells us more than a like. If someone will allocate a budget to a problem now, that problem has a cost for them. Still, one order does not tell us how many people want the product, which part of it this buyer values, or whether the next buyer wants the same thing. It shows that one person accepted one exchange under one set of conditions.

Superhuman's account makes the distinction concrete. The team initially surveyed people who had recently experienced the core product, rather than people who had merely seen a marketing page. They looked closely at those who would be “very disappointed” without it and asked what those people valued. They then paid particular attention to users who loved the product's speed but were held back by something else. A missing mobile app emerged as a major obstacle. The lesson is not to copy a percentage. It is to connect different answers to different decisions: protect the speed that committed users love; remove a condition that stops interested users from relying on the product. [Vohra's account](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/) records both the decisions and how the group being measured was selected.

Return to the hypothetical email service. Suppose it gets its first order. Does the buyer mainly value having someone decide each day which messages require a personal reply? Or do they mainly want every message reduced to a one-line summary? Those are different products. If the seller remembers only “someone paid,” they may mistake the feature they most want to build for the benefit the buyer wants to purchase. After payment, the seller still has to observe how the deliverable is used and ask which part relieved a burden and which part created another one.

It is a little like paying to try a dish while a cook is still refining the menu. Payment means you wanted this meal. It does not mean you want every dish the cook dreams of serving when the restaurant opens. The listed meal must arrive; your response can shape a later menu. The comparison helps separate commitment from exploration. A software service also brings privacy, maintenance, and responsibility for mistakes, so “tasting a dish” does not settle those obligations.

Before taking money, I would write two short statements: “This purchase guarantees...” and “I am trying to learn....” The first is fit to show the customer. The second is for the seller. If the first cannot be fulfilled unless the second succeeds, the offer is too broad for a presale. Narrow it. Offer manual sorting of a defined set of material by a specified date, for example. Once that result proves useful, talk about automation. There is no need to sell a future feature in advance.

## The better AI gets at delivery, the more the seller must learn firsthand

Someone might object that AI can already draft proposals, classify messages, and build interfaces. Why put so much weight on doing the work manually? When the technology is reliable for a repeatable step, using it makes sense. The trouble is counting “this job was delivered smoothly” and “I know how to judge the next job” as the same result. The product can come together quickly while the seller still cannot explain why a customer stays or leaves.

A [2025 survey of knowledge workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) gathered responses from 319 people who regularly used generative AI at work and 936 examples of their tasks. Greater confidence in AI on a particular task was associated with less self-reported critical thinking on that task. Respondents also described more of their thinking as checking information, integrating answers, and keeping the task on course. The study measured reports and associations. It did not track whether founders' abilities actually deteriorated. It still suggests a practical question for a seller: when the deliverable looks complete, can I explain the choices that matter?

In a [randomized undergraduate experiment updated in 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849v2), students learned an unfamiliar subject and wrote an essay under a time limit. They then took unaided assessments, including another about a week later. The group allowed to use AI learned more overall. Students who used it to explain concepts showed more durable gains; those who delegated the writing did not show the same advantage in their later unaided essays. Students chose how to use AI, so comparisons among types of users are not clean causal tests. Nor was this a startup experiment. It helps me state the question precisely: getting through this task with a tool and carrying the experience into the next task are separate outcomes.

Learning also takes more than ending a project with the words “improve next time.” In a [2016 experiment on transfer across subjects](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full), participants read material, practiced recalling it, received feedback, and later applied the underlying idea to a question in a different domain. Retrieval practice beat rereading. When the researchers added a condition that reread the material and then focused on the key information, part of the original advantage was accounted for by that focused exposure. The participants were psychology undergraduates, and the outcomes were learning tasks, not business results. The usable move is narrower: after serving someone yourself, write down the specific obstacle they revealed, then see whether you recognize it earlier on the next delivery.

Suppose the AI sorting tool repeatedly files politely worded complaints as routine questions. The seller should correct the classification. But stopping at “the model needs improvement” misses the lesson. Which words, earlier messages, or subsequent customer actions show that this complaint needs a personal response? Turn the answer into a rule that can be checked against the next batch of messages. The rule may fail. That is useful too: failure shows which condition is missing. The customer bought an organizing service. The cost of learning the rule belongs to the seller.

## Sometimes taking payment first is a bad idea

The strongest objection to caution is straightforward: customers often describe their needs more seriously after paying. Free opinions can be cheap, and a founder can hide indefinitely in product development. I agree that payment filters out some polite praise. I also agree that development without contact with customers can go on far too long. If you can reliably deliver a limited result by hand, a presale may be an honest and efficient test.

But for work where errors can cause serious harm, such as medical, legal, or financial actions, a demo that can be made quickly is far from a service ready to sell. Payment cannot produce a missing license, an unconfirmed third-party integration, or permission to process someone else's sensitive material. Even for low-stakes work, if the seller lacks time to deliver the promised manual fallback, a presale is a bet on the future. Interviews, a sample, or an internal rehearsal are better at that stage because the promise has no dependable base yet.

There is another easy-to-miss limit to Vohra's case: Superhuman surveyed people who had already used the product and refined the group it aimed to serve. The move from 22% to 58% says its measure improved among a selected group. It does not mean any founder can select customers and expect the same outcome. A conversion rate from one presale is no substitute for observing delivery quality, repeat purchases, or what customers actually gain. Numbers can be comforting. Their denominator often changes the story.

For me, the condition for a presale fits in one question: **if the planned new feature is delayed, can the customer still receive a useful result under today's agreement?** If the answer is no, narrow the offer first. If it is yes, ask what you need to learn during delivery and how the customer can say “That is wrong” when necessary. Those questions take a little more work than “Can I charge now?” They can save a much more expensive misunderstanding later.

## Write a small promise tonight

You do not have to build a product tonight. You do not have to charge anyone, either. Pick a small service you hope AI might improve and rehearse a presale on paper. Think of the written offer as the price tag a customer sees in a shop. It should say what the buyer receives. It should not present a skill the shopkeeper has yet to learn as a dependable service. The tag can define the exchange, but it cannot replace a record of what happens after delivery.

1. Write the delivery sentence first: for whom, using which input, by when, and producing what checkable result? If the sentence relies on an untested AI feature, limit it to the part you can perform yourself now.
2. Write the failure sentence next: what would count as not delivering, and would you redo the work, refund the buyer, or stop taking orders? Specify who decides and what permission you need, especially when the material belongs to someone else.
3. Write the learning sentence for yourself: what do I think the customer cares about most? After the first delivery, what behavior will I observe, or what question will I ask, that could overturn my guess? Let the next similar delivery test your conclusion. Do not collect praise alone.

When you finish, notice which sentence was hardest to write. It probably names the boundary you have not settled yet. Work that out before you invite someone to pay.
