# Regulating the Business Cycle

Why does the government regulate the business cycle?

The government regulates the business cycle to control inflation and employment rates. It aims to prevent the economy from growing too fast or too slowly. The economy has cycles, and these cycles can go to extremes—too weak an economy leads to unemployment, while too strong an economy inevitably leads to a future recession. Neither is desirable, so a significant part of the central bank and the Ministry of Finance's work is managing the cycle, which is known as countercyclical regulation.

How does the government regulate the business cycle?

The government mainly regulates the business cycle through fiscal policy, including tax policy and government spending policy. When the government wants to stimulate economic growth, it can cut taxes and increase government spending. When it believes the economy is growing too fast, it raises taxes and reduces spending. Central banks primarily control inflation rates. A central bank that acts quickly to control inflation is called anhawkish (hawkish) central bank, while one that acts quickly to stimulate economic growth and promote employment is called adovish (dovish) central bank. The best approach is to balance between hawkish and dovish policies, controlling inflation while ensuring employment and maintaining steady, healthy economic growth.

The Ministry of Finance's focus is on managing the business cycle through tax policy and government spending policy, ensuring that the economy does not grow too fast or too slowly. A fiscal deficit: often used, when the economy is weak, government spending exceeds the fiscal revenue mainly derived from taxes, increasing the fiscal deficit to expand government spending and stimulate demand. A fiscal surplus: rare, when the economy is strong, government spending should be less than fiscal revenue, increasing the fiscal surplus.

Is government regulation of the business cycle effective? What is the effect?

The effectiveness of government regulation of the business cycle is limited. Government intervention is often out of sync with the business cycle's trend; managing these intervention tools is far from easy. If it were easy, we wouldn't see so many extreme fluctuations in the business cycle. The government can stimulate the economy through fiscal policy by increasing government spending, or it can slow down economic growth by increasing taxes. However, the government's money isn't just windfall—it comes from tax revenue. When the government urgently needs money to push the economy, tax revenue often starts to decline, so it wants to spend but lacks the funds. Eventually, it has to issue government bonds, increasing the fiscal deficit. But how much government debt is reasonable remains a subject of debate. If the government overborrows, people will worry—what will you use to repay it? They say it will be repaid through future taxes, but it seems that deficit spending has never really stopped. That is, each year, income is less than spending, and thus debt keeps growing.

> Since the business cycle can go to extremes during upturns and downturns, the regulatory tools to address these extremes should be countercyclical. We can apply them according to their own cycles.
