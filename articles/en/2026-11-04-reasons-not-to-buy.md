# Reasons Not to Buy Belong in a Product Description

> 发布日期:2026-11-04 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-04-不买的理由，也属于产品说明.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-04-reasons-not-to-buy.md)

---

When you introduce your AI tool, are you willing to tell someone when they would be better off keeping their current method?

I would count that as part of the product's capability. As features become easier to build, a product description needs to help people choose: where their current method is adequate, what the new tool improves, and what outcome would undermine the reason to buy. Making the conditions for declining specific gives us a chance to make the case for buying credible.

That does not guarantee more sales. What interests me is whether customers can make a decision with a little less reliance on the seller's enthusiasm and a little more reliance on facts they can check themselves. For someone who wants to keep working on a product over time, this deserves care. Allowing people to decline means accepting that they have other options. It also means explaining when those options are better, rather than choosing only comparisons that favor your own product.

## Make room for the current method

01 Value needs something to be compared with. Two summaries of saved articles prompted this question for me today: one distinguished products built around short-lived opportunities from products built around lasting reputation; the other discussed helping people recognize the value of your work. Connecting them, I think helping someone understand what you have made does not finish the explanation: they still need to know what they gain by adopting it instead of continuing as they are. Without that step, a more impressive introduction may simply make the decision depend more on how much they like you; liking you can earn their attention, but it cannot establish what to expect from actually using the product.

02 Customers have reasons to keep their current method. It may lack a polished interface, yet it already gets the job done and they know where trouble can arise; switching requires preparing materials again, learning the tool, and checking its output. I think a product description should include those efforts, rather than comparing only the small stretch where the new tool runs most smoothly. Suppose I build an AI meeting-summary tool, while a customer only needs to write down decisions and the people responsible for them: a longer, tidier record may offer no reason to switch, and the price tag alone cannot tell us whether the change is cheap, because the effort of switching is a cost too.

03 Producing more does not necessarily help more. A March 2023 [working paper on professional writing tasks](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf) studied 444 college-educated professionals: the group given access to ChatGPT took about 37% less time than the control group and received higher evaluator scores. This version was marked not peer-reviewed, meaning it had not undergone formal assessment by other researchers in the field; the tasks were short and self-contained, and the experiment did not measure long-term skills or complex work processes. My takeaway is that as some outputs become easier to obtain, a product needs to explain the additional step it solves, rather than merely displaying an abundance of output—or pretending customers do not already have options that can help them.

04 Understand the old method before offering a serious alternative. In Paul Graham's [2013 essay on building startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Viaweb approached merchants about its store-building software; some did not want to use it themselves but were willing to let the founders build stores for them. They took on that work, discovered missing features while building, added them, and continued. This is a participant's retrospective account, not a result that represents every startup, but the process is concrete: serving merchants exposed the developers to real tasks and clarified what needed to be added, while the way they encouraged adoption shifted from asking merchants to learn software to getting the merchants' work done.

05 Doing the work for someone can reveal what you are asking them to take on. Following Viaweb's experience, I care less about how hard the founders worked than about the opportunity they had to discover the distance between the result merchants wanted and the work the software required of them. Returning to the meeting-summary tool, preparing recordings, adding context, and checking omissions all belong in the comparison; counting only the time after someone clicks Generate leaves preparation and cleanup off the record. That inference makes me want a value proposition to specify where the work starts and when it counts as finished, especially who supplies missing context and who confirms it.

## Leave room for facts to challenge the promise

06 A promise should let a customer decide that it does not hold. I would narrow the meeting-summary tool's description to a specific use: helping people who need a record of the discussion organize a draft, while decisions and responsibilities still require checking; someone who only needs an action list can keep their current method. There is no need to rank these needs as more or less sophisticated, because different needs produce different benefits. With that much made explicit, customers can identify which situation is theirs, and developers do not have to treat every nonbuyer as someone who failed to understand; the conversation can move from whether the product is impressive to what each person needs to accomplish.

07 Checkable conditions expose disagreement better than adjectives do. In their [2014 paper on forecasting tournaments](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---forecasting-tournaments-tools-for-increasing-transparency-and-improving-debate.pdf), Tetlock and colleagues discuss how making forecasts explicit and recording outcomes helps people compare records of judgment; their work concerns particular event-forecasting settings and did not test product descriptions. The methodological connection I find useful is to agree in advance on the task, the result, and when to review it, rather than asking customers whether a tool is good. Product promises also involve personal preferences and cannot all become forecasts, but claims about effort and accuracy can be clearer: saving time and removing something that needs checking are different promises, and should not be bundled into a vague claim of efficiency.

08 Clear conditions tell us what a refusal means. With the same meeting-summary tool, one person may not need a record of the discussion, another may need it but lack usable input, and a third may try it and find checking harder than writing the record themselves. Those situations call for different responses: change whom you serve, improve preparation of the input, or rework the feature. Without those conditions, developers who record only that a customer said they did not need it can reach for the same sales pitch every time; more energetic persuasion adds no understanding, while recording where the refusal arose helps determine whether a lower price could solve anything at all.

09 Someone could reasonably say that leading with who a product is unsuitable for will cost sales. An early product's uses are still emerging, developers may not know its eventual purpose, and an overly cautious description could turn people away before they try it. That concern should remain: treating today's conditions as permanent conclusions can close off discovery, and waiting for long-term evidence has a real cost for products whose opportunity is short-lived—we cannot require everyone to choose the same kind of business. There is another problem with drawing the boundary too narrowly: it may leave only the easiest people to serve, making results look better than they will after wider adoption, so praise from those who tried it needs to be considered alongside reasons given by those who declined.

10 Boundaries can change; the cost of exploring should be clear beforehand. An early product can invite customers to explore, distinguish observations from the developer's guesses, and agree on a limited commitment, leaving room for unexpected uses while letting people know they are participating in an experiment. A heat rating on a menu cannot decide whether you will enjoy a dish, but it can reduce the chance of ordering something you were unprepared for; the rating corresponds to a product's conditions of use, and the diner to the customer. A restaurant does not need to prove that people who avoid spicy food have poor taste, and a developer need not interpret every refusal as backwardness; unlike a fixed menu label, however, a product's stated range needs to change as needs, software, and observed results change.

11 When the alternative changes, the old advantage needs to be recalculated. In his [2010 analysis of Iridium](https://steveblank.com/2010/11/01/no-business-plan-survives-first-contact-with-a-customer-%E2%80%93-the-5-2-billion-dollar-mistake/), Steve Blank argues that ordinary mobile service changed in coverage and price while the project was being developed, without a corresponding revision of the original customer assumptions. This is retrospective analysis and cannot establish that another approach would necessarily have succeeded. What matters to me is the comparison: your feature may not have deteriorated while customers' other options have improved, so a once-honest description can still offer an outdated reason to buy; I would treat alternatives becoming substantially cheaper, or formerly difficult steps disappearing, as reasons to review the description, rather than reviewing it only when I release a feature.

## Make a refusal useful for the next improvement

12 A lasting reputation has to hold up on the next use. I do not want to understand reputation simply as the number of times other people say nice things about me; I care more about whether a customer can return with expectations formed last time and have them met under similar conditions. Defining what a product does not do helps maintain those expectations: if the meeting-summary tool has not promised to make decisions for people, an omission need not be the first moment the user discovers that we meant different things by finished. This is a standard I want for a long-term product, not a basis for claiming that the materials cited here establish it as a more profitable way to sell; nor should a promise depend on customers repeatedly explaining it on my behalf—if I keep having to add that they should have understood, the wording itself needs work.

13 Disclosing boundaries has costs too. Competitors may learn whom you serve and which problem you focus on, and may copy your description; customers may also mistake a current limitation for something the product will never do. I still favor disclosing conditions that affect the buying decision, because concealing them transfers the cost to the user. That does not require disclosing implementation details, untested directions, or whose data is involved: giving customers grounds for a decision is different from revealing everything, and what they need to decide can define the disclosure boundary without making confidentiality an excuse to omit necessary explanations.

14 Start by changing a small part of the description today, without rebuilding the product. Choose a feature you are recommending, preparing to charge for, or already giving people to use, and write down the task it addresses, the customer's current method, and a situation where that method is sufficient. Use only materials you are entitled to handle and choose a small task where errors are easy to recover from; save the result of the existing method before having the new tool process the same material. For the meeting-summary tool, check whether decisions and responsible people can be found, rather than substituting length or tidy formatting for that result, and write the conditions before starting so satisfaction afterward does not quietly rewrite the original problem.

15 Then record the whole effort. Include preparing the material, waiting for generation, adding context, checking results, and correcting omissions, while noting which quality defects would make the saved time meaningless. If the existing method works better, keep that result rather than changing the scoring to something the new tool happens to do well; if the new tool works better, record the preparation it depended on. One comparison cannot separate chance from consistency, so try again when another suitable piece of material comes along to uncover problems absent from the first attempt, and do not assume that materials an experienced user already has ready are conditions everyone naturally enjoys.

16 Finally, ask the other person for a reason not to adopt it. Do not supply the desired answer or rush to argue: first see whether the description lets them identify a condition relevant to their own task; if they can only repeat smarter or more efficient, it has not given them enough grounds for choosing. Judge this small experiment by whether it leads you to change a specific promise, add an overlooked cost, or acknowledge a class of tasks for which you should not yet recommend the tool. A customer choosing to keep the old method may mean the description did its job; this checks whether the description helped someone choose, and one positive response cannot validate a sales method.

I want to keep a question for myself as well: which part of the customer's judgment does this description spare them, and which piece of work does it quietly leave them carrying?

Today, start by removing an adjective and adding a condition under which declining the product would be entirely reasonable. Have a conversation with that version, then decide what needs changing next.

Afterward, keep the original wording and the reason for the change. If the person declines, record which condition did not fit, without supplying a reason you would prefer them to have; if they agree, do not count polite approval as an intention to buy. When a similar task actually comes up again, whether they use the description to make a choice deserves further observation. Let a small piece of writing face that test before rushing to give the whole method a name.
