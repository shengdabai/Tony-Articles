# Who Will Train the Next Skilled Worker?

> Advance publication date: 2026-10-16 · Sources collected through 2026-10-14 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-16-谁来培养下一位熟手.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-16-who-will-train-the-next-skilled-worker.md)

2026-10-16 advance edition · Sources collected through 2026-10-14

Paul Graham recalls that, in Viaweb's early days, some merchants did not want to build their own online stores. The team used its own software to build the stores for them. In the middle of a job, someone would discover a missing feature, implement it, and then return to the merchant's store. Graham's [account](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), written in 2013, describes how founders learned from serving customers directly. It is no universal recipe for startup success. But it raises a question that reaches beyond startups: if AI takes over the basic work, where will the next skilled worker learn? If checking shows that AI saved time, that is a gain. If the old work also provided practice and the team has arranged no replacement, the lost opportunity may be a cost.

## What have we saved?

Time, and perhaps practice along with it.

An AI summary of a piece about future human resources trends raises a pointed question: if AI takes over drafting, sorting, and checking entry-level work, how do new workers learn to judge? It is a machine summary of collected material, not an account of my own workplace or proof that any particular industry has already changed. Still, the question matters. We may treat the output of a task and the learning it produces as a single thing. Once the output can be automated, we need to check separately whether the practice attached to the old work still happens.

Sorting information may seem to mean putting items into neat categories. A person doing it also encounters duplicates, contradictions, and outdated entries. Sometimes the old categories cannot hold what the person finds. If an experienced colleague explains why one item matters and why another conclusion goes too far, the worker receives feedback. The deliverable may be a tidy table; the learning happens while sorting, making mistakes, and revising. That is my account of a possible work mechanism, not a finding proven by a saved article. Nor is every hour of mechanical sorting educational.

Think of preparing ingredients in a kitchen. Someone cutting vegetables gets the cutting done, but may also learn to notice spoiled sections and how thickness affects cooking. A machine can cut evenly and dinner can still be served. If the kitchen wants to train a future cook, it must arrange another way for that person to handle ingredients, judge their condition, and be corrected. Office work has no equivalent to the feel of an ingredient, and cutting vegetables does not amount to professional judgment. The similarity is narrower: an easy task can do production and training at once. Remove the production task, and the training needs a new place to happen.

That is why I hesitate to ask only how much time AI saves employees. I would also ask: who learned what during that time before? If nobody can give a concrete answer, the work may have been pure repetition. Automate it. If the answer names a skill that will be needed later, the saved time includes a training cost. Combine both effects into one efficiency figure and the figure will look excellent. Someone else may eventually have to pay the missing bill.

## Where does practice happen?

Between an attempt and a correction.

Drafting a recommendation, comparing options, or checking a document does not automatically make anyone better. A person can copy an old template for years and merely learn to copy faster. Practice requires a specific target: what judgment should improve this time? After making an attempt, the learner needs to know where it fell short and have a chance to try again. In their [review of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), Ericsson and Harwell explain the strict meaning of the term: a qualified teacher sets exercises for what an individual needs to improve, identifies a specific gap, and has the learner revise and try again. Published in 2019, the paper interprets and debates earlier research. It is not a new experiment proving that a particular office training program will work.

So I am not trying to preserve a certain number of manual reports. I want to preserve opportunities to exercise judgment with feedback. If an old job only required copying and pasting, nobody examined the process, and nobody explained the mistakes, little is lost when the job disappears. But if a mundane checking task helped a newcomer spot the gap between a document and reality, and an experienced person explained why that gap mattered, its value cannot be measured by completion speed alone. Both jobs take time. Their value as practice differs.

A [survey of knowledge workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) offers a further warning. Knowledge workers mainly work with information; generative AI here means tools that create text or other content from a prompt. In their 2025 report, Lee and colleagues describe 936 task examples from 319 people who used such tools at work. Participants described their thinking as moving from making material themselves toward verifying information, integrating outputs, and overseeing tasks. The study relies on people's descriptions of their own behavior. It did not directly measure their abilities years later and cannot establish that AI caused a decline in judgment. What it suggests to me is that a move from doing to reviewing is not automatically an upgrade in learning.

Reviewing already takes knowledge. A newcomer who does not know what a recommendation might omit may be able to check only spelling and formatting in a fluent answer. Someone familiar with the constraints of the work might notice why an apparently coherent argument does not fit the case. Asking everyone to perform final review while handing the entire process of forming a judgment to a machine is like asking people to taste the food before they have learned to recognize flavors. Tasting matters. The ability to notice the difference has to come from somewhere.

Should we deliberately let newcomers make mistakes on consequential work, then? No. The useful move is to practice on material where a mistake has limited consequences. Have the learner offer an explanation before seeing AI's explanation, then use a knowledgeable person or a reliable standard to show the difference. Learning requires visible differences and timely correction, not reckless exposure to risk. The greater the stakes of real delivery, the more important it is to keep the practice setting separate from the decision that affects someone else.

## Should people do it all?

Of course not.

Someone might object that customers need accurate, timely work; they did not agree to fund a company's trainees. That objection deserves a serious answer. If mistakes are costly and a reliable tool performs the task faster and more consistently, making a newcomer handle live delivery alone would be irresponsible. If a small team has rapid turnover and does not expect to need a particular skill, maintaining an expensive training program may make little sense. My proposal therefore has a condition: the team must actually need people who can exercise independent judgment in the future, and the old work must actually have helped them practice that judgment.

Today's faster delivery cannot settle that longer-term question. In [experiments on recalling learned material](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf), Andrew Butler had participants read short passages and then either study them again or try to recall what they had learned and receive feedback. The four experiments used different tests a week later: Experiment 1a repeated questions participants had practiced, Experiments 1b and 2 used new questions requiring reasoning within the same subject, and Experiment 3 used new reasoning questions from other subjects. The results available here show that the repeated recall group performed better than the restudy group on the repeated questions in 1a and the new questions in 1b and 2. Experiment 3's results are not available here, so these results do not establish its specific outcome. The 2010 study used controlled passages and test questions. It offers a clue for designing practice; it does not establish that the same procedure will produce skilled workers in any real job. It does make one distinction hard to ignore: rereading information and being able to use it on a new problem are different achievements.

Return to Viaweb. The lesson is not that its founders should have built every merchant's store by hand forever. Their hands-on work exposed missing features. They could later automate repetitive steps, provided the team retained a way to encounter the places where customers got stuck. In the same essay, Graham writes that Stripe's founders would set up the product on the spot for people willing to try it. These are founders' observations, not randomized trials. Going to customers in person may be unsuitable in other markets or under different compliance rules. I draw a narrower principle from the cases: before automating an action, find out what that action has been helping you notice.

Likewise, a team can ask AI to sort most documents while choosing a small set of cases for newcomers to study. It can let a machine produce a draft but have the learner write down a key judgment before reading it. An experienced colleague can take a real piece of rework and explain why the first judgment failed. These arrangements do not require preserving manual work for its own sake. They provide a chance to decide independently, see what happened, and revise the decision. Delivery and training can be organized separately, as long as training is more than a slogan on the wall.

## How should we count the cost?

Treat training as a real cost.

An ordinary efficiency calculation compares how long a task took before and after automation. A more careful one adds checking, error correction, and rework. If the team needs a continuing supply of people who can handle exceptions independently, it should add another question: which old task gave them practice, what will replace it, and whose time will provide feedback? There is no need to attach a made-up cash value to human judgment. Recording the time, owner, and observed training result is more useful than inventing an impressive return figure.

I would ask three questions of a task proposed for automation. Does it actually train an ability that someone will later have to use unaided? Name the ability and a behavior that would show it, rather than saying it develops general excellence. Once AI does the task, where will a newcomer meet the same sort of problem and give a reasoned answer before the result is revealed? Finally, who will point to a specific flaw in that reason and give the learner another piece of material to try? A project may still be worthwhile if the team cannot answer these questions. It should not claim to have solved training as well.

There is a promising possibility on the other side of the ledger: AI can also help create practice. It can produce comparison answers, vary exercise material, and offer preliminary feedback. Work that nobody previously had time to arrange might become practical. But an answer generated by AI is not necessarily a trustworthy answer. Someone must check the important cases, and feedback must address a specific judgment. Rehearsing with a system that may be confidently wrong does not become skill merely through repetition.

The useful comparison is broader than two reports labeled “with AI” and “without AI.” For a similar kind of work, examine delivery quality, rework, training effort, and whether the learner handles a new case independently next time. The first pair concerns today's efficiency; the second asks whether someone will still be able to exercise judgment tomorrow. A team may choose to spend everything on immediate delivery. It can say so plainly. It should not describe lost practice as a costless windfall. And where a team has no lasting need for a skill, the training cost can be low or even absent. The conditions determine the bill.

## Where will the next worker learn?

In a protected attempt with feedback.

Here is a training exercise I propose. Its results have not been established by the research cited here. Choose a recurring task where a mistake in the exercise will have limited consequences, and take a fresh piece of material that has not yet been processed. Ask the learner to write a judgment, the reasons for it, and the point they are least sure of before seeing AI's answer. Then have someone who knows the work identify what both answers missed. After some time, repeat the exercise with different material. Do not record only who was right on the first attempt. Ask whether, on the new material, the learner spots the important difference sooner. If no experienced colleague is available, check against an original source or an explicit outcome standard. If neither exists, AI's agreement is not feedback.

This small experiment can fail. If the learner can only repeat AI's phrasing and cannot explain the reason after another attempt, narrow the task, teach the missing basics, or admit that nobody can yet provide adequate feedback. If practice interrupts work where errors carry serious consequences, move it to completed cases instead of making a customer bear the risk. If the machine genuinely helps the learner understand mistakes faster and perform more steadily on new material, keep using it. I am not defending ownership of an old task. I want someone to have practiced by the time human judgment is needed again.

The next skilled worker will not appear by default.
