# Scale No Longer Requires Sameness

> 发布日期:2026-09-07 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-07-真正的规模化，不再要求所有人一样.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-07-scale-without-sameness.md)

---

This morning, one detail stopped me.

A Phase 3 trial had enrolled 1,137 people, but the experimental component was not simply the same medicine copied 1,137 times. It was designed from the mutations in each person’s tumor. One trial, one production system, and more than a thousand individualized treatments.

I am not a medical researcher, and the therapy is still investigational. The companies announced positive topline results in August 2026, while detailed data and regulatory review still lie ahead. What caught my attention was not a promise that cancer had been solved. It was the shape of the production system.

For a long time, scale has meant sameness. Make one mold, one lesson, one workflow, or one piece of software, then distribute as many copies as possible. Variation is expensive, so the system asks people to adapt to the product.

Now a different kind of scale is becoming possible: keep the process stable while allowing every output to be different.

That sounds like a small change. I think it changes the direction of the entire machine.

## Scale used to erase difference

The old industrial bargain was straightforward. Standardization lowered cost, reduced error, and made quality easier to inspect. A factory could not improvise a new process for every customer. A school could not assign a private teacher to every student. A small software team could not build a separate application for every user.

So we standardized the output.

The same product reached many people. The same syllabus moved a room of students at one speed. The same interface asked every user to learn its menus. This created enormous prosperity, but it also hid a compromise: when the system could not afford to understand each person, the person had to become legible to the system.

“Personalization” often softened only the surface. A message used your first name. A recommendation engine rearranged the same catalog. A course offered three difficulty levels. The underlying product remained largely unchanged.

The individualized mRNA treatment in the trial points to a more demanding idea. According to the [official trial announcement](https://www.merck.com/news/merck-and-moderna-announce-phase-3-interpath-001-trial-of-intismeran-autogene-plus-keytruda-met-endpoints-of-recurrence-free-survival-rfs-and-distant-metastasis-free-survival-dmfs-in-patient/), a tumor sample is used to identify a patient’s unique mutational signature. The resulting synthetic mRNA can encode up to 34 selected neoantigens. The study met its recurrence-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival endpoints, making it the first positive Phase 3 readout for an individualized neoantigen therapy. The same announcement is careful to call the treatment investigational and says the companies plan to present the full data and engage regulators.

The crucial point is easy to miss. The researchers did not make personalization scalable by abandoning standards. They made it scalable by moving the standards.

The output varies, but the chain does not become arbitrary: collect a sample, sequence it, identify mutations, select targets, design the mRNA, manufacture it, check it, deliver it, and track the result. A [May 2026 explanation of the approach](https://www.merck.com/stories/individualized-neoantigen-therapies/) describes the ambition plainly: one medicine for one patient.

The content changes. The rails remain.

Personalization itself is not new. Tailors, tutors, doctors, and craftspeople have adapted their work to individuals for a very long time. What is changing is the cost structure. Bespoke work used to scale mainly by adding more skilled labor. Now part of the work—reading many kinds of input, translating differences into specifications, and generating a first result—can be handled computationally while the same quality process is reused.

## Standardize the rails, not the cargo

It would be tempting to call this an AI breakthrough. That would be too simple.

AI and algorithms help interpret biological variation and select candidate targets, but they are only one layer. Sequencing, mRNA engineering, manufacturing, quality control, clinical operations, and logistics all have to work together. If any one of those links fails, a clever model produces nothing useful.

This is why I find the example valuable beyond medicine. It offers a better engineering question.

Instead of asking, “How can we make one answer serve everyone?” ask, “Which parts must remain fixed so the answer is allowed to differ?”

Consider learning. A scalable course usually fixes the content: everyone watches the same explanation, completes the same exercise, and advances on the same schedule. An AI tutor can easily decorate that model with friendly chat while leaving the logic untouched.

A genuinely individualized learning system would standardize something else: diagnose the learner’s current model, choose a task just beyond it, observe the attempt, compare the result with a clear standard, and update the next task. One learner may need a concrete example. Another may need to explain the idea without notes. A third may understand the concept but fail when the context changes.

The reliable learning loop stays the same. The next lesson does not.

The same applies to creative work. If I ask AI to turn every idea into the same polished five-part article, I have scaled a template, not my thinking. Real personalization means my evidence, doubts, previous decisions, intended reader, and standard of quality can alter the result. The publishing checks can be fixed—verify sources, remove private information, test the counterargument, check every link—without forcing the thought itself into one mold.

For a personal AI workbench, this distinction matters even more. The durable asset is not one universal prompt. It is a stable process that can accept changing context: what I am trying to do, what I already know, what I refuse to trade away, what failed last time, and what counts as finished today.

The workbench scales when it can handle more difference without losing control.

## Not every difference deserves automation

There is a trap here. Individualized does not automatically mean better.

A bad recommendation can be precisely tailored. A learning system can become so responsive to current preferences that it never asks a student to struggle. A writing tool can imitate a person’s habits so faithfully that it preserves every weakness. More variables can create the appearance of care while making the system impossible to understand or audit.

Real personalization therefore needs a shared quality standard. In the medical case, unique treatments still enter a controlled trial with common endpoints. Difference is allowed in the treatment design, not in the demand for evidence.

That principle travels well.

A learner can take a different path, but “I feel I understand” is not enough; the learner still has to retrieve, explain, apply, or build something. A creator can use a different voice, but factual claims still need evidence. An AI agent can choose a different route, but permissions, budgets, tests, and rollback conditions should remain explicit.

The goal is not infinite customization. It is useful variation inside a trustworthy system.

There is another limit. Some differences cannot yet be translated into reliable instructions. A person may say, “This does not feel like me,” without being able to explain why. That feeling matters, but a production system cannot learn much from it until someone turns it into a comparison, a boundary, or an observable signal.

AI lowers the cost of generating different outputs. The harder work is still deciding which differences matter and how to verify them.

## A four-question test

I now use four questions to examine any task that claims to be personalized:

- What must stay the same for the result to be safe and trustworthy?
- What must be allowed to differ for the result to be useful to this person?
- Which input will cause a real decision in the process to change?
- How will we verify that the different result is better, not merely different?

You can try this today with one recurring task.

Suppose you use AI to plan your study sessions. Put the fixed parts in one column: the session ends with recall from memory, one applied exercise, and a short error note. Put the variable parts in another: the topic, current misunderstanding, available time, and the next smallest challenge. Then add one decision rule: if I can explain the idea but cannot apply it, the next session begins with a new case rather than another explanation.

That is already more personal than asking for a “personalized study plan.” It contains a variable that changes the next action, a stable loop, and a way to inspect the result.

The industrial age taught us how to make more by making things alike. The next step is not to throw that discipline away. It is to aim it at the process instead of the person.

Standardize how we listen, translate, produce, check, and learn. Then let the outcome carry the difference that actually matters.
