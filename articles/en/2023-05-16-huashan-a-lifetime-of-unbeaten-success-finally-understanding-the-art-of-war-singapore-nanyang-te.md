On January 31, 2023, Huashan gave a lecture titled “A Lifetime of Unbeaten Success, Finally Understanding *The Art of War*” at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore.

▲ Huashan lecturing on *The Art of War* at Nanyang Technological University

This article is based on the lecture, and by studying it, you can finally understand *The Art of War*!

First, let's look at the logical structure of the 13 chapters of *The Art of War*:

**1）Counting Chapter:** If you can't win, don't fight.

**2）Operations Chapter:** If you can win, but can't afford to fight, don't fight.

**3）Strategic Attack Chapter:** If you can win and can afford to fight, think again—can you solve the problem without fighting?

**4）Military Form Chapter:** If you can't avoid fighting, then wait—fight only after you're sure to win.

**5）Military Momentum Chapter:** Tactical overview, divide the forces, attack with the main force, win with the unexpected.

**6）Real and Virtual Chapter:** Always maintain the initiative in battle, fight on your terms, not theirs—time and place are under your control.

**7）Competition for Position Chapter:** The key to military success lies in controlling morale; if morale is strong, so is the victory.

**8）Nine Changes Chapter:** Tactical principles for different terrains. A general must have no weaknesses; when a person is desperate, they have no enemies.

**9）Marching Chapter:** Notes on military movement, reconnaissance manual: 32 methods for observing enemy conditions.

**10）Terrain Chapter:** Six basic types of terrain and their tactical principles. The professional ethics of a general.

**11）Nine Regions Chapter:** Geopolitical psychology.

**12）Fire Attack Chapter:** Ancient "nuclear warfare."

**13）Using Spies Chapter:** Who is most likely to become a spy. You must be willing to spend money and take risks on espionage, because the cost of war is too high—ending the war as soon as possible is worth every penny!

**Huashan:**

Dear leaders, teachers, and students, good day!

Today, I will be sharing my thoughts on *The Art of War*. I have summarized *The Art of War* in four words: **"A lifetime of unbeaten success."** Today, I will mainly walk through the basic logic of the 13 chapters of *The Art of War*.

Recently, there was a very popular TV series in China called *Kangbiao*, where the black boss solves problems by reading *The Art of War*. That's just "Bullshit!" This is the biggest misunderstanding of *The Art of War*—there are no strange tricks or clever strategies in it. If there were, they would have been learned long ago, and you wouldn't be learning it now.

So, before studying *The Art of War*, you need to have a **"Consumer Warning":** Don't learn *The Art of War* randomly, or you might end up as someone who is smart but overreaches, calculating too much and ultimately ruining your own life.

The central idea of *The Art of War* should be summarized in three sentences:

**1）*The Art of War* is not a method of warfare, it is a method of avoiding war.**

**2）It is not a method of winning, it is a method of avoiding defeat.**

**3）It is not a method of winning through war, it is a method of winning before engaging in war.**

*The Art of War* first teaches you not to fight, because once you start fighting, you lose control of the situation. For example, the war in Europe—had they not started fighting, there would have been no war. But once they started, it was impossible to stop. So *The Art of War* is first about avoiding war, and avoiding war means avoiding defeat.

Second, don't always think about winning. In business, we often think about defeating our competitors, but in reality, no one can be defeated by you. No one is actually defeated by you; everyone defeats themselves. If you want to compete with someone, you can only defeat them when they make a mistake.

Third, *The Art of War* is not a method of winning through war, but a method of winning before engaging in war. It doesn't tell you to go to war and then figure out how to win; it tells you to figure out when you've already won and then decide to fight.

These three points are the core ideas of *The Art of War*. Now, I will go through the content of the 13 chapters in detail.

**1、Counting Chapter**

The first chapter of *The Art of War* is called "Counting Chapter." Many people misunderstand *The Art of War* from the start. When they hear "Counting Chapter," they think it's about strategy, about clever tricks and conspiracies.

But actually, **the "count" in *The Art of War* is not about strategy, but about calculation.** Many people often combine *The Art of War* with *The Thirty-Six Stratagems* in one book, which is completely wrong. I imagine Sun Tzu would be so angry if he saw this!

Telling people about strategies is useless. In *Kangbiao*, it's said that Gao Qiang learned *The Art of War* and used strategies to solve all his problems. I want to say, **don't think that *The Art of War* has any strategies that can help you solve problems.** For example, "diverting the enemy's attention," which is often used in football penalty kicks, but can you always score? No, the key is still your technique and basic skills.

The "count" in *The Art of War* is not about using strategies or clever tricks, but about calculation, about comparing the strength of both sides. How do you calculate? It's **"Five Matters and Seven Counts"**:

**Original Text:** Therefore, the five matters should be considered, and the seven counts should be compared to understand the situation: one is the way, two is heaven, three is earth, four is the general, five is the method. The way is to make the people agree with the ruler, so they can follow him to death and to life, without fear. Heaven is the yin and yang, the cold and heat, the seasons. Earth is the near and far, the dangerous and safe, the wide and narrow, the life and death. The general is wisdom, trust, kindness, courage, and strictness. The method is the curved formation, the official system, and the use of supplies. All these five matters, the general must be aware of them. Knowing them leads to victory; not knowing them leads to defeat. Therefore, by comparing the counts, we can understand the situation: who has the way? Who has the capable general? Who has the favorable heaven and earth? Which laws are enforced? Which army is stronger? Which soldiers are more trained? Which rewards and punishments are clearer? With this, I can know the outcome of the battle.

This means we should compare the strength of both sides from "five matters and seven counts."

Through these comparisons, we can "know victory," meaning we can know whether we have a chance to win.

First is "the way." The way is not about the justice of war, because justice is something that can't be clearly defined. You have your justice, I have mine. In the "Counting Chapter," the way refers to "the way is to make the people agree with the ruler." That means **if the entire nation supports this war, you have the way.**

**"Heaven" and "earth" are easy to understand—they refer to favorable weather and terrain.** "The general" refers to the strength of both sides' commanders. "The method" refers to whether your military law and discipline are effective. For example, "Revolutionary soldiers must always remember the three main rules and eight points of attention," which is the "method" of the People's Liberation Army.

After comparing these five aspects, you can "know victory." **"Victory is knowable but not achievable."** You can calculate whether you have a chance to win, but if the calculation shows no chance, you can't force it.

In our company, there's an "Operations Hall," a meeting room where everyone can sit. It's called the "Know Victory Hall." All strategies and ideas proposed by the company must be tested here. Before you make a move, you need to know whether it will work—before you make a move, you need to know victory. Only if you know victory can you make a move; if you don't know whether it will work, you're fighting to win, which goes against the principles of *The Art of War*.

Our company's method of "knowing victory" is to hold a full company project "Know Victory Meeting" every Monday, with no excused absences. All proposals to be presented to clients must first go through my review.

This is like me being a chess master, with 60 games going on at the same time. Every Monday, I move one step forward, though not every game needs a move every week—depending on the project's progress, sometimes it's three or five games, sometimes seven or eight. This is our "Know Victory Meeting."

On March 3rd every year, we also have an annual "Know Victory Day," which we call "March 3rd, Know Victory for the Whole Year." Now we have over 60 clients, and on March 3rd and 4th each year, we hold two days of meetings, with each project only allowed three minutes to explain what the strategic focus is for this year—what one thing you will do this year to help the client win.

If I say, "I know victory," then the proposal is okay. If I say, "I know I won't win," then the proposal needs to be redone. Through this "Know Victory Day," I can have a clear idea of the battles I need to fight this year.

"Those who calculate victory before battle are in a favorable position; those who don't are in a disadvantageous one. More calculations lead to victory, fewer lead to defeat, and how much more so if there are no calculations at all? With this, I can see the outcome of the battle."

This is about the issue of victory. *The Art of War* talks about "calculation in the temple." Often people say "calculation in the temple leads to victory," meaning you need to calculate a lot to win. But actually, the original meaning of *The Art of War* is not that. "More calculation" means more than 60 calculations, which is more than 60 points, giving you a 60% chance of success. "More calculation" and "less calculation" refer to the probability of your victory.

Now I'd like to ask you a question: who do you think is better at using strategies, Zhuge Liang or Sima Yi? Many people think it's Zhuge Liang, because you still think of "strategy" as a trick. In our cultural stories, Zhuge Liang is the master of clever strategies.

But in *The Art of War*, "strategy" is about calculation, about the comparison of the "five matters and seven counts." So in reality, Zhuge Liang is actually not qualified. Let's do a comparison analysis between the states of Shu, Wei, and Wu:

Sima Yi's state of Wei has over 5 million people and 600,000 troops; Wu has over 2 million people and 200,000 troops; Zhuge Liang's state of Shu has less than 1 million people and less than 100,000 troops.

You're fighting with a 1:6 ratio, using 100,000 troops to attack a large state of 600,000, and you have to cross the Qinling Mountains and the Sichuan Gorge, which are mountainous and difficult to pass. How can you possibly win? But every time it's Zhuge Liang who starts the war, trying to win in a war with no chance of success. Moreover, he has to prepare two to three years' worth of food just to go out, and if he doesn't win within two months, he has to retreat. Why? Because if he doesn't retreat, there will be no food left to return, and the entire army will starve to death there.

Sima Yi accurately calculates how long the food will last and never fights him, just hangs up a sign saying "no fighting." Zhuge Liang even sends women's clothes to him, trying to provoke him with a challenge, but Sima Yi still doesn't fight. Finally, at Wuzhangyuan, Sima Yi still hangs up the "no fighting" sign, and Zhuge Liang dies of depression, dying at Wuzhangyuan.

So **the "strategy" in *The Art of War* is not Zhuge Li, the clever tricks of Zhuge Liang, but calculation, which can be said to be the modern SWOT analysis.**

To summarize the central idea of the first chapter, "Counting Chapter," it is: "Before fighting, you must first do a SWOT analysis of both sides. If you can't win, don't fight." Therefore, *The Art of War* is first and foremost a method of avoiding war.

You might think that with so many soldiers on the front lines during war, they must eat a lot. In reality, the amount of grain consumed by soldiers on the battlefield is only a small fraction of the total grain consumed in war. Sun Tzu wrote that it was one part out of twenty, 20:1, which refers to the cost of transporting grain to the front lines, specifically in the plains over a thousand miles. The cost of transporting one cart of grain was twenty carts.

When Emperor Wu of Han waged war against the Southwest Yi people, which is today's Hunan, the cost of transporting grain to the front reached 120:1. Transporting one cart of grain to the front required the cost of 120 carts.

Emperor Wu was enthusiastic about expanding the borders, willing to engage in aggressive warfare. When he took power, China had 50 million people, but by the time he died, the population had dropped to 25 million. According to *Zizhi Tongjian*, it recorded that "half the people died unnaturally," meaning that half the population died in non-natural causes, and the entire country, from the imperial court to the people, went bankrupt.

Why did half the people die unnaturally? It was because Emperor Wu wanted to expand the borders and kept waging wars. In ancient times, human life was considered very cheap, and as Hugo said, "the people who are used as cannon fodder always show reverence to the cannon firers." So the people still revered Emperor Wu as the Great Emperor Wu, and considered Emperor Wen and Emperor Jing less impressive.

When Emperor Wu took power, he inherited the prosperity of the Wen and Jing era, with a well-filled treasury. But after he engaged in aggressive expansion, he used up all the wealth saved by the previous generations. When the treasury was empty, he called on the wealthy to donate and imposed property taxes.

For example, if you had one hundred million in assets, you had to pay 6% in property tax. People didn't want to pay, so he introduced the "reporting of concealed wealth" decree. If you claimed to have only twenty million, and your neighbor reported you, you would have to pay fifty million, and the accuser would get half of it. The U.S. still has this measure today, where anyone who reports economic crimes can get 30% of thecase-related amount as a reward.

Even with this, the money was still insufficient for war. What could be done? He then introduced head taxes. Emperor Wu lowered the age of head tax from 16 to 3. In such a situation, the country went bankrupt, and people lived in despair. Parents would kill their sons before they turned three to avoid paying taxes.

In his later years, Emperor Wu issued the *Luntai Zui Ji Zhao* (the *Luntai Self-Blame Edict*). This edict read: "Since I took the throne, my actions have been reckless and chaotic, causing the whole country to suffer. I will now abolish all policies that have harmed the people." He, who was so proud, finally used such harsh language to criticize himself, showing that he had faced a huge political crisis in his later years. In reality, the country was nearly ruined. **War is a drain on the whole country.**

Drucker said that all disruptive innovations reduce costs by more than 30%. If I can significantly reduce costs, I can win the war, right? Is there a disruptive innovation in war costs? Yes, there are two: Genghis Khan and Napoleon.

Why could Genghis Khan advance all the way to Europe? If Emperor Wu had tried to go to Europe, the grain transportation cost would have been 1200:1. How did Genghis Khan manage to do it? Because he greatly reduced the cost of military rations.

The Han Chinese had to carry grain when they fought, but the Mongols, as a nomadic people, didn't eat grain—they ate beef and mutton. They could drive cattle and sheep along with them, and the cattle and sheep could reproduce continuously. They were moving along the grasslands of Eurasia to the west, so their horses and cattle had no trouble with feed.

Moreover, the nomadic people had the technology to make dried beef, which is like modern compressed biscuits. One person could carry enough dried meat for two weeks, greatly reducing the cost of military rations.

Napoleon was even more impressive. His secret was not only reducing the cost of military rations but also reducing the cost of human lives. Napoleon's significance in military history was that he initiated the era of people's war.

Before Napoleon, European wars were nobleman wars, with no connection to the common people. The armies were composed of nobles and mercenaries, and the cost of war was very high. If 3,000 people died in a single war, it might take three years for the country to recover.

However, on August 23, 1793, Napoleon started a new era of European warfare called people's war. China had always practiced people's war, but Europe had not before. People's war meant conscription, allowing everyone to participate in war.

At that time, Napoleon was very proud and said, "Now in Europe, human life is as cheap as dust." He turned the entire population into war volunteers. The kings had mercenaries and noble armies, while he had the whole nation as volunteers. This created a crushing cost advantage over the anti-French coalition. He had no cost.

When the last Russian Tsar, Alexander, organized the anti-French coalition, the normal war would have required winter encampments and resumed fighting in the spring. But he wanted to continue fighting in the winter because he couldn't afford the time to raise more troops. If winter passed, he could bring in another 300,000 soldiers.

The central idea of *The Art of War* is: **"You can win, but you can't afford it—don't fight."**

**3. *Attack by Stratagem* (Planning to Win Without Fighting)**

*The Art of War* first says to wait and calculate whether you can win. If you can't win, don't fight. The second chapter says you can win, but you should still think about whether you can afford it. **The third chapter says you can win and can afford it, but you should still wait and think whether you can solve the problem without fighting.**

So Sun Tzu was truly remarkable. Most generals are eager to fight every day because they gain money and power.

*The Art of War* is universally recognized as the first military book. British military strategist Li Dehata said: "The first military book for all humanity is *The Art of War*; Clausewitz's *On War* can only be second. Moreover, he said that *On War* is outdated compared to *The Art of War*. *On War* appeared more than two thousand years after *The Art of War*, but Li Dehata said it was outdated, meaning that *The Art of
of War* had led Western military thought ahead by more than two thousand years.

Let's first look at this sentence from *Attack by Stratagem*: "A hundred victories in a hundred battles is not the highest form of excellence; to subdue the enemy without fighting is the highest form of excellence." Here it says **a hundred victories are not good, and in fact, repeated victories will lead to destruction.**

King Wen of Wei asked Li Ke: "What caused the fall of Wu?" Li Ke replied: "Because they fought too often and won too often." King Wen said: "Fighting often and winning often is a blessing for the country, so why did the country fall because of war?" Li Ke said: "Frequent fighting exhausts the people; frequent victories make the ruler arrogant. To govern with arrogance over exhausted people is why the country fell. Therefore, those who love war and exhaust their armies will never survive."

On the other hand, how could you reach the state of a hundred victories? A hundred victories mean you only see the hundred victories, but you never think about why you had to fight a hundred battles. You should solve the problem in one battle. **A hundred victories show that the quality of your victories is low; you win every time, but you never fully resolve the issue.**

**War is not for victory, but to solve a problem.** What we want is not a hundred victories, but to subdue the enemy without fighting. If we have to fight, we want to resolve it in one battle.

So how can we subdue the enemy without fighting? Sun Tzu explained four points: **"The highest form of attack is to attack the enemy's plans; the next is to attack their alliances; the next is to attack their armies; and the lowest is to attack their cities."**

The meaning of "上兵伐谋" is not that **you should use strategy in war**, but that **you should eliminate the enemy's plans.** "伐" is the verb, and "谋" is the object. To eliminate the enemy's plans means to neutralize their strategies.

How do you eliminate their plans? One way is to eliminate the person who is the main planner, removing their central figure, so they can't fight anymore, and no one has ideas. The second is to eliminate their desire to fight, making them not want to fight anymore.

There is a story called "晏婴：折冲樽俎" (An Yin: Turning the Battle at the Banquet). In the Spring and Autumn period, the powerful state of Jin planned to attack Qi. To understand the situation in Qi, Duke Ping of Jin sent Minister Fan Zhao to visit Qi. King Jing of Qi hosted a grand banquet for Fan Zhao. During the banquet, as the wine flowed and the mood was warm, Fan Zhao, drunk on wine, asked King Jing, "Your Majesty, please give me a cup of wine." King Jing told his attendants, "Pour the wine into my cup and bring it to me." Fan Zhao drank it all at once.

An Yin saw this and angrily ordered his attendants, "Quickly throw away this wine cup and bring another one." According to the etiquette of the time, the king and ministers each used their own cups. Fan Zhao intentionally violated the etiquette to test the reaction of King Jing and his ministers, but An Yin saw through it.

After returning to Jin, Fan Zhao reported to Duke Ping of Jin: "Now is not the best time to attack Qi. I tested the reaction of King Jing and his ministers, and An Yin saw through it." Fan Zhao believed that Qi had such a wise minister, and attacking would have no guarantee of victory, so he abandoned the plan.

**The second way to subdue the enemy without fighting is not to only reward "焦烂之功."**

"焦烂之功" refers to a situation where someone is building a house and the chimney is straight up, with a pile of firewood below. Someone advises him to bend the chimney and move the firewood away to prevent a fire caused by sparks from the chimney. But the person ignores the advice, and a fire breaks out. The villagers come to help put out the fire. After the fire is out, he thanks everyone and hosts a banquet, especially inviting those who helped put out the fire to the main table, but he doesn't invite the person who gave the advice.

Someone reminds him, "You invited all the people who helped put out the fire, but why didn't you invite the person who advised you to change the chimney and move the firewood? If you had listened to him, the fire would never have happened. Now, when you're rewarding people for their help, why didn't you invite the person who gave the advice?" The person finally realizes this and invites the person who gave the advice.

Now, are some countries or companies not doing this? They often reward "焦烂之功." Often, people don't want to help because if the problem hasn't reached a critical point yet, you help, and people won't remember your contribution. Only when the situation is about to collapse do you step in, and then they remember. But this is not subduing the enemy without fighting.

In the Three Kingdoms period, Zhang Lu in the northwest wanted to declare himself king. His advisor Yan Pu advised him not to declare himself king, saying that once he did, the imperial court would attack them, and they wouldn't be able to fight back, and they wouldn't even be able to become a warlord. Moreover, he was already a king in fact, so they should just wait and protect their territory. Let the others fight; if they can't win, they shouldn't declare themselves king but should declare themselves emperor and take over the world. If they can win, they should surrender, and they could still be granted a title of marquis.

This is actually the strategy of not fighting. Later, Zhang Lu indeed surrendered, and Cao Cao, upon hearing this, first granted Zhang Lu a marquis title and then also granted Yan Pu a marquis title. Yan Pu was just a minister of Zhang Lu, so why did Cao Cao grant him a marquis title? Because he wanted to show that if you could stop a warlord from declaring himself king, you could also be granted a marquis title by surrendering. This is how you subdue the enemy without fighting.

Therefore, *The Art of War* is not just a military book; it was also written for kings to read.

After World War I, Keynes wrote a book called *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, which you can also read. After the war, Britain and France were determined to take revenge and demanded that Germany pay huge war reparations.

Keynes suggested, "We should not only not demand reparations from Germany, but we should also lend them money to help them recover their economy, otherwise a second world war will break out." At that time, Keynes was just a relatively low-level official in the British Treasury, and he participated in the Paris Peace Conference, proposing this idea, but no one listened. Then he wrote the book *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*.

When World War II ended, all the victorious countries did not demand war reparations from the defeated countries, and the "Marshall Plan" was implemented, which followed Keynes's logic. Because of the lessons from World War I, everyone knew that only this way could they subdue the enemy without fighting, otherwise, the crazy people might do anything, and they might come back in a few decades.

## 4、Tactical Dispositions

Tactical Dispositions是讲“知胜”最重要的一篇。

孙子曰：“昔之善战者，先为不可胜，以待敌之可胜。不可胜在己，可胜在敌。故善战者，能为不可胜，不能使敌之可胜。故曰：胜可知，而不可为。”

善于作战的人，先让自己成为不可战胜的，然后等待敌人可以被战胜的时候再去战，不可战胜完全在于自己的修为，可胜的完全在于敌人。

所以**最厉害的人，能让自己不被战胜，但即使是最厉害的人，也做不到一定能战胜某某。** 这就是“可胜在己，不可胜在敌”。所以“胜可知，而不可为”。做之前可以知道我能不能胜，但如果你想强行去夺取胜利，那是不可能的。这就是兵法的意思。所以你说《狂飙》里高启强学了兵法就能解决所有问题，这可能吗？这是不可能的。

“善战者无智名，无勇功。”因为“所谓善战者，胜于易胜者也。”那些所谓的善于打仗的人，都是十个打一个，打的都是比他弱小的人，所以他们没有什么智名和勇功。

“胜兵先胜而后求战，败兵先战而后求胜。”就是说我们要赢了再打，这就是战中求胜和胜中求战的区别。“善用兵者，修道而保法，故能为胜败之政。”**我觉得兵法里最值钱的就是这四个字，叫“修道保法”。**

计篇里我们讲要计算“天、地、道、将、法”，这里又讲“修道保法”，就是让你加强自身“天、地、道、将、法”的修养，别想着去打去赢。这样一看好像什么兵法也没学到，其实兵法就是什么都没有，**在什么都没有的情况下你要想先胜后战，靠什么？靠等待。**

我创业21年了，我在创业之初就为**华与华的客户战略制定了一个伟大的原则，就一个字——等，我们绝不出去找客户。** 可能你会觉得不出去找客户，那不得饿死吗？饿不饿死其实是看你有多少人，

如果只有一个人，那就是一个人吃饱全家不饿，这就只要有一个客户就够了。所以当有客户跟我们耍赖，不按时付款的时候，我就要求立即熔断。

以前有客户拖款，我们的人还使劲给他干活，希望稳住他继续付钱合作，我就告诉他们，宁愿这个客户不要了，也不许继续给他干活，如果你再继续给他干活，我就连你都不要了。这个观念我扭转了好多年，现在他们都服了，没有人会再继续给不按时付钱的客户干活了。

**5. The Chapter on Military Momentum**

The most important thing in the *Military Momentum* chapter is six characters: "With the main force, meet the enemy; with the reserve, achieve victory." Note that this character is pronounced "jī," not "qí." If you pronounce it "qí," it's completely wrong. Many people want to win with a surprise attack, want to use a clever move to win, and want to achieve a lot with little effort. But physics tells us that you can't use four ounces to move a thousand catties; you can only use a thousand catties to move four ounces. That's what's called **decisive overwhelming force.**

In "With the main force, meet the enemy; with the reserve (jī), achieve victory," the "reserve (jī)" refers to the reserve force. In fact, the importance of this sentence in *The Art of War* is not great, it's quite ordinary. Because everyone misunderstood it, it became the most famous sentence in *The Art of War*. **In essence, "With the main force, meet the enemy; with the reserve (jī), achieve victory" is just a standard tactical principle.**

In ancient battles, when two armies met on the battlefield, what was behind the general? That was the reserve force. Cao Cao explained it simply: "Those who go out first are the main force; those who go out later are the reserve force." The ones who went out first to fight were the main force, and those held back were the reserve force. The reserve force could strike unexpectedly, disrupting the enemy's formation during the battle, making it the key to victory.

Going back 4,000 years, the Yellow Emperor had a minister named Feng Hou, who wrote a military book called *The Book of the Odd*, which also talks about the concept of the reserve force.

**Dividing the main and reserve forces is a basic tactical principle, called the "divided battle method," and battles must be fought with this method.** No matter how many troops you have, you must divide them. If you have a hundred thousand troops, you must divide them; even if you have a squad attacking a hill, you must divide them. Using the divided battle method, we can understand many battles. Let's use Han Xin's "Backwater Battle" to explain the divided battle method.

Han Xin led three thousand troops to attack a Hanzhong army of twenty thousand. *The Art of War* says that the many can defeat the few, but why did twenty thousand men get defeated by three thousand? The reason is the "divided battle method" of "With the main force, meet the enemy; with the reserve (jī), achieve victory."

**First Stage: Dividing the main and reserve forces.** The Hanzhong commander Chen Yu did not divide his forces, keeping all twenty thousand in the camp. Han Xin used the divided battle method, sending two thousand reserve troops out at night, thirty miles before the Jinxing Pass. The task of these two thousand troops will be explained later. In the morning, he sent one thousand troops to form a battle line along the river.

The Hanzhong army laughed, saying they had never seen such an arrangement. With one thousand troops on the riverbank, how could they possibly withstand twenty thousand? Of course, the Han army also realized this. With the river behind them, there was no way to retreat, so they had to fight to the death. This is the origin of the idiom "Backwater Battle."

But don't misunderstand. If the "Backwater Battle" only had the one thousand troops on the riverbank, no matter how fiercely they fought, they couldn't defeat twenty thousand. The key was the other part of the story. In the first stage, the two thousand reserve troops had already been sent out at night, and we didn't know where they were. In the morning, the one thousand troops formed a battle line along the river, and this was also a reserve force. Han Xin had another move: he led another force, estimated to be one thousand troops, as the main force to engage the Hanzhong army.

How did Han Xin divide the main and reserve forces? One main force and two reserve forces. One force engaged the enemy, while the other two reserve forces were stationed in different places. One was on the riverbank, visible to the Hanzhong army, and the other was hidden, unknown to anyone, the two thousand troops who had been sent out at night.

Did the Hanzhong army divide their main and reserve forces? You could say they did. Before the battle, they did not divide their forces to block the Jinxing Pass, but all stayed in the camp. Now, one army was out to fight, which was the main force. The rest in the camp were the reserve force. So Han Xin's next move was to call the reserve force in the camp to come out and attack, making the camp empty.

**Second Stage: Switching Main and Reserve Forces.** In the second stage of the battle, the main forces of both armies engaged. According to historical records, the battle lasted for a long time, but the Hanzhong army was still larger in numbers, and it seemed that Han Xin was losing, retreating in a disorganized manner. His banners, drums, and ceremonial equipment were scattered all over the ground.

Why did he throw these things on the ground? He was using them as bait to lure the Hanzhong soldiers to come and fight for them, especially those in the camp who were guarding it. If they came out to fight, they would be the ones to be caught.

Where did Han Xin retreat to? He retreated to the troops on the riverbank, joined them, and then charged back to fight. At this point, Han Xin's forces were no longer one main force and two reserve forces, but one main force and one reserve force. He combined his own troops with the reserve force on the riverbank, and then charged back to fight, becoming the main force. The two thousand troops sent out at night were still the reserve force, waiting for the right moment to strike.

How did Han Xin create the opportunity for victory? He used the bait he had thrown on the ground—his banners, drums, and ceremonial equipment. The Hanzhong soldiers saw these and wanted to claim the glory, and those who could capture Han Xin's banners would receive great rewards. The Hanzhong reserve force in the camp rushed out to fight, and at this point, the Hanzhong army had already used all its cards.

Han Xin was waiting for this moment, when the Hanzhong army had used all its cards, and Han Xin still had one card left—the two thousand reserve troops sent out at night. The moment had come, the time to strike unexpectedly had arrived. The two thousand reserve troops, who had been sent out at night, were each given a Han banner. They then rushed into the Hanzhong camp, doing one thing: they pulled down the Hanzhong banners and replaced them with Han banners.

**Final Stage: Achieving the Unexpected Victory.** How did Han Xin switch the main and reserve forces again? His two thousand reserve troops had now become the main force, taking over the enemy's camp. When the Hanzhong soldiers looked back, they saw that all the banners in their camp had been replaced with Han banners. They realized their camp had been taken. They didn't know how many people were inside or what had happened, and their morale collapsed instantly.

Han Xin's battle resulted in the defeat of the twenty thousand Hanzhong army, the killing of the Hanzhong commander Chen Yu, and the capture of the Hanzhong king Zhao Xie. In this battle, we saw the use of main and reserve forces. Han Xin always had both, while the Hanzhong army had only the main force. If the Hanzhong army had a reserve force in their camp, the two thousand Han troops would not have been able to enter. This is the meaning of "With the main force, meet the enemy; with the reserve (jī), achieve victory."

**6. The Chapter on Reality and Illusion**

That means if I arrive at the battlefield first and prepare my attacks, I can wait for the enemy in ease. You run, you're exhausted, and I can take advantage of your weakness and defeat you.

**"Attacking the vulnerable is about always being the one to take the initiative and leave the enemy in a passive position."** This should be easy to understand. There are two other points that might be less clear. One is that "virtual" and "real" don't mean the enemy is virtual and I am real. Instead, both the enemy and I have real and virtual aspects.

We often hear the phrase, "If others have nothing, I have it; if others have it, I have something better; if others have something better, I change; if others change, I move faster." That is, if you defend the front, the rear will be exposed; if you defend the rear, the front will be exposed; if you defend the left, the right will be exposed; if you defend the right, the left will be exposed. **8. The Nine Changes**

Starting from the chapter on the Nine Changes, I'll explain it more simply. The *Art of War* focuses on the earlier chapters, and the later ones are tactical points. **The Nine Changes chapter is about various changes. Its main content can be divided into three levels:**

The first level is that Sun Bin first talks about how to respond to different terrains. Some roads should not be taken, some cities should not be attacked. Understanding this flexibility allows a general to use the terrain and control the army.

The second level is that the general must consider both the advantages and disadvantages when making decisions.

The third level is that Sun Bin warns generals of five personality flaws that lead to danger.

The first two points can be found in the book or the course on *The Art of War* from Dedao. Here, I mainly discuss the third point, the five personality flaws of a general. The original text says, "A general has five dangers: if he is certain to die, he can be killed; if he is certain to live, he can be captured; if he is quick to anger, he can be insulted; if he is clean, he can be disgraced; if he loves the people, he can be troubled."

**"If he is certain to die, he can be killed"** means if you are too fearless of death, your risk tolerance is too low, and you will be reckless, eventually dying for it.

**"If he is certain to live, he can be captured"** means if you are too afraid of death, you lack courage and fighting spirit, and you will be captured.

**"If he is quick to anger, he can be insulted"** means being quick to anger and easily provoked, like a firecracker, makes you susceptible to the enemy's provocations.

**"If he is clean, he can be disgraced"** means this "cleanliness" is not about being corrupt, but about valuing one's reputation so much that you can't tolerate any blemish. If someone disgraces your reputation, you will be willing to fight to the death to prove your integrity, and you will fall into the trap willingly, even if you know it's a trap.

**"If he loves the people, he can be troubled"** means that loving the people will make you very troubled. For example, Liu Bang, when captured by Xiang Yu, had his father and wife, Lady Lu, captured. His father was stripped and tied to a chopping block, with a large pot of boiling water next to him, forcing Liu Bang to come out and fight. Xiang Yu said, "If you don't come out, I'll cook your father and drink the soup, and then kill your wife."

## 9、Tactical Dispositions

**The Army on the March讲的是行军的战术原则**，比如说在不同的地形，你应该怎么安排行军宿营。然后讲侦察兵的32条行动法则。

比如你看到敌军走路都拄着拐杖，那说明他们没有粮食了；出来打水的士兵看到水源就迫不及待地大口喝水，那就说明他们没水了。这具体的32条法则，大家也可以在我那本书里或者Dedao课程看到，我就不一一举例了。

所以，The Army on the March中心思想就是**“行军注意事项，侦察兵手册：32种敌情观察法。”**

**

## 10、Terrain

Terrain主要就讲了六种地形的战术要点，我就挑一个“隘行”来讲，隘行就是两边高山中间有山谷的地形。

“隘形者，我先居之，必盈之以待敌。”如果是我军先遇到这种地形，“必盈之以待敌”，“盈”就是满，就像把水装满一样，你的兵力部署一定要把山谷的两头填满。隘形一前一后两个口，在这两个口你的兵力都要和隘口平齐布阵，这样敌人才进不来。

世界军事史上最著名的隘口之战就是斯巴达三百勇士的温泉关之战。三百个斯巴达勇士盈满了隘口并与隘口平齐，斯巴达人通过这种作战阻挡了波斯十几万大军三天，杀得波斯人血流成河。

那么波斯人后来又是怎么攻下温泉关的呢？他们通过当地的向导找到一条小路，绕到了关口的后面。这就证明斯巴达人没有做到将兵力盈满隘口的两头，他只填了一头，所以被波斯人从后面攻进去了，因为斯巴达人没有读过《The Art of War》啊！

在Terrain里还有句话很重要，就是**“进不求名，退不避罪，唯人是保，而利合于主，国之宝也。”** 这是讲将领的职业操守和自我修养，就是不会因为怕人说我是想逞英雄就不进，也不会怕人骂我贪生怕死就不退，只要我能保全我的队伍，有利于我的国君，保护民众，我认为该进就进，该退就退。

那在公司里呢，如果说上级对你有不合理的要求和不合理的目标，那你要怎么做？我送大家一句话，叫做**“努力工作，等待被开除。”** 当你做到这点的时候，你就是进不求名，退不避罪，唯人是保，利合于主，你就是公司的保障。

所以Terrain的中心思想，就是**“六种基本地形及其战术原则，以及将领的职业道德。”**

**

## 11、The Nine Situations

Terrain是讲地形，地形是地理问题，The Nine Situations就是讲地势，地势其实是形势问题，一共九种，我把它称为叫地理心理学，为什么这么说，我举个例子，比如散地。

“诸侯自战其地者，为散地”。在自己本国的境内作战就是散地。什么东西容易散呢？军心容易散，因为离家太近了。“进无必死之心，退有投归之处”。就说士兵很容易就跑回家了，所以大家的战斗意志不强。

讲完散地的定义，你就理解地形和地势的区别了吧。地形就是研究地貌是山地还是平原，而地势则涉及到军队的心理问题以及这个地方的战场形势，所以我说它的中心思想，**是“地理心理学。”**

**

## 12、Attack by Fire

Attack by Fire，火攻是古代极具杀伤性的进攻方式，相当于古代的“核战争”。这一篇也分为了四个层次：

**第一层，是火攻的物资对象；**

**第二层，是讲了火攻的八种方法，可以应用的八种工具；**

**第三层，强调了火攻比水攻更有效，但是危害也大；**

**第四层，孙子又强调了慎战的思想。**

具体的话还是可以去看《华杉讲透<The Art of War>》这本书。

**

## 13、The Use of Spies

The Use of Spies，是《The Art of War》的最后一篇。主要讲了三点：

**第一点，指出了用间谍有多么重要，用得好就能避免战争；**

**第二点，讲了五种间谍类型；**

**第三点，讲了使用间谍上的花钱之道。**

在《The Use of Spies》，孙子重复了《Attack by Stratagem》的内容，他说：“凡兴师十万，出征千里，百姓之费，公家之奉，日费千金”。这里还是在说战争太花钱了，战争要花那么多钱，所以在间谍上，更应该可以往死里花钱，因为在间谍上不管花多少钱都是最便宜的，他就一个人嘛，你就需要买通一个人而已。

**

**用间谍的核心理念就是要舍得花钱，花钱越多，成本就越低，投入越大，风险就越小。** 刘邦是靠买买买打赢项羽的，楚汉相争，依我看刘邦的天下很大程度上就是他花钱买下来的。

刘邦很大方，给了陈平4万斤黄金，让他去买通项羽的将领，而且刘邦还不要账本，他还知道陈平是个喜欢贪污的人，只要能买通不管陈平花了多少钱，反正你搞定就行。最后陈平也成功地买通了很多人，让项羽都不知道身边那些事好人哪些是间谍了，结果就疏远了钟离眛，撵走了范增。项羽的叔父项伯，就是刘邦在项羽身边最大的间谍。

但是项羽呢，不舍得给人钱，韩信说他说得很形象，他说项羽平时非常平易近人，谁病了就赶紧去看望，而且还陪着哭，把自己的吃喝都分给他。但等到属下将领要论功行赏的时候，官印都刻好了，项羽就是舍不得给出去，把印拿在手里磨啊磨，印角都磨圆了就是舍不得给出去。

这一篇，孙子也讲了哪些人最容易发展成间谍，比如说你要在某个董事会里发展间谍，董事长和总经理肯定买不通，但那个负责记录的秘书可能比较容易，然后负责打扫董事长办公室的阿姨也可以。

民国的时候，戴笠要除掉投靠了日本人的伪上海市市长傅筱庵，通过谁呢？就通过他最亲信的跟了他家两代人的“两代义仆”——厨师朱升源。朱升源虽然是两代义仆，但他现在有更大的民族大义，因为傅筱庵毕竟是投降日本做了汉奸。

结果军统就做通了朱升源的工作，结果朱升源趁傅筱庵在他的虹口官邸熟睡的时候，用菜刀把他的脑袋给砍下来了。这个故事告诉我们要重视大人物身边地位低的人，这些人最能帮你办大事。

以上就是《The Art of War》13篇内容的解读，时间有限我并没有全部展开讲，还是推荐大家去读我的《华杉讲透<The Art of War>》。
