# The Odds Depend on What You Do

> 发布日期:2026-11-07 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-07-胜算里要算上自己的动作.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-07-odds-depend-on-actions.md)

---

GreenPal's founding team spent nearly a year building an app, but users did not automatically arrive. Co-founder Bryan Clayton described that gap in a public account of the company's growth. To me, it points to something we leave out when judging whether an undertaking can succeed: what we intend to do next. Change the approach, and we may be facing a different problem.

[Clayton's account, published in 2023](https://buffer.com/resources/bootstrapping-growth/), describes the growth of a marketplace for lawn care services. It is a successful founder's own account; I have not independently audited the experiences or business figures. It can help us examine actions, but it cannot tell us the probability that a startup will succeed.

An AI-generated summary of a startup interview raises a similar question. It discusses how small teams can avoid abandoning an attempt at the analysis stage when larger companies have greater resources. I want to take that question further. When we say the odds are poor, are we judging external conditions, or are we also passing judgment on an approach we have never seriously designed?

## Misconception One: An Assessment of the Environment Settles What to Do

A competitor may have more money and more talent. Those can be facts. But a step is missing between those facts and the conclusion that an undertaking is not worth pursuing: whom do you intend to serve, what will you solve, and how will you give that person a reason to try? Different answers can lead to different decisions under the same external conditions.

Separate the questions first. Entering a competitive market and building another version of a large company's product do not involve identical difficulties. When assessing the former, we should not quietly assume the latter and present the resulting conclusion as the fate of every possible approach.

In [his paper](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/readings/ellsberg.pdf), Daniel Ellsberg used thought experiments about drawing balls from urns to examine choices made when probability information is unclear. One urn contains a hundred red and black balls in an unknown ratio; another contains fifty of each. These examples challenge a theory of decision-making. They are not a representative survey establishing how many people fear the unknown.

The distinction is useful. Knowing that half the balls are red and not knowing the ratio both leave you unsure what the next draw will produce. But the missing knowledge differs. In the first case, you lack the outcome. In the second, you also lack a reliable basis for estimating it. Expressing both situations as precise percentages may give you precision of wording rather than knowledge.

An undertaking adds another layer: you are usually doing more than drawing a ball. After building the app, GreenPal's team could still change how it reached users, the scope of its service, and the delivery process. Those actions could affect what happened next. Treating the situation as a sealed urn and discussing only how many red balls it contains leaves out the work itself.

We should also distinguish two kinds of action. Asking customers how they find a service first changes what the team knows. Managing whether providers actually arrive changes delivery conditions more directly. New information does not guarantee an improvement. It has to inform the next approach before it can affect results. Confusing those stages makes thorough research look like accomplished improvement.

Of course, an urn does not change because you stare at it, and a market does not cooperate because you care. The analogy helps us distinguish conditions that are given from conditions our actions might influence. It does not grant us the power to change outcomes at will.

So I would make my questions to AI more specific: under the current approach, which conditions are most likely to cause failure? Is there another approach that could change one of those conditions? The first question helps inspect a plan. The second helps find one. Neither substitutes for the other.

## Misconception Two: More Plans Mean More Choices

Asking AI for many directions at once can give you a sense of possibility. But if ten plans assume the same users, the same way of reaching them, and the same reason to pay, perhaps only the product names have changed. A longer list does not necessarily expand your choices.

Look at what a plan changes. In GreenPal's account, the team asked early customers how they normally found lawn care. A recurring answer was that they asked friends and family first, then searched online if that failed. This gave the team a specific direction for finding customers. It later shifted from competitive big-city search terms toward terms associated with surrounding towns.

The change was small, but it mattered. The team had not acquired a complete theory of all customers. It had identified more precisely where to reach them. For a small team, that may change where the next investment should go.

AI can help list questions, organize answers, and compare routes. But I still need to know whether a suggestion rests on something customers actually said or on a reason the model supplied on their behalf. Both can appear in a fluent report. They deserve different weight in a decision.

[A writing-experiment preprint released in October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30945v1) offers a related clue. A preprint is a research draft shared publicly; sharing it does not establish that other researchers in the field have reviewed it. This article relies only on the supplied v1 text and has not checked later versions.

Its final analysis included 297 US participants whose first language was English, each completing two AI-assisted brief-writing tasks. The group initially given prompts to clarify goals and break down tasks received higher overall writing-quality scores. An advantage remained in the immediately subsequent task after the prompts were removed. The main scores came from AI, with human ratings for a subset; the two rating methods did not fully agree. The study did not measure long-term business outcomes.

What interests me is the action the interface put back into the process: describe the intended result before deciding how to produce it. At least in this writing task, that set of prompts helped. We cannot use it to calculate a company's chances of survival. We can use it to reconsider whether all the preparation before a request really deserves to disappear.

Returning to a problem like GreenPal's, asking AI to recommend a promotional method differs from asking it to compare how different customers find a service. The latter also requires source material. Where material is missing, a useful response should include questions that remain to be investigated.

Here is a standard I am willing to use: after removing the product name, can the plan still specify whose action it intends to change, and which action? If it cannot, narrow the plan. A difference that helps me decide what to do next is more useful than a string of plausible directions.

Different approaches also need to be compared against the same objective. Narrowing an intended service to a more specific group can be sensible, but we should say that the objective has changed. We cannot keep changing what success means while claiming the odds of success have improved. Objectives can change; the record should remain. Otherwise, every departure can be repackaged as having been right.

## Misconception Three: Hands-On Help Is an Inefficiency to Eliminate

A process that runs automatically can easily look like progress. Yet the process being automated may still be poorly understood. Removing operations does not automatically supply the missing understanding.

GreenPal's account describes concrete problems: providers failed to arrive on time, mowed only the front yard, or left a gate open. A booking could be complete inside the app while the service the customer wanted remained incomplete. The team still had to work out how to help providers do a good job and hold them accountable when they did not.

At that point, a better-looking page was insufficient. The customer was buying a service performed as agreed, and the app handled only part of that promise. Without understanding delivery, adding features faster might never touch the part that affects a customer's decision.

In [his 2013 essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham argued that founders should initially recruit and help users themselves, and sometimes perform manually the work they intend to automate later. This is practitioner advice built from cases, not a measured probability of success for those who follow it. It explains the information that direct involvement may provide.

It resembles fitting a garment. If raising an arm makes the fabric pull tight, the fitting reveals something about how the garment and body work together. Sewing the same garment faster will not automatically fix it. A service is more complicated than clothing, and people can change their needs, so direct help does not mean taking measurements once and knowing the answer forever.

That does not make doing everything personally a good idea. Clear, repetitive, verifiable steps are reasonable candidates for tools. The contact worth preserving is contact that exposes something unknown: where a customer hesitates, where delivery breaks down, or which promise requires someone else's extra effort to keep.

Too much help can also conceal a problem. A customer may complete a task smoothly because you supplied an explanation, changed the material, or performed an operation for them. Unless that help is recorded, whether the customer can succeed without you remains unanswered.

When taking part directly, I would therefore keep a record of what extra help was needed and how the same rescue might be reduced next time. Experience that can gradually become clear instructions or a reliable process gives a system a chance to improve. Perpetual improvisation can simply create more work.

Hands-on involvement also has a cost. Help that satisfies a customer may consume more time than the team can sustain. When recording a rescue, include that cost: can instructions resolve it, can a tool take it over, or must an experienced person continue doing it? The last option can be viable, but pricing and promises have to support it. Recognizing demand completes only part of the work.

## Misconception Four: Influencing Conditions Means Effort Guarantees Success

Someone might object that successful founders can always explain why their past actions made sense, while unsuccessful founders may have worked just as hard. That deserves a serious answer. GreenPal's account gives us a process to examine, but no group of otherwise comparable companies taking different actions.

Even when business later improves, customer interviews, search traffic, and service improvements may all contribute. I cannot separate their contributions from one retrospective account, much less turn one success into a promise for everyone. What we can learn is how the team turned a broad question into specific work.

Some constraints are also hard. If you cannot fulfill a promise, cannot bear the consequences of an error, or must commit resources needed to maintain your life, your room to act shrinks considerably. Choosing not to proceed can be clear-headed. Recognizing that we can influence some conditions should coexist with recognizing that we cannot control them all.

Conversely, prediction becomes more useful when a mature process has accumulated sufficient records and its approach is relatively stable. Estimating demand and cost carefully may then be more valuable than continually inventing new actions. What I want to correct is a judgment that leaves its assumed approach unstated; prediction itself remains necessary. In particular, when a decision imposes costs on others, my willingness to try does not entitle me to count them among the willing participants.

In [his 2024 memo](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/the-folly-of-certainty), Howard Marks emphasized that people can hold clear views while recognizing that their judgment may be wrong. This is an investor's experiential discussion of uncertainty. I agree with one practical implication: recognizing the limits of your evidence can lead you to check more carefully without making your position vague.

For me, there is another form of reassurance to guard against: interpreting a lack of progress as insufficient effort and continually increasing the commitment. An action has informational value when it actually returns something that can revise our judgment. Being busier, more practiced, or better at explaining ourselves does not necessarily meet that condition.

Likewise, failing to find evidence can mean different things. The way we approached people may not have elicited their actual experience, or the desired demand may genuinely be absent. A single cool response may not distinguish them. We need to examine how large a question that particular action can answer. We should neither inflate a local signal into a verdict on the whole market nor endlessly dismiss the evidence to justify carrying on.

Here is a small check I propose. This four-step method has not been shown here to improve the odds of success. Choose an undertaking you already consider difficult but have not formally abandoned. There is no need to start a new project. Write down the following in order:

1. Specify the approach you are currently judging: whom it serves, what result it offers, and how you plan to reach those people. A broad direction is insufficient.
2. Identify one condition you can influence but lack evidence about. Check existing records first. If a gap remains, arrange a specific conversation and ask how the person actually accomplishes the task.
3. Before that conversation, write down answers that would change your judgment: which would support continuing, which would call for an adjustment, and which would indicate that proceeding is unsuitable for now. Also bound what you will commit to this attempt.
4. Afterward, compare the result with your original record. Separate what you have newly learned from what you still do not know. Only the former supports an update; keep the latter unresolved.

The check may conclude that there is no suitable action available. Good. At least you will know whether the obstacle is access to people, capability, resources, or the objective itself, rather than letting “too much competition” cover every problem.

Here is a specific question: when you decide an undertaking is not worth pursuing, have you seriously compared different approaches, or only made a thorough list of external difficulties? I need to see the difficulties clearly. I need to see my own possible actions clearly too.

GreenPal spent nearly a year building its app, and the work still was not over. That gap guaranteed no happy outcome. It did leave something open to change: what the team would do next.
