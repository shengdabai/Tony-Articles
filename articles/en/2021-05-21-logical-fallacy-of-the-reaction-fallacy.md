I've gained a more comprehensive understanding of the reaction fallacy. There's a question in the book asking what causes our reaction fallacy? Roland Jepson wrote in his book that once we accept a certain viewpoint, our self-esteem makes us unwilling to admit we're wrong. When our viewpoint faces opposing opinions, we're more concerned with finding ways to counter them than with considering how much truth or reasonable insight they might contain. We'd rather strain to find new support for our own views than face any new facts that clearly contradict our position. Think about how terrible this is. Our first reaction to a problem is to avoid it, to refuse to accept new perspectives, and to try our best to prove the other person wrong. Not wanting to admit our mistakes seems to be about face, about maintaining our image, but digging deeper, it's likely a trend of inertia. Change is so difficult, so it's fine just like that—I'm definitely fine, the problem isn't me, I'm great... Such thoughts are all signs of the reaction fallacy, and these errors happen unconsciously.

Thinking about myself, shifting the burden of proof and attacking critics are mistakes I often make. Sometimes my family calls me "stubborn," and I used to think it was a personality issue, or maybe staying true to my own thoughts was sticking to my original intention. Is that really the case? Today's lesson made me reflect further: why do I stick to my own opinions and not listen to others? Digging deeper, I'm trying to make the other person prove my viewpoint wrong, or that their viewpoint can't be proven correct. But how does that relate to my viewpoint? Just because the other person is wrong, does that make me right? Shifting the burden of proof and refusing to take responsibility for it are my logical thinking flaws, which to a large extent make me unwilling to listen to others. There's also attacking critics, like a hedgehog. When there's a conflict of views, like when I argue with my family about whether traditional Chinese medicine is scientific, I think it's not scientific, many treatments lack evidence, and I believe the toxicity of traditional Chinese medicine is definitely less than that of Western medicine. My family has many traditional views on this, and I can't stand it, so I criticize their viewpoint, even go as far as to slander them. In the end, I say, "I won't talk to you anymore." In fact, their viewpoint is just a viewpoint, and it has nothing to do with them. I associate it with them, so I get very angry. Now I think it's a bit funny. Logic really matters.

The straw man fallacy is attacking a position that the opponent does not hold. Intentionally using the straw man fallacy is unethical because distorting the opponent's position is to further deceive. However, in most cases, the straw man fallacy is not intentional. People may simply not realize they've over-simplified a somewhat complex position, or they may have generalized and made a bold claim from an originally specific and cautious stance.

Over-simplification straw man fallacy:

Argument:

(General premise) Evolutionary theory says humans and apes are the same.

(Specific premise) Humans and apes are different because humans are obviously smarter.

(Conclusion) Evolutionary theory is wrong.

Error reason: Evolutionary theory does not say humans and apes are the same.

Concept substitution straw man fallacy (common in political rhetoric):

(Reporter) Question: Did you embezzle campaign funds?

(Politician) Answer: Great question! I've received a lot of generous campaign donations, and my favorite one was a handwritten card thanking me for everything I've done. I like this card because I value the contributions of the working class.

Error reason: The question was changed to something easier to answer.
