Pavlov's classic experiment on classical conditioning is well known. By reinforcing a dog's response to a bell, he eventually conditioned the dogs to salivate automatically at the sound of the bell.

Later, he used whistles, metronomes, and lights as stimuli, and the dogs' behaviors were shaped by environmental stimuli, which were transmitted to the nervous system and brain, triggering corresponding reactions.

But the experiment had a continuation that is even more concerning. It relates to a sudden flood.

In the 1920s, a major flood struck Leningrad, and Pavlov's laboratory was empty, but many dogs were locked in cages. These dogs nearly drowned, with only a small space between their noses and the top of the cages for breathing. This led to extreme stress for them.

After the flood receded, Pavlov immediately noticed that the dogs' behaviors had changed. One dog, which used to love its trainer, now showed indifference. What Pavlov did next was even more shocking.

Pavlov subjected many dogs to stress, causing them to break down mentally, and then attempted to restore them. He meticulously documented all of these experiments.

He discovered: (1) he could classify the dogs and predict how easily each one would break down; (2) the dogs that were least likely to break down were also the least likely to recover to their previous state; (3) all dogs could be broken down; (4) he could not restore a broken-down dog to its previous state unless he re-applied the stress.

This outcome echoes modern cases of people who have been severely impacted. People have varying levels of resilience to stress, and can pressure truly become motivation? But pressure can certainly lead to breakdown.

In fact, this is very close to today's term "PUA" or psychological manipulation. For example, typical pyramid schemes use PUA to exert pressure on members, and under such high pressure, most people's behaviors become involuntary, and they may not even know what they're doing, only continuing to recruit more people and earn more money.
