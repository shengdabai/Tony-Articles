# When Should AI Interrupt You?

> 发布日期:2026-09-30 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-30-什么时候该让%20AI%20打断你.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-30-when-should-ai-interrupt-you.md)

---

Imagine reading an article in English and pausing your cursor over an unfamiliar word. A definition appears instantly, and you keep reading with barely a break in your flow. The next day, the word turns up again. You still do not recognize it. The tool completed the lookup, but it did not help you acquire the ability to recognize the word next time. Would it have helped to ask you to guess before showing the definition? That depends on whether you were trying to finish the article or learn the word.

A link summary about personal AI agents described the ideal experience as work done quietly in the background, with an occasional report of the result. By agent, I mean a software assistant that can plan its own steps once given a goal and boundaries. Another, separate recording summary raised a related point: looking up a word and remembering it are different things. Both are secondhand summaries, not firsthand product tests, and neither establishes that a particular interaction works. Together they raise a question: as AI gets better at helping without being asked, which interruptions are needless, and which preserve a chance to learn?

Here is my view. A personal agent should be judged by more than the steps it removes. It should also recognize judgments worth handing back to the person. Routine, low-risk actions can happen quietly. When someone is learning a judgment they will need again, a well-timed pause may be more useful than seamless completion. The aim is not to make the machine talk more. It is to make an interruption earn its place as a real attempt at practice.

## What disappears when help becomes invisible?

Suppose an agent handles the material you read every day. It filters documents, groups them, extracts conclusions, and hands you a clean summary. You save time. You may also lose contact with the contradictions, missing evidence, and exceptions in the originals. If all you need to know is whether something needs a reply today, that is a welcome trade. If you want to learn to judge whether a document is reliable, those omitted parts may be exactly where the practice happens.

I am not arguing that effort automatically teaches us anything. Copying messy pages into a spreadsheet by hand is unlikely to make someone a better analyst. The effort worth preserving can change a future choice: predict a conclusion before checking the evidence; identify a possible error before asking the machine to explain it. Often, the useful piece to hand back is the moment before the problem has been tidied up.

In a [2026 randomized study by Shen and Tamkin](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills), 52 developers who used Python but were new to a particular library learned to work with it. The group using AI scored an average of 50% on a quiz immediately after the task, compared with 67% for the group coding by hand. The AI group finished a little faster, but the study could not rule out chance variation as an explanation for that small speed difference. The largest quiz gap concerned debugging: a person can obtain working code without being equally able to explain why it fails elsewhere.

This was a small study of one library and immediate understanding. It cannot tell us that long-term skills inevitably decline, much less that every personal agent damages learning. The researchers also observed that people who asked conceptual follow-up questions and checked their understanding tended to do better than people who mainly let AI generate code, and than people who mainly relied on AI to debug it. Those were separate patterns, not one group that delegated both tasks. The observation is an association, not proof that asking such questions caused the higher scores. What I take from the study is narrower and more useful: two people can both be “using AI” while doing very different amounts of thinking.

The [2025 survey by Lee and colleagues](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) offers another angle. Its 319 respondents provided 936 examples of using generative AI at work. Greater confidence in AI for a given task was associated with being less likely to report having used critical thinking on that task, such as checking evidence and whether a conclusion holds up. That question asked whether they had done so, not how much thinking they had done. Respondents also described a shift in where they thought: less effort on making the initial material, more on checking information, integrating responses, and keeping the task on course. These are associations in self-reports, not evidence that AI has reduced anyone's underlying capacity to think. They do suggest that when execution becomes easier, the ability to review it may need more practice.

I think of this as a cost that is easy to leave off the ledger. If I count only the pages an agent read, the words it wrote, and the buttons it clicked, I may overestimate my progress. When the next document has a different structure, or a conclusion contradicts its source, will I notice? If not, yesterday's smooth process has yet to become my judgment. I do not need to redo every task from scratch. I do need to notice when removing a step also removes the only chance I had to practice it.

## When is an interruption worth its cost?

Interruptions are costly. A person pulled away from writing may need time to recover the thread; someone under a deadline may have no appetite for an impromptu lesson. “It helps people learn” cannot be an unlimited license for an agent to speak up. I would first ask what an interruption is meant to buy: preventing a mistake now, developing a judgment that will help next time, or merely making the interface look active. The third purpose does not justify the disruption.

Return to the unfamiliar word. On its first appearance, showing a definition immediately may be the best choice. If the reader has chosen a “help me remember” mode, the tool could wait for a natural pause, hide the answer briefly, and invite a guess before explaining it. That should not become an obligation attached to every lookup. Reading something you urgently need to finish and deliberately building vocabulary are different jobs. The person's goal should determine the timing.

I would use three questions before inviting an agent to interrupt. Will this judgment come up again? Does the person have enough clues to try, or do they first need basic information? Can their attempt receive prompt, clear feedback? If all three answers are yes, a short pause might be worth it. If there is no reasonable basis for a guess, insisting on one simply creates frustration. If a one-off task has an easily checked result and no learning goal, quiet execution makes more sense.

A [2015 study by Bosse and colleagues](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-015-0286-5) offers a limited comparison of feedback frequency. Forty-seven medical students repeatedly practised placing a nasogastric tube, which runs through the nose into the stomach, in a mannequin. Expert feedback meant comments on what worked and what needed changing in the attempt just made. Both groups received it after attempts, but at different frequencies. Both groups improved. The group receiving more frequent feedback scored better on a final rating of overall smoothness, while the difference on a checklist of individual steps was less clear. This study measured early performance in a simulated medical procedure and did not compare feedback before an attempt with feedback after one. It prompts me to ask which kind of performance feedback improves, but cannot tell us when an AI agent should interrupt.

This leads me to an untested design hypothesis. If an agent reveals the answer before I have made a judgment, it has substituted for me. If it waits for a testable guess and then points out a specific mismatch, the prompt may serve as feedback. On screen, these experiences could differ by a single click; as opportunities to learn, they differ a great deal. Think of cooking: an automatic stove can hold the heat steady and get dinner served faster. If you want to learn to judge the heat yourself, making the occasional adjustment and checking what happens is closer to practice. Cooking offers smells, touch, and immediate consequences that reading research or working with AI often lacks. The analogy explains the proposed order of attempt and feedback; it is not evidence that a particular interface will work.

## Make a prompt into practice, not another notification

We have been trained by too many notifications to dismiss them on sight. If an agent celebrates every completed step and calls us over for every minor issue, it will eventually be treated like a pop-up ad. The interruption I want is brief and close to the work: show one disagreement, ask me to choose between two defensible interpretations, then explain the evidence. “I found a problem” is not enough. It should help me see which clue I had used to make my choice.

Imagine using AI to summarize a public study. The summary reads well, but the agent notices that the paper measured only short-term performance while your sentence claims a lasting effect. It could silently rewrite the sentence. Or it could flag the mismatch, ask how far the conclusion may safely go, and then show the relevant passage. Under a deadline, with another person already assigned to check the document, flagging the issue for review may be the better choice. If you are practising how to read studies, trying a revision yourself before seeing the evidence may teach you that a sample and a time window cannot be stretched by intuition. The warning looks similar in both cases; its purpose differs.

Practice also needs a different example. In [2021 experiments by Schorn and Knowlton](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-021-01168-z), participants practised several keypress sequences: blocked practice repeated one before switching to another, while interleaved practice mixed them. The next day, they were tested on either the old sequences or similar new ones. Blocked practice produced faster improvement during training. For old sequences, people trained with interleaving retained performance under either test order, while those trained in blocks did worse when the test mixed sequences. For new sequences, the interleaved group improved under both test orders; the blocked group improved only when the new sequences were also presented in blocks. The overall difference between training methods for new sequences, however, was inconclusive. These were laboratory keypress tasks; the paper does not show that reading studies, writing, or using an agent will respond in the same way. I use it to propose a design hypothesis to test: try the underlying judgment in a new-looking task, rather than asking someone to acknowledge the same alert again and again.

Today, for example, a prompt may point out that a study tested only immediate performance. Tomorrow, a different topic could present the same kind of time limit without a red warning, and ask you to find where the conclusion goes too far. If every example carries the same marker in the same place, you may learn to spot the marker instead of reading the evidence. By “transfer,” I simply mean being able to use a judgment when the material changes. A keypress experiment has not verified that this writing exercise will work. A new piece of material must test it.

The agent need not show every intermediate step, either. It could keep a short record of what I chose, the reason I gave, and the later correction. When a similar task returns, it can point to the difference. Such a record is useful because it lets a person recover the path of their own judgment, not because a product gets to boast that it remembers them. It should retain only what the task calls for, with the user deciding whether to keep it.

## The strongest objection: I use a tool to make life easier

This objection deserves a full answer. One person must finish a document before the end of the workday. Another needs a tool to take over many actions because of a disability or the strain of sustained attention. Turning every automated step into a quiz would damage both efficiency and accessibility. Adults also have every right to decide that today they want the result. Forcing “growth” into each interaction is another way of making a choice on their behalf.

That is why I would make learning prompts a choice within the task, rather than a moral stance taken by the tool. A user can say, “Just finish this,” or “Help me learn to tell these cases apart.” The same person may need different responses when rushing today and practising tomorrow. An action with serious, hard-to-reverse consequences requires prior confirmation for reasons of authorization and risk. A pause designed to develop a skill requires a genuine wish to learn. Those are different reasons to interrupt, and mixing them makes every prompt look like a lesson.

There is a harder question, too: who decides what is worth learning? An agent can detect words you repeatedly look up, conclusions you often revise, or a kind of error that keeps returning. It cannot decide for me that the underlying skill matters in my life. It may propose, “You keep changing conclusions here; would you like to practise this once?” The choice is still mine. A machine can be good at detecting patterns; my goals decide which patterns matter. That is why even a proactive personal agent should not quietly take over the direction of my learning.

The prompt must also change as the person changes. Requiring someone who already knows a task to explain its elementary steps again and again is low-level interference. Asking a beginner to make a judgment without the underlying concepts is unfair. A sensible interruption first provides examples and necessary information, then leaves room for an attempt, and becomes less frequent as the skill grows. Later it can return when a real error or a novel condition appears. It should not be a classroom that never closes.

## Try setting your own threshold for a week

You need not wait for a perfect personal agent to test this idea. Pick one recurring task you give to AI and would gradually like to do better yourself. Reading a public source and deciding whether its conclusion overreaches is one example. Write down the judgment you want to learn: “I can identify which people, time period, and task a result actually covers.” If you cannot name a skill you want, let the tool finish the job. There is no virtue in a quiz for its own sake.

Then give the tool a narrow rule. It should organize material as usual. At the judgment point you named, it should hold back the final answer, show the necessary source passage and two plausible interpretations, and let you choose and explain why. Only then should it reveal the source location and a counterexample. Interrupt one judgment at a time; do not turn an entire document into an exam. This is a small experiment I am proposing, not a general recipe established by research.

Finally, do not judge the week only by how smooth it felt. A day later, find an unmarked source, make the same kind of judgment yourself, and check it against the original. If you have not improved, ask whether the prompt came too early, the question was too hard, the feedback was unclear, or the skill was not worth the time. [Steve Blank's 2015 discussion of the minimum viable product](https://steveblank.com/2015/05/06/build-measure-learn-throw-things-against-the-wall-and-see-if-they-work/) makes a related methodological point: state the hypothesis before designing the experiment that will teach you something. Here a “minimum viable product” means the simplest thing you can show potential customers to test a specific guess; it need not be finished software. This is advice about testing business assumptions, not experimental evidence about AI and learning. I borrow the discipline of naming what I mean to test. A prompt appearing on screen is not proof that a person learned.

Now return to that unfamiliar word. On a day when you need to finish the article, a definition that flashes up and gets out of the way is excellent help. On a day when you want to build vocabulary, a pause, a guess, and a later attempt to recognize the word in another sentence may be better help. A good personal agent should tell these wishes apart and let you change your mind. I want it to remove pointless busywork, while leaving just enough pause where I have chosen to grow stronger.
