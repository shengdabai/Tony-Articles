# You Become What You Can Measure

> 发布日期:2026-08-06 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-06-你会变成自己会测量的东西.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-06-you-become-what-you-can-measure.md)

---

The more AI content I see, the more I feel that the real problem is not that machines have no taste.

That sentence is too easy.

It lets humans feel superior for five seconds, then returns us to the same shallow workflow: ask for output, dislike the output, blame the model, ask again with stronger adjectives. "Make it more premium." "Make it more human." "Make it less AI." The machine tries to obey, but the result often moves from one average shape to another average shape.

The deeper problem is colder:

A system improves where the feedback is clear.

Code improved quickly because code has many external judges. It can compile or fail. Tests can pass or fail. A program can run faster or slower. A bug can be reproduced. Not every software problem is easy, of course, but the field has a thick layer of measurable reality. The model can try, receive correction, try again, and gradually learn where its output touches the world.

Writing, design, teaching, strategy, taste, and personal judgment are different.

They are not impossible for AI. They are not magic. But the feedback is messier. A line can be clear but lifeless. A design can follow every rule and still feel wrong. A lesson can deliver the content and fail to change the learner. A strategy can look clever in a deck and collapse on first contact with a customer.

The dangerous part is that these failures often still look acceptable.

Average survives very well in subjective domains. Sometimes it even wins the first round, because average is familiar, smooth, and hard to object to. But a thousand smooth pages do not become a mind. A thousand "good enough" designs do not become taste. A thousand summaries do not become learning.

This is why "AI slop" is not only an aesthetic complaint. It is a measurement problem.

## The Machine Learns the Ruler

Modern AI did not become powerful because someone whispered intelligence into it. It became powerful because we found ways to turn more of the world into learnable signal.

The 2017 Transformer paper mattered because it gave language models an architecture that could scale with data and compute.[^1] Later instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback showed another important move: human preference could be turned into training signal, making models more useful and aligned with what people actually wanted.[^2]

That second move matters here.

The model did not simply "understand helpfulness" in some pure philosophical sense. It was trained against examples, comparisons, rankings, and feedback. Humans made judgments. Those judgments became data. The data shaped behavior.

This is also why the quality of judgment matters so much.

If the ruler is shallow, the system becomes shallow at scale.

If the feedback rewards confidence, the model learns confidence. If the feedback rewards politeness without truth, the model learns smooth evasion. If the benchmark rewards short answers, the model learns to compress. If the platform rewards outrage, creators learn outrage. If a person measures only the number of tasks finished, that person slowly becomes a task-finishing machine, even if the tasks are not worth doing.

This is not only an AI problem. It is a human problem made visible by AI.

We become what our feedback loops ask us to become.

## Subjective Does Not Mean Untouchable

The mistake is to place everything subjective inside a black box and call it "human."

That is comforting, but lazy.

Good writing is subjective, yes, but not entirely. We can ask whether the argument has tension. We can ask whether the opening creates a real question. We can ask whether each paragraph changes the reader's position. We can ask whether the ending returns to the beginning with more pressure than it had at the start.

Good design is subjective, yes, but not entirely. We can ask whether hierarchy is clear, whether spacing is consistent, whether color supports attention, whether the interface tells the user what can be done next, whether the page breaks on mobile, whether the choice of visual weight matches the importance of the action.

Good learning is subjective, yes, but not entirely. We can ask whether the learner can retrieve the idea later, apply it in a new situation, notice when it does not apply, and change behavior when the change is inconvenient.

The word "subjective" should not be a permission slip for vagueness.

It should be an invitation to decompose.

Some parts of a subjective field are close to objective. They can be checked, scored, tested, or compared. Other parts require human judgment, but even there we can improve the quality of the judgment by asking better questions, keeping examples, making tradeoffs explicit, and reviewing decisions after time has passed.

This is the work most people skip.

They want taste as identity, not taste as practice.

They want to say "I have good taste" without building the uncomfortable machinery that would expose whether their taste survives contact with reality.

## The Average Is a Very Strong Enemy

AI exposes a strange fact about creativity:

The average is not weak.

The average is strong because it is statistically well represented. It has many examples. It offends few people. It sounds like the thing we have seen before, which makes it feel safer than it is.

For objective questions, the most common answer can be the right answer. If the question is arithmetic, consensus is useful. If many working programs solve the same task in similar ways, that similarity may contain real knowledge.

But in creative work, the most common answer is often merely the most common answer.

It is not the sharpest answer. Not the most alive answer. Not the answer that could only have come from a particular person, a particular experience, a particular risk, a particular refusal.

This is where many people misunderstand AI assistance.

They think the choice is between "AI does it" and "human does it." The more useful distinction is between average-seeking loops and edge-seeking loops.

An average-seeking loop asks AI for a finished thing, then accepts the first thing that does not embarrass anyone.

An edge-seeking loop asks AI for many possibilities, then uses a stronger human measurement system to find what is actually worth keeping.

The second loop is harder. It requires the human to have standards before the output arrives. It requires writing down what "good" means for this case, not in abstract virtue words, but in observable signs.

For an essay, maybe good means: the reader sees an old problem with a new handle; the argument contains at least one uncomfortable implication; every example serves the central pressure; the ending changes the opening sentence.

For a learning system, maybe good means: the learner can use the idea next week without rereading the note.

For an AI workflow, maybe good means: the human finishes with sharper judgment, not merely with a completed artifact.

Without that kind of ruler, AI will usually drift toward the smooth middle.

Not because it is evil.

Because the middle is well-lit.

## Personal Growth Is Also an Evaluation Problem

This is where the topic stops being about models and becomes about us.

Most people do not lack information. They lack a feedback system that tells the truth about whether information changed them.

They read. They save. They highlight. They ask AI to summarize. They collect methods. Their notes become heavier, but their behavior does not move very much.

Why?

Because they measured contact, not transformation.

Contact is easy to measure. How many books? How many notes? How many hours? How many prompts? How many articles? How many saved ideas?

Transformation is harder. Did I make a better decision? Did I notice my old pattern earlier? Did I stop doing one thing that was wasting my life? Did I build a small system that makes the better behavior easier tomorrow? Did I become more capable, or merely more informed?

AI can make this worse. It can turn learning into the rapid production of beautiful study materials. It can generate the flashcards, the summaries, the mind maps, the quizzes, and the weekly plan. The person can sit inside a palace of preparation and still avoid practice.

But AI can also make this better.

It can help us build private evaluation loops.

Before reading, ask: what do I expect to be true?

After reading, ask: what changed?

Before using AI, ask: what standard will I use to judge the answer?

After using AI, ask: did this make my judgment stronger or weaker?

Before starting a project, ask: what would count as evidence from the world?

After shipping, ask: which belief died?

These are small questions, but they change the direction of measurement. They stop rewarding the appearance of learning and start rewarding the ability to update.

That is the line between using AI to become lazier and using AI to become stronger.

## Build the Ruler Before You Build the Machine

There is an old warning often summarized as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it can stop being a good measure.[^3]

That warning is real. Measurement can corrupt. Metrics can be gamed. Scores can replace reality.

But the answer is not to abandon measurement.

The answer is to build better, richer, more honest measurement.

Especially for the parts of life we like to hide behind fog: taste, judgment, learning, creativity, wisdom.

The practical move is simple:

Before you ask AI to produce, define what will count as better.

Not perfectly. Not forever. Just enough to create pressure.

If you are writing, define the reader change you want.

If you are designing, define the behavior the interface must make easier.

If you are learning, define the future action that will prove the idea survived.

If you are building a system, define the feedback that will let it improve without flattering you.

Then use AI inside that ruler.

Let it generate options, expose weak points, challenge examples, simulate readers, compare drafts, find missing cases, and return you to your own standard when you begin to drift.

The human does not become valuable by refusing the machine.

The human becomes valuable by bringing a better measuring system to the machine.

This is also a kinder way to think about taste. Taste is not a mysterious badge that some people own and others lack. It is a trained relationship with feedback. It is the accumulated ability to notice the difference between smooth and alive, correct and useful, impressive and true, finished and worth finishing.

AI will not remove the need for that ability.

It will punish the people who never trained it.

And it will amplify the people who did.

So the question is not only: what can AI do for me?

The better question is:

What kind of person is my AI workflow training me to become?

Because every tool has a hidden curriculum.

Every prompt rewards a posture.

Every dashboard teaches a desire.

Every feedback loop shapes a self.

In the end, you do not rise to the level of your tools.

You rise, or fall, to the level of your measurements.
