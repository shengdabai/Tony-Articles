139th Original Article

What is beauty? Have you ever created something beautiful, and what did it look like? What was your state like at the time?

I recall my undergraduate graduation project. I want to re-examine my state at that time. I majored in civil engineering, but in the spring of my senior year, I chose to enter the education industry and become an English teacher. Here, I will analyze my state during the two months of completing the graduation project using the flow model.

1. Setting a Goal
To complete the graduation project, create a BIM information model for architecture, and submit it to a national design competition, thus giving a perfect ending to my civil engineering major.

2. Learning New Skills
During these two months of the graduation project, I was fully focused, working from 7 a.m. to 11 p.m., with only half an hour for lunch. I was immersed in the flow state, tackling one design problem after another, trying and making mistakes step by step. First, I learned the importance of communication. In my usual work, I tend to be dominant, taking charge of everything myself. However, in the design project, it was a team task, and among the five partners, communication was essential. Even though I had already completed the model, I had to make sure the others understood exactly what they needed to do. Sometimes, I also had to help my teammates solve problems.

Second, I learned to tolerate solitude and endure. When I was in the flow state, I paid little attention to the outside world. After completing the task, I felt a great sense of satisfaction. Sometimes, when I looked up, the room might be empty, with everyone leaving on time. Sometimes, someone might leave early due to an emergency. But for me, that was the only thing I was focused on. Other matters came second. I could handle them during my lunch break. Sometimes, I felt uneasy, wondering why only I was working so hard on the model. But gradually, I accepted it. It was my choice, my own standard, and had nothing to do with others. I was present in the moment, experiencing the beauty of the work, allowing the flow to flow through my space.

Third, setting clear goals. Often, I set many goals for myself because I was dissatisfied with the status quo. However, given the limited time and energy, setting detailed small goals is especially important. I also sometimes felt anxious and troubled, questioning why I wasn't more efficient. Gradually, I came to understand my own limits. A little bit of effort at a time, going with the flow, might be more helpful for me.

3. Establishing Internal and External Feedback

Internal Feedback: Often, I ignored internal feedback and focused more on external feedback. However, in this design project, I was constantly reflecting on myself and adjusting. For example, I could use my own professional knowledge to verify whether my design of a single floor was reasonable. I could check if the internal layout was optimal by comparing and validating it with my experience. I needed to keep adjusting. From an architect's perspective, I should pay attention to the exterior appearance, aesthetics, and spatial layout. From a civil engineer's perspective, I should consider structural design, earthquake resistance, wind resistance, and coordination with the building. I also needed to consider the perspective of electrical and water design. Was that enough? Of course not. I also needed to consider other perspectives, such as user experience, the needs of real estate developers, and regulations from government supervisors. I needed to analyze and think from more angles. Using internal feedback helped me focus better on the flow state.

External Feedback: Besides internal feedback, we also need to refer to external information. For example, the feedback from team members. Sometimes, I was limited by my own understanding. The help from my teammates gave me new perspectives, allowing me to see things from other angles. During the design process, based on my limited internal knowledge, the guidance from teachers, the search for online resources, and the advice from professional software engineers were all essential external feedback. Some problems that I thought about for a long time could be resolved with just one sentence from the outside. This was also a process of continuous trial and improvement. I shouldn't blindly trust authority, nor should I dismiss everything. Beyond design, I also need to consider human factors. For instance, the grief from a family member's death, the anxiety from entering a new field after graduation, and the pain of a breakup—all these external feedbacks had different impacts.

Keep going and cherish the moment. I am grateful for that period of focus and immersion. Flow is very precious to me. In work, I often feel lost and exhausted, and flow seems out of reach. This analysis has made me realize that I can do better. I can enter a flow state for each task, and by analyzing and thinking more, I can work better. Leaving the engineering industry, I am grateful for the engineering mindset and the valuable knowledge and experience. In the future, in the education field, I will combine what I have with the present and strive toward my goals. Let's go!
