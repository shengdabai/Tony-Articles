When it comes to "delayed gratification," most people should be familiar with the concept.

It's often used in various motivational articles, linked with self-discipline and excellence, as if it's a must-have trait for successful people.

It originates from a "cotton candy experiment," where children who could wait to eat two cotton candies in kindergarten ended up with better academic performance and higher income later in life. This ability to wait was called "delayed gratification" by the experiment's designer, Walter Mischel.

However, **a significant portion of the media's portrayal of delayed gratification is misleading**. Many parents haven't fully understood it and have rushed to test and train their children, potentially harming them for decades.

This article will discuss the essence of delayed gratification from a neuroscience perspective and tell you how to cultivate this ability in your child. The article is over 7,000 words long, which is quite lengthy, so I recommend you bookmark it first.

#

**01**

**Misunderstandings**

People have many misunderstandings about delayed gratification, with the biggest one being the belief that the causal relationship is simple.

Parents often guess: if a child waits to eat a cotton candy, they'll be more successful later.

Then, a sudden idea strikes: I want my child to be more successful later — so I should train them to wait.

And then, they start to assume they can "train" the child.

On a short video platform, there is a #DelayedGratificationChallenge topic with millions of participants.

One of the most popular videos is: a father places several cherries in front of a two-year-old child and tells the child, "Dad is going to the bathroom, you can eat them after I come back." Then he secretly records the child's restless behavior and posts it online, receiving over 100,000 likes.

Then, this father seems to have found the formula for traffic, replacing the cherries with cookies, candies, and jelly, and the comments from parents read: "Wow, this child is amazing, he will definitely become a big success."

**Honestly, this kind of test only satisfies the parents' desire for control. Even more amusing is that the results of these tests are completely opposite to the concept of delayed gratification.**

Walter Mischel's original words are as follows:

> To function effectively, individuals must **voluntarily** postpone immediate gratification and persist in **goal-directed** behavior for the **sake of later outcomes.**

**— To function more effectively, individuals must voluntarily delay immediate gratification and persist in goal-directed behavior for the sake of better outcomes.**

**1. Voluntary**

In Mischel's experiment, the child was told they could choose to eat the cotton candy anytime.

In the video, the father required the child to wait until he returned to eat.

**2. Better Outcomes**

In Mischel's experiment, the child knew that waiting for the adult to return would result in two cotton candies.

In the video, the child waited until the end and received the same reward.

**3. Goal-Directed**

In Mischel's experiment, clever children would talk to themselves, cover their eyes to distract themselves, or use abstract thinking to imagine the cotton candy as a cloud to reduce its temptation.

In the video, the child used no strategy and just stared at the cotton candy, muttering to themselves: "Dad said I can't eat it."

**This kind of intentional waiting "training" has nothing to do with delayed gratification and can have no positive effect.**

You might ask: If I get all the key points right, can I train delayed gratification?

Yes, but it works for mice, not for humans.

The following experiment comes from a 2021 paper published in *Science*.

Researchers designed a long tube. One end was called the "waiting room," and the other end was called the "reward room." The waiting room had a sensor that recorded how long the mice stayed. The longer the mice stayed in the waiting room, the more water they could drink in the reward room.

Scientists trained the mice for four weeks and successfully taught them to "delay gratification."

At first, the mice just wandered around, occasionally triggering the mechanism and running to the other end to find water. On the first day, their waiting time was about one second. Later, the mice realized that the longer they stayed, the more water they could drink. By the 15th day, the mice could wait for more than five seconds.

Scientists even used the **increase in dopamine levels** in the mice's brains to calculate that **the mice were using a continuous thinking decision-making model while waiting.**

The mice's inner monologue: "Wow, I can drink more water if I wait a bit, but I really want to go and drink it right now! I can't go yet... Hmm, I think I'm ready to go and drink now!"

This experiment met all the criteria for delayed gratification: voluntary decision-making, better outcomes, and goal-directed behavior. The results also confirmed that the mice were successfully trained.

However, the key point is that this was done under laboratory conditions with no interference. Not only is it difficult to create such a closed environment, but even if you did, it would take several days to train a child.

Even if you managed to create such a condition and successfully trained the child's waiting ability, it would not be applicable in real life.

According to educational theorist Bandura, in a social environment, **human behavior is influenced by the interaction of cognition, experience, and environmental factors.** If one factor changes, human behavior will also change accordingly.

Take the most intuitive example: someone is about to faint from hunger, and there's a loaf of bread in front of them. They can eat it immediately or wait two days to get two loaves. In that case, their waiting has no meaning, right?

In summary, delayed gratification is not simply about waiting or enduring temptation; it requires voluntary action, goal-directed behavior, and better outcomes. In a complex and changing social environment, delayed gratification is not a universal solution but needs to be applied flexibly. Therefore, we shouldn't blindly imitate or train delayed gratification but should understand it rationally.

**02**

**Delayed Gratification Is an Adaptive Decision-Making Process**

In a social environment, delayed gratification is an adaptive decision-making process, with the following three characteristics.

**1. Continuous Thinking and Judgment**

In the decision-making process of delayed gratification, our rational brain constantly fights against instinctual impulses. The rational brain is in a state of continuous activation. Once the rational brain area is underactivated, people will follow their instincts and tend to get immediate rewards.

**2. Influenced by Multiple Factors**

In real life, delayed gratification involves assessing the situation, making decisions, executing them, and bearing the consequences.

Is my waiting worth it? Should I wait? How long should I wait? When should I stop waiting? What if I miss out on more rewards and regret it later?

This actually involves the child's growth environment, trust in adults, resource availability, and the values passed down to them.

**3. Stable Preference in Decision-Making**

Most parents' parenting styles are stable and do not change significantly. During childhood, children with stronger delayed gratification abilities are influenced by their parents' parenting styles. As they grow, this ability remains relatively stable, forming a snowball effect over time.

You see, **this decision-making ability is complex and influenced by multiple factors. It's not something that can be "trained" through simple methods.** If you hear about some "delayed gratification training" or read an article that makes you excited and immediately want to apply this trait to yourself or your child, the result could be very bad.

**03**

**The Essence of Delayed Gratification: Decision-Making Ability — Prefrontal Cortex**

Since delayed gratification cannot be simply trained, do we still need to explore further?

Yes, **investigating the essence of things and finding their connections is the first step in correct thinking** and the first step in truly cultivating delayed gratification. Let's continue.

**From a neuroscience perspective, the activation level of the prefrontal cortex is the most significant factor affecting delayed gratification ability.**

Casey and others at the Sackler Institute of Developmental Psychobiology studied how self-control is formed in the brain by finding people who participated in the delayed gratification experiment 40 years ago in kindergarten.

Researchers designed an experiment to suppress impulsive urges and simultaneously tested the activity level of specific brain regions during the experiment.

The results showed that those who waited longer in childhood had stronger self-control as adults. When they needed to suppress their reactions to temptation, **the part of the brain responsible for thinking and control (the prefrontal cortex) became more active.**

In contrast, those who ate the cotton candy quickly as children had weaker self-control as adults. In the same situation, **their prefrontal cortex activity was inhibited, while the part of the brain responsible for feelings and motivation (the ventral striatum) became more active.**

How do we understand the prefrontal cortex and the ventral striatum?

You can imagine the brain as a car, with the prefrontal cortex as the driver and the ventral striatum as the accelerator.

The driver's job is to control the direction and speed of the car, while the accelerator's role is to make the car speed up.

When you face temptation, it's like there's an attractive target ahead of you, such as a delicious cake.

If you want to resist temptation, you need to make the driver **more focused and alert so that he can judge whether the target is worth pursuing**, whether it aligns with your long-term interests, and whether it affects your other plans. At the same time, you also need to make the accelerator less sensitive so that it doesn't react strongly to the target and make the car rush forward. If you can do this, it shows you have strong self-control.

However, some people, when facing temptation, are like a tired or drunk driver who can't think clearly or make judgments. They just follow their instincts, regardless of the consequences. Their accelerator is also easily triggered, and they get excited the moment they see the target, forgetting to brake or steer. If you are like this, it means you have weak self-control.

**From a neuroscience perspective, when deciding whether to delay gratification, people make rational decisions, and the prefrontal cortex plays a decisive role.**

**

The truth is now coming to light.

Why were children who waited longer for the cotton candy in childhood more successful later in life?

It wasn't because their waiting ability was stronger, but because their decision-making ability was stronger. In the experiment, decision-making ability was quantified through the length of the waiting time.

**This decision-making ability influences the child throughout their life, affecting their choices in every small detail, and ultimately reflecting in their academic performance and career achievements as adults.**

**How to Cultivate Delayed Gratification Ability?**

The following three methods may seem unrelated to delayed gratification at first glance, but if you understand what I said earlier, you'll know the reasoning behind them. Each subheading includes a real case from the community members. Note that we use "cultivate" here, which is a long-term process.

##

**1. Cultivate Children's Decision-Making Ability**

**
**

> **If a decision requires the child to bear the consequences, let the child make the decision.**
>
> — Li Xiaolai, *The Truth About Parenting*

  * In daily life: For example, choosing what to wear, what to eat for breakfast, or what toy to play with. These small decisions can help the child feel that their opinions and preferences are respected, and they can learn to weigh pros and cons and take responsibility for the consequences.

  * In learning: For example, choosing what topic to study, what method to use, and how much time to spend. These can help the child develop self-directed learning abilities and habits, as well as the ability to set goals, make plans, and adjust strategies and progress.

  * In developing interests and hobbies: For example, choosing which extracurricular activities to join, what skills to learn, and whom to befriend. These can help the child discover their potential and talents, as well as the ability to explore new things and broaden their horizons.

Of course, when parents teach children to make decisions, they should also pay attention to the following points:

  * Provide appropriate guidance and suggestions: Parents should not completely let go or completely control the child's decisions. Instead, they should provide appropriate guidance and suggestions based on the child's age and ability, helping the child analyze problems, assess risks, and find information.

  * Encourage trying and failing: Parents should not overly interfere or criticize the child's decisions. Instead, they should encourage the child to try different choices, even if they fail, and help the child learn from their experiences and lessons.

  * Cultivate emotional management ability: Parents should not ignore or suppress the child's emotions. Instead, they should teach the child how to recognize, express, and regulate their emotions, especially when facing difficulties or setbacks, helping the child maintain a positive and optimistic mindset.

Every child is an independent individual. **Within the range of consequences they can bear, parents can give them the right to make their own decisions.** Once the child gains a sense of autonomy in certain areas, it will greatly stimulate their enthusiasm for participation and willingness to cooperate. It will also unconsciously promote the development of the child's prefrontal cortex, enabling them to make more rational decisions when facing delayed gratification.

**2. Cultivate Children's Abstract Thinking Ability**

Abstract thinking ability refers to the ability to extract common characteristics from specific things. It is the process of forming concepts by extracting the essential features of things during analysis and using these concepts for reasoning and judgment.

**Abstract thinking ability involves multiple areas of the brain, with the prefrontal cortex and parietal lobe being the most important.** These areas achieve the process of abstract thinking through the connection and communication of neurons and neurotransmitters.

Note that abstract thinking ability strengthens as the child's brain develops, and it is a gradual process. When cultivating it, you should start from the child's interests and follow their cognitive level, without rushing.

Here are three suggestions for you:

  * Summarize and generalize: In daily life, inspire the child to abstract general rules or concepts from specific things.

For example, if the child sees a small cat and a small dog on the way, you can ask the child: What are the common features of these two?

The child might say: They both have four legs, two ears, a tail, can bark, can run, and can eat.

You can then ask: What category do they belong to?

The child might answer: They belong to animals.

In this way, the child abstracts the concept that they are both animals and can use this concept to classify other living beings.

  * Logical reasoning: Teach the child to infer possible outcomes based on known conditions.

For example, you tell the child a story.

The king and queen had a beautiful princess. One day, a dragon came to the king's castle and demanded to marry the princess. The king was very scared and didn't know what to do.

At this point, you can stop and ask the child: If you were the king, what would you do?

The child might come up with several solutions, such as finding a brave knight to defeat the dragon or using gold and jewels to exchange the princess's freedom.

In this way, the child is using logical reasoning and hypothetical deduction to think about the story's ending.

  * Abstract symbols and concepts: Teach the child to use abstract symbols or concepts to express specific things.

After the child watches *Snow White*, they might think the story's message is about kindness and beauty. You can then ask the child: Do you think Snow White is like someone you know in real life? Why?

If you want the child to understand a concept, you can also reverse the process by telling a story to abstract the concept.

Besides textual concepts, you can also use mathematical symbols to inspire the child in daily life.

**3. Authoritative Parenting: Warm and Principled**

What kind of parenting style leads to stronger delayed gratification ability in children?

Contrary to common belief, it's not the strict control of "authoritarian" parenting (often scolding the child, ignoring the child's feedback, and only wanting the child to follow their requirements), nor is it the "permissive" parenting (where the child's choices are always correct, and the child doesn't learn if they're not interested or don't want to go).

Instead, it's "authoritative" parenting — warm and principled.

Christian F from the Psychology Department at Miami University conducted a delayed gratification experiment. Before the experiment, he surveyed the mothers' parenting styles and observed the mothers' instructions to the children. Finally, through data analysis, he concluded: **children of authoritative mothers are more likely to have delayed gratification ability.**

The paper was published in *Psychology, Human Development Research and Theory* in 2000.

The experimental process is not detailed here, but interested readers can check the references at the end of the article. Here, I'll focus on explaining what "authoritative parenting" means.

**Authoritative parenting refers to parents who have reasonable requirements and rules for their children, along with warm care and support. It was proposed by American psychologist Diana Baumrind.**

Authoritative parents explain the rules to their children, respect the child's opinions and personality, encourage the child's autonomy and creativity, and also provide appropriate rewards and punishments to cultivate the child's sense of responsibility and self-discipline.

How can you use authoritative parenting to educate your child?

  * Establish good communication with your child, listen to their thoughts and feelings, respect their choices and differences, and avoid criticism and blame.

  * Set reasonable and clear rules and expectations, so the child knows what is right and what is wrong, what is allowed and what is not, and explain the reasons and values behind the rules.

  * Give the child appropriate autonomy and opportunities to participate in family affairs and decision-making, encourage the child to try new things, and cultivate the child's independent thinking and problem-solving abilities.

  * Treat the child with positive affirmation, praise their efforts and progress, provide emotional support and encouragement, and enhance the child's self-esteem and confidence.

  * Establish a punishment system, so the child understands the consequences of their behavior and helps them correct mistakes and learn from experience.

Careful readers will notice that **setting and explaining rules, and allowing the child to make decisions, overlap with the earlier mentioned methods of cultivating the child's "decision-making ability" and "abstract thinking."** Moreover, "establishing a punishment system" is also consistent with the concept of [“punishment mechanism” as an essential evil in family education](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzkwNTM5Mzc5Ng==&mid=2247483868&idx=1&sn=394de757d76115200a55332a04646802&chksm=c0f92237f78eab216867565ca42312a47c2e6b745181f5d017988a1bd58a4a19c0e8db4cd60d&scene=21#wechat_redirect) mentioned by Li Xiaolai in his course.

**05**

**Values**

Finally, let's talk about the things beyond the methodology: values.

There is a primitive tribe in Canada called the Cree. In the past, the Cree people's main source of food was hunting, but hunting was not an easy task. The Cree people often went hungry because they couldn't catch enough game.

In the 19th century, white settlers came to the Cree people's land to expand their territory, and for the next few decades, frequent wars and conflicts occurred between the white settlers and the Cree people.

Between 1870 and 1970, 150,000 Cree Indigenous children were forcibly taken from their parents and sent to residential schools to receive education. Due to language barriers and cultural differences, these children often faced abuse and even sexual assault in the residential schools.

In this context, different values developed between the Canadians and the Cree people.

**Due to their hunting experiences and the impact of food shortages, the Cree people were more inclined to seek immediate rewards. Additionally, the Cree people were more inclined to be aggressive, and even used aggression as a measure of maturity.**

In 1975, Gary Granzberg from the Department of Anthropology at the University of Winnipeg in Canada found 100 Cree boys from the same school and conducted a delayed gratification experiment with Canadian boys.

At that time, the Cree people's material conditions were no longer in short supply, and their food was no longer scarce. They had received the same education as Canadian boys.

The results were astonishing: over 50% of Canadian boys completed the waiting task, while less than 10% of Cree boys did. At the same time, the Cree boys' abstract thinking ability was also poor.

> We view the child as starting out neutral with no innate direction and being propelled one way or the other according to cultural definitions of maturity.

**— We believe that children start out neutral with no innate direction and are influenced by cultural definitions of maturity to develop different abilities in resisting temptation and abstract thinking.**

This experiment proved that **from a societal perspective, delayed gratification is a process shaped by culture.**

In fact, from a family perspective, it's even more so: **parents' words and actions will unconsciously shape the child's values.**

If you often convey anxiety and impatience to your child, and criticize them if they don't achieve results, the child will internalize the value of scarcity and the idea that immediate rewards are more valuable than future ones.

If you often scold your child, only require them to follow rules without explaining the reasons, or if your parents often argue with each other, the child will internalize the idea that family communication doesn't need logic or reasoning, but rather who is more dominant, just like the Cree boys who used aggression as a sign of maturity. This leads to poor abstract thinking and logical abilities.

These ideas can be deeply ingrained in the child's mind, affecting every decision they make throughout their life.

Unfortunately, many parents are not aware of the importance of passing on values and even unknowingly use outdated and partial ideas to educate their children.

Years later, when the child has problems, they often look for quick fixes, resulting in hasty and ineffective education.

**Problems in family education are not formed overnight.**

**Achievements in family education are never achieved overnight.**

Role modeling and constant exposure are the best forms of education, and they require parents to first set an example for themselves before expecting the same from their children.
