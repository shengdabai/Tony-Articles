A Discussion on First Principles

The "first principles" vary across industries and knowledge domains. Our actions and thinking are often swayed by temporary events and situations, leading to a tendency to forget the first principles. Whether we call it "original intention," "original purpose," or "first principles," what we're emphasizing is the awareness of first principles.

*Waiting for Godot* is a famous absurdist modern play. On stage, two people are just talking nonsense and doing silly things from start to finish. But every time a segment is about to end, one of them says, "What are we doing?" The other replies, "We are waiting for Godot." "Who is Godot?" "We don't know." Then they start talking nonsense and doing silly things again, repeating the same dialogue when the next segment begins.

At first glance, *Waiting for Godot* seems boring, but by the end, it's deeply moving: it's exactly our survival state. It's a description of our behavior and thinking. From a local perspective, doing something might seem meaningful—like you hit me, I hit you; you squeeze me, I squeeze you… competing for superiority in various ways. But when someone asks, "What are we doing?" you say, "We are waiting for Godot"—a pre-set answer. But who is Godot? No one knows. Godot is supposedly a phonetic variation of "God" in French, but whether he's a person, a god, or a train, no one knows. *Waiting for Godot* vividly portrays our modern survival state: forgetting priorities and first principles, being busy but without meaning.

Are we really making mistakes due to a lack of first principles? For example, my mistake was based on a principle or assumption I believed in. I tried doing this thing, and although the facts proved I was wrong, it was meaningful—it proved that something was wrong. On the other hand, you might commit a mistake without even knowing the purpose behind it. When we suffer a loss, we say, "This is paying the price," "This is a cost." But you need to figure out *for what* you're paying the price or making a cost. Otherwise, you'll always be paying the price, and the mistake will be meaningless. Is the mistake worth it? Are you paying for *what*? Are you paying the price for *who*? When you understand *for what* and *for whom* you're paying, you need to look at that *what*—entering the second level of thinking. Through thinking, you'll gradually realize: this is not worth it, and you can end this mistake. A "correct mistake" is one where you know why you're wrong, and it gives you a warning mechanism and valuable data. A "mistaken mistake" is one that doesn't provide substantive data.

Elon Musk proposed that rockets shouldn't be so expensive; their price should be reduced tenfold. Musk's tenfold thinking comes from first principles. What does that mean? If you're making small, incremental improvements—like a technology that reduces costs by 18%—we're not interested. We want to do something that reduces costs by tenfold.

Six methods for the "tenfold thinking":

Try to avoid outsourcing; produce 80% of the components yourself.

Make procurement more flexible.

Connect with the larger market. That might be a key point as well.

Recycle and reuse as much as possible.

Don't rely only on specialized aerospace talent; also look for people in the broader market.

Use Occam's Razor to simplify the manufacturing process.

First principles are most suitable for places like NASA, where entrenched systems, insufficient market competition, and distance from equilibrium exist. Take Tesla's recently released truck product as an example. Jerome Guillen, Tesla's global sales and service vice president, explained the logic behind Tesla's truck manufacturing: "We just thought: what do people want? They want reliability. They want low cost. They want driving comfort. So we rethought everything: what is a truck really?"

Neil Strauss then explained this way of thinking in his own words: "In other words, if you want to create or innovate, start from a blank page. Don't just accept any idea, convention, or standard just because everyone else is doing it. For example, if you want to make a truck, it must be able to reliably transport goods from one place to another. It must follow only the laws of physics; everything else is negotiable, even government regulations. Just remember that the goal is not to reinvent the truck, but to create the best truck, regardless of whether it resembles the original one or not."

Neil Strauss said: "This way of thinking allows Musk to view the industry more objectively than those who have spent a lifetime in a particular field." Indeed, Elon Musk has achieved remarkable results in both the automotive and rocket industries using this way of thinking.
