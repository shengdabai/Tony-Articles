The training process of martial arts is actually not well known, with phrases like "flower punches and embroidered legs" being the most common description I've heard. This is definitely wrong, but I can also understand it. After all, our logic often leads to logical fallacies, and it's easy to define something or someone too quickly, which fits our nature—**the principle of least effort**. Of course, there are also stereotypes, such as the long-standing misunderstanding of martial arts. Some might say, "If it's not flower punches and embroidered legs, then just fight someone, and if you win, it's not fake." But even if you win, it's just a mess of punches. Martial arts itself isn't about competition or winning a fight. Then you might ask, what's the point? Killing people!

Of course, modern martial arts no longer require such things. Instead, it's more about arranging and improving during the process of inheriting tradition. For example, the national martial arts routines have been adjusted according to modern aesthetic needs, making the performance more prominent. But this doesn't mean practicality has weakened—it's just hard to demonstrate. Although sparring is included in competitions and performances, these are all pre-rehearsed moves. Traditional killing techniques are rarely seen in modern competitive matches, which indeed doesn't meet modern competition standards. Many moves have been removed or modified. For those who know a bit about taekwondo, like Tony Sheng, they've noticed that the original Hwarang-do is quite different from the styles promoted by today's ITF and WTF taekwondo federations.

A bit more to add: I've come across various opinions about martial arts on platforms like Zhihu, with mixed views. I don't want to argue with them, simply because outsiders have various perspectives to challenge, and even insiders might not be confident. With the development of the times, martial arts has its changes and constants. I understand the constant as the root, and the change as the branches. As long as the root is held firm, the contribution it makes to the inheritance of Chinese civilization will benefit us.

The road is long and winding, I shall seek far and wide!

Let's continue talking about Tony's training and the basics of martial arts techniques: the fist, palm, and hook.

> **Fist**
> **Action Description**: Tighten the five fingers, with the thumb pressing on the second joint of the index and middle fingers.
> **Requirements**: The fist should be tightly clenched, the palm flat, and the wrist straight.
> **Notes**: A flat fist has the palm facing down, while a standing fist has the palm facing up.

> **Palm**
> **Action Description**: Straighten and bring together the four fingers, with the thumb curled and pressed into the fleshy part of the thumb.
> **Requirements**: The four fingers should be together, and the palm fingers should be raised.
> **Notes**: The palm should be open, and the fingers should be vertical.

> **Hook**
> **Action Description**: Bring the five fingers together to form a hook, bend the wrist, and clench the first joint of the fingers.
> **Requirements**: The five fingers should be tightly clenched, forming a plum blossom shape.
> **Notes**: Bend the wrist.

These three movements are the foundation of all martial arts training. Combined with various stepping, kicking, and body movements, they can create a variety of combinations, each with different application scenarios. To put it simply, the more standard these three techniques are performed, the less damage you'll suffer. For example, take the fist: if the palm isn't flat, even if you focus the strike on the protruding part, the probability of injury is still high. Of course, this is another matter when using tools.
