Selfishness, Self, and Arrogance—Do You Truly Understand Them?

1. **Selfishness—Care Is Limited to the Five Relationships**

Care is limited to the five relationships, and this definition is remarkably precise. It confines attention to interpersonal relationships, with the focus of communication being oneself. A selfish person fears that they might have done too much and lost out, or that the other person might have gained more. In junior high, I was once told by a teacher that I was selfish. At the time, I didn’t understand. I said I was always thinking of others, as a class monitor, working hard and taking on responsibilities. Why was I selfish? The homeroom teacher thought I was a “good person,” someone trying to maintain balance between teachers and classmates, not wanting to offend anyone. Looking back now, I was indeed somewhat selfish. I was only concerned with not being scolded by the teacher and maintaining good relations with classmates. What was missing was communication—sincerely talking with the teacher about my thoughts on class management, and communicating more with classmates to understand their real needs and find better ways to move forward with class work. My understanding of class work was to serve others, provide support, and the prerequisite for that was communication—good communication, learning to communicate.

There is a book that everyone is familiar with, *The Selfish Gene*, by Richard Dawkins. The author shattered the illusion that humans are centered on themselves. He pointed out that humans, like other living beings, are merely carriers of genes, and the existence of a living body is just to better replicate and spread the genes within it. In extreme cases, genes may even harm the organism that carries them in order to spread themselves. This is the theory of the selfish gene. This theory opened up a new perspective for understanding nature. With this theory, we can offer more concise and elegant explanations for complex phenomena like natural selection. The author argued that although genes are selfish, this doesn’t mean that nature is filled with only selfish and self-serving behaviors. This is because nature has many situations of repeated interactions that are not fixed in number. In such situations, selfish genes can also evolve to exhibit altruistic behaviors.

In the age of commercialization, many people promote the idea that “human nature is selfish.” “Is there really such a thing as altruists in the world?” has become a popular philosophical debate. Psychologists have long studied “altruistic behavior,” which is also known as “prosocial behavior.” Altruism is a human instinct, but in social environments, people may abandon helping others due to various factors. Humans, from infancy, are willing to sacrifice themselves to help those in greater need.

Altruism is an instinctive impulse. Although altruism is a human instinct, many factors in the social environment can lead people to abandon helping others. Altruistic behavior is the most important trait in choosing a partner. Among many traits, “altruism” is considered the most attractive quality by both men and women. Therefore, if we want to attract the opposite sex through personal charm, we should help others more. Besides being related to genes, altruistic behavior can also be cultivated through brain training in later life. Some research has found that meditation training can effectively cultivate empathy, gratitude, and prosocial motivation. Moreover, after such training, the brain can undergo real physiological changes.

2. **Self—Others’ Problems Are Not Mine**

Self, in simple terms, is “when it doesn’t concern you, just leave it alone.” I understand this as a very cold phenomenon, and it also reflects a lack of love. My grandfather always told me to spread love, and communication is the process of expressing and passing on love. The phenomenon of self is quite common. For example, as an English teacher, I’ve seen some students in a self-centered state. Perhaps they are the so-called smart kids, but their lack of emotional resonance and empathy for others makes them lose out in interpersonal interactions. In my university experience, I met a self-centered friend. My requirement for myself was to provide some value to others. Whether it’s a simple greeting or a small act of kindness, I thought it was acceptable. Our world is diverse and beautiful. If we carry kindness and release warmth, isn’t that great?

A person’s understanding of self, life, and the world may contain many errors. These errors are never seriously addressed, and people end up believing, “I am like this,” “Life is like this,” “The world is like this,” and then they give up and avoid things in large quantities. The latter two are relatively easier to solve, as they can be changed through learning, communication, and personal experience. However, the mistaken understanding of self is different. As one grows older, the persistence of self becomes stubborn, and once one has determined what kind of person they are, they are unwilling to change. In reality, self is elastic and can change.

Human beings are a very strange kind of existence. Even though they carry unnecessary desires and are filled with incorrect beliefs, they can still live in the world and seem to live well. But how does the true feeling of self feel? That might only be known by the person themselves. If a person always seeks external validation to prove themselves, their self is likely problematic, and they may have overfed a beast of gluttony within their body. However, if a person can reflect inwardly, examine themselves, and turn their gaze inward, analyzing both their own likes and dislikes objectively, they may come to understand their true self and thus have the possibility of real improvement, eliminating the gaps between themselves, others, and the world. Such a person may have desires, but they won’t be crushed by them. They may have errors, but they always have the possibility of self-checking and correction. When they achieve satisfaction, this feeling can last for a long time, allowing them to find real joy in life.

3. **Arrogance—My Thoughts Are the Answer**

Arrogance, I must admit, I have a bit of it. During my university years, I participated in many roles, including school work, college work, and club activities. I started as an ordinary member, following the arrangements of seniors and completing tasks as required. Because of my diligence and perseverance, I eventually became a leader. When I sat at the front of meetings, I knew I couldn’t show nervousness. I began to train myself in public speaking, prepared thoroughly before meetings, and considered various factors. However, sometimes meetings ended without much discussion, and I wanted to make decisions. My arrogant mindset emerged, and I took the initiative to decide. Looking back now, it was because I didn’t know how to communicate and couldn’t fully engage everyone in discussion. As students, we are tested by organizing activities from scratch. Organizing everyone to participate and discuss leads to better results.

I don’t know if it’s because I was the eldest child in a large family, but often I have a strange feeling that I am right, and everyone should believe me. This is a form of self-deception. I fear controversy and worry about not being authoritative enough. But sometimes, admitting my limitations and openly acknowledging my shortcomings can earn more trust and establish authority. I think the above three issues are the ones I need to improve the most.

In psychology, the concept of “high self-esteem” and “low self-esteem” refers to “self-esteem,” which is different from the “self-respect” commonly used by Chinese people. In English, it’s called *self-esteem*, which can be understood simply as a person’s self-liking, whether they affirm themselves and accept themselves. “High self-esteem” is somewhat like saying someone “feels good about themselves.” High self-esteem is actually an advantageous trait. People with high self-esteem are more proactive in their work and are willing to stick to their ideas and beliefs. High self-esteem is particularly important for certain professions, such as sales, where frequent rejections have less negative impact. Research also shows that high self-esteem is related to a person’s level of happiness.

“Arrogance gets attention, but humility creates results; pride can get you on the news, but humility creates real outcomes.” Therefore, leaders or anyone aspiring to be a leader should ask themselves if they have the confidence to remain humble and acknowledge that they are not all-knowing.

4. **Emotional Intelligence**

At first, my understanding of emotional intelligence was the ability to perceive one’s own and others’ emotions—not just sensing one’s own emotional changes, but also perceiving others’ emotions. Here, I quote an article by Thomas Chamorro-Premuzic, professor of psychology at the London School of Business and a organizational psychologist, published in *Harvard Business Review*, which outlines five key steps to cultivating emotional intelligence:

First, from self-deception to self-awareness. Personality, or emotional intelligence, consists of two parts: self-evaluation (how we see ourselves) and others’ evaluation (how others see us). For most people, the difference between self-evaluation and others’ evaluation leads them to ignore external feedback and their own behavioral deviations. True self-awareness is the ability to recognize one’s strengths and weaknesses and understand how these compare to others.

Second, from focusing on oneself to focusing on others. People with low emotional intelligence struggle to see things from others’ perspectives, especially when there is no clear right or wrong. Focusing on others starts with basic appreciation, acknowledging the strengths, weaknesses, and values of team members. Frequent short discussions with team members can help us better understand how to motivate and influence others. These discussions promote mutual assistance, teamwork, and external communication.

Third, becoming a valuable person to interact with. Successful people are often seen as more valuable to interact with; they are team-oriented, friendly, trusting, and selfless. Frequently sharing knowledge and resources without expecting anything in return is highly beneficial.

Fourth, controlling one’s emotions. If you often react emotionally, showing your moods openly, reflect on what situations make you angry or discouraged. When facing setbacks, observe your impulsive tendencies. Not only should you know what emotions you experience in real situations, but also understand what causes those emotions.

Fifth, maintaining humility, even if it’s pretended. Leaders with leadership ability usually don’t care about packaging their titles because they appear humble. “Balancing confidence and humility, being open to opinions, and being willing to admit mistakes are rare and valuable abilities.”

The general principle of training emotional intelligence is not to change your personality but to abandon inappropriate behaviors and replace them with more suitable ones. Overcoming negative tendencies, developing new habits, and improving others’ impressions of you. We need to continuously refine ourselves, sensing emotional changes. Emotional intelligence tests and various assessments are just indicators; more importantly, how we improve emotional intelligence through communication. With this mindset, gaining feedback from communication can lead to more improvements in emotional intelligence. Let’s encourage each other!

5. **Dunning-Kruger Effect**

Finally, I’d like to share a psychological phenomenon: the Dunning-Kruger effect. You are not as good as you think you are! The Dunning-Kruger effect is what we commonly refer to as “feeling good about oneself,” “the ignorant are fearless,” or “overestimating one’s abilities.” In academic terms, the Dunning-Kruger effect is defined as: people with lower ability tend to overestimate their own abilities, at least rating themselves above average; whereas people with higher ability tend to underestimate their own abilities.

“People with lower ability tend to overestimate their own abilities.” This is an extension of the Dunning-Kruger effect. In our lives, people who overestimate themselves are everywhere. Honestly, I am like that too. We can ask ourselves: do you also think you are better than your superior? Even more, do you think you are not far behind your superior? If our feelings are truly like this, then our overestimation might fall under the Dunning-Kruger effect.

Why does the Dunning-Kruger effect occur? This is related to the benchmark people use when evaluating themselves. If God were to evaluate everyone, God would consider all information to make a judgment. But we are not God. When we evaluate ourselves, we first recall our own information, i.e., we first think, “How am I?” and then use that self-evaluation as a benchmark to judge others, evaluating, “How are they?” Therefore, the difference in evaluation benchmarks leads to the illusion that we are very capable.

From another perspective, in the areas where we are relatively less capable, we lack information about others’ true abilities. Because our ability is low, we find it hard to understand people who are more capable than us. Therefore, many of others’ strengths we don’t recognize, and when we evaluate, the easiest to recall is our own strengths, and we compare ourselves to others’ weaknesses. This comparison easily leads to confidence.
