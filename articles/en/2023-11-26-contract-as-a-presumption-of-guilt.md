In commercial activities, contracts are regarded as the foundation of enforceability. Many people believe that signing a contract can help avoid risks, but contracts often contain various issues.

The two parties involved in a contract are rarely on equal footing. One party often has more resources and advantages, allowing them to take the initiative in contract negotiations and set unfair terms.

For example, many companies add unreasonable or trap clauses in contracts, while the other party may lack the time and experience to check whether the contract content is legal and fair.

In such cases, the terms in the contract are reasonable for the experienced and well-capitalized party, but overly harsh for the other.

Contracts are often drafted using templates, but these templates usually fail to fully adapt to specific situations.

As a result, contracts become generalized and fail to provide accurate judgments on the details of the issues they are meant to cover. This generalization may lead to misleading or omitted information, potentially causing losses to the other party.

Some terms in contracts are ambiguous or open to interpretation, which may bring about risks of uncertainty and irreversible losses.

For instance, some contracts explicitly stipulate uncertain obligations and costs, such as termination clauses and liability provisions in commercial contracts. Often, due to a lack of complete information and expectations, the other party is unable to properly assess and manage these risks.

Contracts are not inherently equal for both parties; they are generalized and uncertain. Based on this, we can propose the concept of "presumption of guilt," meaning that contracts should be viewed as such.

We can approach contracts from this perspective and be more cautious when signing them to avoid unnecessary risks and losses.
