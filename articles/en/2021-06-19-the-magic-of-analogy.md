Wikipedia: Analogy (English: Analogy, from ancient Greek, meaning proportional), or analogy, is a cognitive process that transfers information from one specific thing to another. By comparing two things, analogy clearly reveals their similarities and applies the characteristics of a known thing to an unknown one, though the two may not have a real origin in common, and the analogy may not necessarily be "reasonable." It plays an important role in memory, communication, and problem-solving. Different disciplines have their own definitions. For example, the nucleus of an atom and the electron orbitals can be compared to the way planets orbit the sun in the solar system. In rhetoric, metaphor is sometimes a form of analogy, such as comparing the moon to a silver coin. In biology, analogous structures formed by convergent evolution, such as the wings of mammals, reptiles, and birds, are similar concepts. Analogy is an important way of thinking for humans, useful for identifying problems, explaining concepts, and discovering new things or functions. Analogy can be divided into two types: near analogy, where two things are mostly similar, allowing the characteristics of A to be equated to B; and far analogy, where two things are very different but share an important similarity.

The process of analogical learning involves accessing the concept of the known analogy source, comparing it with the target object, evaluating the match between the analogy and the target, accessing the inferences between them, and extracting their commonalities. The process of analogical learning has been very enlightening for me. First, learn, and then gradually try to create your own analogies.

For example, analogy is something we use in daily life. We tell our children that we go to work every day and try not to be late or take time off. But if there's a more important matter, such as a child getting sick and needing to go to the hospital, or a major school performance that the child needs to participate in, we would choose to take time off, go to the hospital, or attend the performance. So the child naturally understands that sometimes there are no ready-made answers to problems.

Good analogy requires us to see beyond the surface of things and understand their essence. But how do we know what the essence of something really is? The answer is that there is no "inner," unique, or "essential" nature to things. What you see depends on how you look at it—that is, your perspective. Take a Tesla electric car, for example. You can say it's a means of transportation, or that it's a relatively expensive item, or that it represents the application of artificial intelligence in autonomous driving, or that it's an achievement of Elon Musk, or that it's a new project of cooperation between China and the U.S., or that it's fast, or that it's environmentally friendly. You can say any of these things. Different people have different perspectives, and even the same person can consider a thing from multiple perspectives. Each perspective brings one or more mental models, and each model can be used for analogy. Analogy is only good or bad; there is no right or wrong. In this sense, analogy can be considered an art. Why does the world allow people to use analogy? Because the world is rational. Because there are always fewer principles than things, and principles are universal. One principle can be applied to different things in different fields. I guess all principles are some kind of mathematical structure, projections of Plato's world into our world. Whether or not this is true doesn't matter—you can still use analogy. Since the world's principles can change endlessly, analogy has no rules. The role of analogy is to provide you with a hypothetical idea, inspiration, and possibility.
