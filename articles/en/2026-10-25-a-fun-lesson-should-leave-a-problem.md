# A Fun Lesson Should Leave a Problem

> 发布日期:2026-10-25 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-25-好玩的课也该留下难题.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-25-a-fun-lesson-should-leave-a-problem.md)

---

One AI tutor turns an abstract curve into a graph you can drag. You move a point, the graph responds, and suddenly you want to keep learning. Another tutor waits until you seem to understand, puts the graph away, and asks you to explain a different problem yourself. I want both. Learning offers two kinds of pleasure: the pleasure of wanting to begin and the pleasure of discovering that you can actually do the work. If AI teaching perfects only the first, a student may leave happy while also handing away the next chance to think independently.

## Fun is a serious part of learning

A clipping about returning to guitar and calculus divides learning into several visits: approaching out of curiosity, returning because the subject is useful, and eventually coming back for love of the activity itself. That is the writer's account of their experience, not a law everyone must follow. Still, it raises a concrete question. If people repeatedly leave the same subject halfway through, can we first change what learning feels like so they want to return?

I think we should. Advice about long-term memory, however sound, cannot help someone who never starts the problem. Images, adjustable graphs, and explanations that answer the next question can make an abstraction visible and workable. They reduce the effort needed to find an entrance. For someone already intimidated by a subject, that is more than decoration. It may be one condition for beginning at all.

Suppose you are learning derivatives. Here is the plain-language version: put time on the horizontal axis and distance traveled on a bicycle on the vertical axis. How steep the curve is at a point tells you how quickly the distance is changing at that moment. Drag the point along the curve, watch the steepness change, and connect it to speed. A moving line may prompt a more useful question than a page of symbols: if the curve briefly becomes flat, does that mean the bicycle is not moving forward at that moment?

The graph in this hypothetical lesson matters because you change something yourself and immediately see what happens. It has limits. Real rides involve measurement noise, while the smooth curve is there to make the mathematical relationship visible. Understanding this graph does not mean you can solve every problem about rates of change. But an entrance does not need to be the whole room. It needs to help you walk in and start noticing how the room is built.

Someone might say: if AI can use examples from your interests, answer questions on the spot, and make practice feel like using a little interactive tool, why deliberately make learning harder? Adults have limited time and energy. Many do not refuse to think; they simply cannot persist with a conventional textbook long enough to get a return. Make the process lighter first, then raise the difficulty. That argument takes real constraints seriously, and I agree with it.

Fun can also do more than keep someone on a page. When a learner moves the graph, predicts what will happen next, and tests a new idea against an everyday experience, the learner is already doing some of the work of understanding. Calling all of that shallow entertainment is unfair. AI's strength is its ability to redraw an explanation in response to the next question, giving a stuck learner something else to try. A printed page will not redraw itself when you frown. An interactive tool can.

The trouble is the success signal. Wanting to click into the next lesson tells us the entrance worked. Explaining an unfamiliar problem, spotting an error, and correcting it without being carried through each step tells us something closer to whether the learner has learned. Those signals can arrive together, or they can separate. We easily mistake the first for the second because it comes quickly and feels good. That moment when an explanation suddenly makes sense gives a learner a reason to check further, not proof that the learning is done.

## When a lesson feels too smooth

[A randomized study by Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) makes this distinction hard to ignore. It asked 52 developers who already used Python but did not know a particular library to complete a new task. The group with AI averaged 50% on a quiz immediately afterward; the group without AI averaged 67%. The AI group finished slightly faster, but the difference was too small to rule out chance variation, so the study cannot establish that AI made this task faster. The quiz emphasized reading code, finding bugs, and grasping concepts. This was a small study of short-term programming learning. It cannot settle what happens in calculus or over months of study.

I care less about which score looks prettier than about the two different things a task can leave behind: a working result and a person's ability to notice trouble next time. AI can help produce the first result. If the learner never has to make a judgment while producing, explaining, or checking the result, the second may fail to grow alongside it. That is a working hypothesis I draw from the study, not a claim that AI use inevitably makes people learn less.

There is another detail worth placing beside the bicycle graph. When the researchers reviewed screen recordings and grouped participants by how they used AI, they found lower quiz performance among some who let it write everything or debug for them. Some who asked conceptual questions or pressed for explanations of generated code did better. Those patterns are observational; they do not establish that a particular way of asking questions caused the higher scores. They do suggest something worth examining: the interface may be the same, while the step the learner hands over is different.

Return to the cycling curve. If AI marks the correct slope whenever you hesitate and then supplies the full explanation, the graph becomes an answer display. You may see the right answer many times and come to recognize it, yet still have no idea where to put the first mark on an empty set of axes. If the tool asks you to predict which part shows greater speed, then reveals the difference and asks why you changed your mind, your mistake points toward the next move. Both interactions can feel smooth. They exercise different actions.

This distinction predates AI. In [experiments published by Karpicke and Blunt in 2011](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf), undergraduates studied science texts by either drawing concept maps while looking at the material or reading and then trying to recall and reconstruct it without the text in view. A concept map puts ideas in boxes and uses lines to show how they relate. For these learners and materials, recall practice led to better performance on comprehension and inference questions a week later. The study did not test AI or compare every possible way to learn. It shows that bringing information back from memory can itself be part of learning, rather than merely a way to check a score.

Think of learning to ride a bicycle while someone holds the saddle. The support helps you dare to start, and you can enjoy moving forward. But if the helper corrects your direction before every wobble, you cannot tell whether you can balance. The graph plays a similar role: interaction helps you see a relationship, while independent reconstruction exposes whether you can bring the relationship back yourself. Physical balance and mathematics are different skills. The analogy explains only this mechanism: help that always takes over the crucial correction can hide the error a learner needs to see.

## Who should do the hard part?

A further objection is that unaided work is an outdated demand. If AI will be available at work, why insist that someone answer without it? That is a serious argument. When the goal is to deliver familiar work faster, letting a tool complete part of it is sensible. Delivering an output and developing a new ability call for different ways of judging success. Turning every work task into a closed-book exam would waste people's time.

But an available tool does not eliminate the need for human judgment. You still have to decide whether the question was framed correctly, whether the answer can be used, and what to do with an exception. As output expands, the area that needs checking may expand too. [Anthropic's analysis of roughly 400,000 Claude Code sessions](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-expertise) covers October 2025 through April 2026. It found that sessions showing more task-specific knowledge in the user were more likely to meet the researchers' definition of "verified success": the session was judged to have achieved its goal and had at least one strong piece of supporting evidence, such as passing tests or a code commit matching the task. Most of the difference appeared between novice and intermediate users. This is a correlation based on classification of session transcripts. The researchers did not observe the final real-world fate of the work, and they did not establish that expertise alone caused success.

The practical judgment I take from it is simple. We need not perform every action ourselves, but we should learn to state constraints, notice anomalies, and change course when a result does not fit. While learning something new, those abilities are unlikely to grow from watching an uninterrupted demonstration alone. In the bicycle-graph example, if you can describe speed only while copying the graph, but cannot explain why switching the vertical axis to a water level changes the meaning, you have not yet grasped the relationship well enough to reuse it. The rate at which the water level rises does not automatically equal the rate at which water flows into the tank; the tank's shape affects how the two are related.

Another kind of difficulty is often mistaken for needless punishment. A teacher explains a basic principle, then asks students to construct a plausible but flawed solution, identify the flaw, and repair it. The point is to make them meet the boundary of a rule. In [research published by Sarah Shi Hui Wong in 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-026-09819-x), 276 university students first learned principles of experimental design. They then practiced by deliberately designing and correcting their own invalid experiments, correcting errors made by others, or designing valid experiments without that error step. The group that made and corrected its own errors did better on a more complex new design task. The study still tested experimental design. It did not show that deliberately making a calculus error would produce the same result.

Even with that boundary, the study suggests a teaching move worth trying. After understanding a graph about speed, a learner could deliberately draw a curve that would mislead someone and explain why. Suppose the vertical axis quietly changes from distance traveled to distance remaining, while the explanation keeps the old claim about a positive or negative slope. Identifying the moment the variable changed may reveal more about the rule's boundary than answering another familiar question correctly. This exercise belongs in low-stakes practice with immediate correction. Deliberate errors have no place in work that must be delivered accurately.

Difficulty has to be calibrated. Someone who has never seen coordinate axes may learn little from being asked to invent a faulty graph; they may not even know where to look for the fault. In Wong's study, participants were taught the principles first and could consult the material during practice. That detail matters. Give someone an entrance, then let them work on the boundary. That is different from leaving them to guess at an unfamiliar problem.

## Connecting the two pleasures

Here is how I propose designing an AI tutor: have it adjust its grip like the person holding a bicycle saddle. At the start, draw the graph and make the learner willing to try. Once the learner says they understand, do not rush to the next lesson. Leave a stretch in which the learner steers. The tutor can give a hint, explain again, and point out a gap after hearing an answer. It need not become stern. It only needs to stop filling every blank before the learner reaches it.

You can try this today without changing software. If you are using AI to learn a new concept, first ask it for an explanation using something familiar and for a graph or small exercise whose conditions you can change. For rates of change, start with time and distance. Move along the curve and predict how its steepness will change. The familiar example gets you talking. It does not award a diploma.

Then hide the explanation for a moment and write one complete sentence of your own: what does each axis represent, and what does the slope mean in this situation? If you cannot write it, look back, then close the explanation and try again. It feels clumsy, but it exposes what is vague. A nod inside your head is not enough. AI can turn a half-understood sentence into an elegant paragraph so easily that you may mistake its paragraph for your own thought.

Finally, ask AI for a situation that looks different on the surface but may involve the same relationship. Ask it to hold back the answer. Move from distance traveled on a bicycle to the water level in a tank, for example. Decide for yourself where the idea of a rate of change still applies and where the changed meaning of the quantities forces you to reason again. If you are wrong, ask for the first faulty judgment only. Fix it yourself before requesting the full explanation. I also suggest trying another similar variation independently the next day to check what you can still explain after some time, instead of relying only on answers from the same sitting.

This is my proposed exercise, not a standard procedure validated by these studies or a result from my own practice. It brings together mechanisms from several studies: an inviting entrance, a chance to recall without the material in view, an occasion to explain and correct an error, and a check in a changed situation. It suits learning when you actually want to gain an ability. If you only need to understand a document well enough to handle a one-off task, a direct, clear AI answer may serve you better. Making every use of a tool feel like an exam would exhaust people before they learned anything.

I would not take temporary frustration as proof that an exercise worked, either. To judge a lesson, ask both whether you want to continue and whether, after some time, you can explain independently and identify what changes when the problem changes. The first signal protects interest; the second protects ability. Either can mislead us on its own. Too hard, and people leave. Too smooth, and they leave believing they have already learned.

If there is a subject you only seem to understand while AI is beside you, would you leave yourself one problem to answer first and, if necessary, correct after your next lesson?
