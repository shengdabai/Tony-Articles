# AI Raises the Average—and Eats the Tail

> 发布日期:2026-09-13 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-13-AI 会抬高平均分，也会吃掉长尾.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-13-ai-raises-the-average-and-eats-the-tail.md)

---

While sorting through my notes this morning, I noticed something slightly unsettling.

The material came from very different places. One note was about learning. Another was about building products. Another was about how small teams experiment. Yet after being processed into neat summaries, they began to take the same shape: one central insight, three supporting reasons, and a tidy recommendation at the end.

Each note had become clearer.

The collection had become flatter.

That is the AI trade-off I think we are underestimating. We usually judge AI one output at a time. Is this draft better than my first draft? Is this idea more polished? Is this answer useful?

Often, the answer is yes.

But a shelf can contain better-written pages and still become a worse library. If every page improves toward the same center, the average rises while the edges disappear.

Those edges are where many new ideas begin.

## A Better Draft Can Make the Whole Room Poorer

There is now evidence for this uncomfortable split between individual quality and collective variety.

In a preregistered experiment on short-story writing, access to generative AI ideas led evaluators to rate stories as more creative, better written, and more enjoyable. The gains were especially strong for writers who scored lower without assistance. But the AI-assisted stories also became more similar to one another. The researchers described this as a social dilemma: each writer had a reason to use the tool, while the group produced a narrower range of work. [The full study was published in *Science Advances*.](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)

A separate set of five experiments found that ChatGPT helped people generate more creative solutions than working alone or using conventional web search. The improvement was stronger for incremental ideas than for radically new ones. [That result appeared in *Nature Human Behaviour*.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01953-1) A later reanalysis of the same experimental data argued that the pool of ideas became less diverse even as the average idea improved. [The reanalysis makes the distinction explicit.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02173-x)

This difference matters far beyond writing.

Imagine ten people in a product meeting. Without AI, several suggestions may be clumsy, two may be impractical, and one may sound almost embarrassing. With AI, everyone arrives with a coherent proposal, a market rationale, and a plausible execution plan.

The meeting looks smarter.

But if eight proposals begin from the same model-shaped assumptions, the team may have upgraded the presentation while shrinking the search space. The awkward suggestion might have been the only one based on a strange customer behavior that no standard framework could explain.

Average quality is visible immediately. Lost variation is usually visible only after the opportunity has passed.

## The Tail Often Disappears Before the First Sentence

The problem is not simply that AI produces clichés. Current models can produce surprising language, unusual combinations, and useful counterexamples. The more subtle problem is sequence.

When I ask AI for ideas before I have recorded my own observations, its answer becomes the starting point. A fluent starting point is a powerful anchor. I begin editing its map instead of drawing mine.

The raw details then get filtered out.

In learning, the valuable detail may be the wrong explanation I almost believed. It reveals how my mental model broke. If AI gives me the clean explanation first, I may understand the answer without discovering my own failure mode.

In product work, the valuable detail may be one odd complaint that does not fit the dashboard. A generic analysis will classify it as noise. But that complaint may be the first sign that users are doing something the product was never designed to support.

In writing, the valuable detail may be a physical scene: a delayed reply, a tool left unused, a sentence someone could not finish. It looks less important than the abstract lesson. Yet it is often the only part another writer could not have generated from the same public information.

The long tail is not a collection of random eccentricities. It is evidence that has not yet earned a category.

If we let AI categorize too early, that evidence is compressed into what the system already knows how to name. Once the detail is gone, asking the model to “be more original” rarely brings it back. The model can generate variation, but it cannot recover an observation we failed to preserve.

## Homogenization Is a Risk, Not a Law

It would be convenient to stop here and declare that AI always makes people think alike. The research does not support such a clean conclusion.

One large dynamic experiment, involving more than 800 participants across more than 40 countries, found that high exposure to AI-generated examples increased collective idea diversity without improving the creativity of individual ideas. In that setting, AI made ideas different, not better. [The study examined what happens when AI ideas enter a continuing cultural loop.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13481)

That result points to an important correction: AI does not carry a fixed “homogenize” switch. The effect depends on the task, the examples, the people, and the point at which the model enters the process.

A recent preregistered preprint offers a particularly useful clue. Participants either created metaphors without AI, received AI-generated ideas, or formed their own metaphor first and used AI only to refine the explanation. AI-led ideation compressed collective diversity, while AI refinement largely preserved it. The paper is new and has not yet gone through peer review, so it should not be treated as the final word. But its workflow distinction is practical enough to test today. [The preprint reports the design and its limitations.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26899)

The question, then, is not “Should I use AI for creative work?”

It is “At what moment should AI enter?”

If it enters before contact with reality, it can become a substitute for observation. If it enters after the observation has been captured, it can become a multiplier.

## A Tail-First Workflow

I am experimenting with a simple rule: human first for variance, AI second for leverage.

Before opening an AI tool, I spend a few minutes making a raw evidence note. It needs only three things:

- one detail that surprised me;
- one exception that does not fit the obvious explanation;
- one provisional judgment I am willing to revise.

The note does not need to be elegant. In fact, elegance is suspicious at this stage. Its job is to preserve contact with the event before a framework cleans it up.

Then I give the material to AI, but I do not ask for a thesis yet. I use a prompt closer to this:

> Sort these observations into common patterns, anomalies, and conflicts. Tell me which detail a generic summary would erase. Challenge my provisional judgment, but do not replace it yet.

Only after that do I choose the bet: which anomaly deserves to become the center of the piece, experiment, or product decision? Once that choice is mine, AI can help with structure, counterarguments, research, editing, and translation. This is where its leverage is enormous.

There is also a quick test I now find useful:

> If my private observation were removed, could almost anyone produce the same result from public information and the same model?

If the answer is yes, the work may be polished, but its source of difference is gone.

This test does not demand novelty for novelty’s sake. Routine work should converge. A contract checklist, a deployment procedure, and a factual summary often become more valuable when variation is removed. Consistency is the point.

Discovery work has a different objective. Its job is to search before it standardizes. Using one pipeline for both production and discovery is like asking a factory line to run efficiently while changing the product at every station.

We need two modes: one that protects variation and one that exploits what has already been learned.

## Do Not Outsource the Source of Difference

I do not want to romanticize rough human work. Most first ideas are not hidden masterpieces. Many are simply incomplete, repetitive, or wrong. AI is genuinely good at raising the floor, and that is a major gain.

But raising the floor is not the same as choosing where to build the next floor.

The strongest human-AI system is not the one in which the machine supplies every first thought. It is the one in which a person keeps bringing back material the machine could not have encountered on its own: a local contradiction, an embodied experience, an uncomfortable exception, a question that does not yet have the right vocabulary.

Then the machine can do what it does best. It can connect that small piece of reality to a much larger map.

This, to me, is one meaning of becoming stronger with AI rather than merely becoming faster. We should automate the expansion of an idea without automating away its origin.

Before asking AI what to think, write down what you saw that it could not have seen.

It is a small habit. It may also be the line between using the same powerful tool as everyone else and slowly becoming more like everyone else.
