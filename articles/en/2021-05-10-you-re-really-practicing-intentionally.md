# You're Really Practicing Intentionally?

Intentional practice is a way of practicing that can bring faster results. Intentional practice requires staying in the learning zone, receiving clear feedback, continuous supervision, and adjusting strategies when there are no results. Due to limitations in resources, energy, and motivation, not everyone has the opportunity to engage in intentional practice. Mr. Wan Weigang mentioned four steps of intentional practice: first, practice only in the learning zone. Second, do a lot of repetitive training. Third, continuously receive feedback. Fourth, maintain high concentration.

What am I doing now? I'm doing my postgraduate studies, which is my intentional practice. The educational leadership course falls into the learning zone for me—it's challenging, but not easy enough to make me comfortable. The academic journey of a postgraduate student is a great learning environment for me. It requires a lot of repetitive training, reading and reflection, continuing to read and reflect, massive input, and constant comparative learning. Professors and classmates are all sources of feedback for me, and I always have feedback, which is very helpful for my growth. Maintaining high concentration naturally means being fully immersed, entering a state of flow, and being deeply engaged in the postgraduate process. In addition, I believe that my current reading, writing, and short videos are all forms of intentional practice. The reason is simple: these are all things I spend a lot of time on, allowing me to focus on them. The feedback I receive from intentional practice also thanks the power of the community. In many cases, we can only do things silently on our own. Among the four points mentioned earlier, three can be done independently, but feedback is relatively harder to achieve. Having partners makes it easier to carry out. We ourselves can also do self-feedback, but for most people, this is difficult. It requires continuous intentional practice to develop the ability for self-feedback.

Thinking about intentional practice, I recall the difference between ordinary athletes and top athletes: ordinary athletes are more willing to practice familiar moves, while top athletes focus more on practicing high-difficulty moves they haven't mastered yet. Over time, even if the training time is the same, or even if ordinary athletes invest more time, they will never become experts. The reason is that they always stay in the comfort zone, repeating at a low level. Mental training is the same. We need to step out of the comfort zone and engage in intentional practice. In the long run, intentional practice is very challenging, and I need to keep doing it. I can plan my intentional practice projects, break them into detailed parts, and practice according to the plan. Every day, I can ask myself questions, deeply solve problems, and for each problem, I need to gather materials, organize resources, analyze and argue, and seek solutions. Using problems to practice, deep thinking, and continuously improving my problem-solving abilities. Also, in today's fragmented era, how to efficiently use time and constantly reflect is a simple and practical method we can practice.

I'm still far from where I need to be. For intentional practice, I once thought it was the 10,000-hour rule, believing that as long as I could do it long-term, I could achieve it. But that's not the case. The 10,000-hour rule has two major issues: first, the time required to learn a skill varies across different fields. Second, moving from a novice to an expert requires a lot of practice, not just repetitive training that's the same every time. A person needs to break out of the comfort zone, practice in the learning zone with goals and methods, and engage in intentional practice to make progress and grow. The correct approach is intentional practice, so this is not an easy thing to do. It requires persistence, deep thinking, and determination. It's hard to do for a long time, think deeply, and act firmly. Let's be friends with time and take it slowly.

I believe intentional practice can bring quick results. After all, skills acquired through intentional practice are not easily obtained. The abilities we can master through intentional practice are definitely powerful and bring natural benefits. However, the problem is that this process is very long. I'm doing my best, constantly adjusting. Doing things correctly is not enough; we also need to do the right things, and keep doing them, with a long-term perspective, and practice together!

Stay true to theoriginal purpose of intentional practice. That's why it's called "intentional"—because it requires focusing attention and being fully engaged. Protect your courage, and keep going long-term. Our courage may fade over time, so we need 120% of courage, even if some is used up, we still need to keep 100% of it and act steadily (●°u°●)'

After all, intentional practice is in the learning zone, so it's definitely not as easy as the comfort zone. It requires a lot of repeated practice, which may be painful and tough, but without effort and being strict with ourselves, how can we get through it? Intentional practice requires testing and feedback, which means exams. Think about how long it's been since many of us last took an exam. Isn't that right? We also have performance evaluations, but the nature is different. Performance evaluations check the results of work, while process-based evaluations are hard to reflect. Exams are unpleasant, but such testing is essential for our intentional practice. No matter what form, intentional practice will make us go through what's called pain, but after breaking through, things will naturally develop. Thank you for the current process that may feel painful—it will get better soon ⊙▽⊙
