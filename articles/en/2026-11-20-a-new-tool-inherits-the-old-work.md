# A New Tool Inherits the Old Work

> 发布日期:2026-11-20 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-20-工具换了旧活也得有人管.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-20-a-new-tool-inherits-the-old-work.md)

---

Superhuman’s early users liked how quickly they could get through their email. For one group that valued speed, the main thing holding them back was the lack of a mobile app. The company’s founder, Rahul Vohra, wrote about it in 2018. It got me thinking about what we take on when we ask someone to switch tools. We’re also volunteering for some of the work their old tool already does. When the new feature is especially tempting, it’s worth asking how much of that work we’re ready to take on. [Superhuman’s founder on what the team learned](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)

These people had already tried the core of Superhuman and told the team what they valued in a survey. The mobile gap came from a particular group. They valued speed and would have been somewhat disappointed to lose the product, but they wouldn’t have been very disappointed. This is an account of how active users gave feedback to one company. It made me wonder what lies between using something new for one job and turning over the rest of the work.

## Some people want to switch the whole thing

Making one little feature sounds like a safe bet. From the user’s side, why should I have to open another page every day, paste the same material back and forth, and remember which copy is current? I have enough to do. If one tool can bring scattered steps together and let me finish the job in one place, switching the whole thing may be easier.

Sometimes doing more of the work is how you find out where the trouble is. In 2013, Paul Graham wrote about the early days of Stripe. When someone agreed to try the service, the Collison brothers would set it up right there on the person’s laptop. Graham also described how the accounts that let merchants accept payments appeared to open instantly. Behind the scenes, the founders set them up by hand. It’s an account of one startup Graham knew well. How much these particular moves contributed to growth is hard to tell from the account alone. The team took on the trouble of getting people started so they could actually use the service. [Paul Graham’s account of early startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)

There’s a condition. You have to know what you’ve taken on and be able to do it when the user needs it. A founder setting up the service in person can stop and see what went wrong. A newcomer on their own needs a way to start over or reach someone who can help. Whether software handles that work or the team does it by hand at first, it takes time. If taking it on pays off, go ahead. Count that time before you decide.

A saved summary about AI product decisions says that when a new experience takes the form of an old tool, users still check for familiar features. That suggests doing the accounting from the other direction. We tend to start with a new capability we have and look for a familiar tool to put it in. The user hears they’re supposed to switch tools and thinks about what they need to get done tomorrow morning. The new feature can already be useful, and the user can still have a good reason to stay put if an everyday step is missing. This is my inference from the summary, not a research finding about user behavior.

## Where the feature lives changes what people test

Add a rice cooker to the kitchen and people want to see how well it cooks the rice. They can still use the pot they normally use. Redo the whole kitchen and the questions pile up. Will the big pot fit in the sink? Will the fridge door open? Can two people cook at the same time in the morning? The rice cooker can turn out the same rice, and someone still has to answer all those questions. The renovation changes things people could already do. It’s hard to switch back to the old plumbing each day during a remodel. Software can be more flexible. As long as the old tool is still there, some jobs can move between the two for a while.

Suppose you make an AI feature that turns a public meeting record into a draft list of action items. As a small tool alongside email, it gives people text they can copy into the email program they already know. Recipients, attachments to forward, and earlier messages all stay where they are. A person trying the feature needs to see whether the draft gets the owners and due dates right and flags anything that’s still open. They can correct a mistake and still send the email the usual way.

Put the same feature in an inbox that’s supposed to replace the old one, and users have good reason to try a few more things. Can I find that email thread on my phone as I’m heading out the door? After I change the recipients, can I check who the message was sent to? When I open the thread tomorrow, will the original attachment still be there? The meeting notes can be just as good. But missing an attachment that needs to be forwarded can send someone straight back to the old app. Putting the feature in a different place changes what work the user is handing over and what kind of mistake will send them back.

I also want to know when people start treating the new tool as the place to check what happened. When a small tool hands you a draft, you still look in the old inbox for the message you sent. Once the new tool sends mail, it has to show which message went out and which one is still waiting for approval. If both places keep a copy, people need to know what happens when they change one. Sometimes what they’re handing over is the job of keeping the record they’ll rely on later.

Ask about the actual step. One person who says they want to switch inboxes may just want to read their mail in one place. Another needs to find the same thread across devices every day. The first person’s willingness to try tells you little about the second person’s needs. I’d like to know which thing you used to be able to do would send you back to your old tool the moment you couldn’t do it today. That tells a builder what the word “replacement” amounts to in that person’s hands.

## Test the useful step on its own

In the spring of 2023, Fabrizio Dell’Acqua and colleagues ran randomized experiments with consultants at Boston Consulting Group. In each experiment, consultants first did a comparable task without AI. Researchers then randomly assigned them to a group with no AI, GPT-4, or GPT-4 plus guidance, and compared how they did on the next task. On a set of tasks involving a new shoe, answers scored higher for consultants with access to that version of GPT-4. In a separate exercise, consultants had to use interviews and a spreadsheet to pick a brand for a fictional company. Across the two groups with AI access, the share who picked the right brand was about 19 percentage points lower than in the group without AI access. That’s about 19 fewer people getting it right out of every 100. The follow-up recommendations still scored higher for quality. The experiments measured how consultants at one firm did on assigned tasks at the time. A quality score and the share who chose the right brand answer different questions. You can make a good case for the wrong brand. [Dell’Acqua and colleagues’ working paper](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf)

Back to the meeting record. To check the draft, I’d open the original and see whether a date that was still up for discussion ended up looking final. That checks the new feature. To check the new inbox, I’d also step away from the computer and try to pull up the original discussion and its attachment on my phone. That checks whether the old work can continue. I can do both checks on the same day and learn that the draft is useful and the inbox is still unusable for that job.

Looking at those results separately helps with the next decision. If the inbox fails, did the thread fail to show up on the phone? Was the attachment missing? Did the user lack permission to see a particular email in the first place? The first two might call for a fix. The last may be a restriction the work needs to preserve. With only one satisfaction score, it’s easy to pour more effort into the draft everyone can see. Making a good draft better and making sure the right email goes out take different changes.

Even when you know what to fix, the fix has to be worth doing. Someone who looks up an attachment once at the end of the month might be fine going back to the old tool. Someone who answers email from a phone every day might give back all the time saved on copying if they have to look up every attachment again. The same gap can justify a different amount of work. I’d look at how often it comes up and how people recover from a mistake. Then I can decide whether to take over the attachment step or keep the draft feature usable on its own.

## Spell out what you’re taking over

Even a phrase that sounds clear needs a rule for what counts. In a 2019 replication of a study of violin students, Brooke Macnamara and Megha Maitra asked separately about practice alone and practice designed by a teacher. The two can overlap. A student can practice an exercise assigned by a teacher on their own, or pick a passage to play on their own. The researchers asked the students to recall their practice hours and found no evidence that hours of teacher-designed practice explained differences in student performance better than hours of solo practice. This was a small comparison based on recollections. It’s useful for discussing what the two counts capture. Assessing how well a particular teacher teaches would take other evidence. [Macnamara and Maitra’s replication study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745/)

What interests me is that they asked two separate questions. One asks whether you practiced alone. The other asks who decided what you would practice. Which question you use changes which activities get counted. Bring that level of detail back to tools. When someone says they handled email in a new app, I’d ask where they wrote it, where they sent it, and where they found it again later. Those steps might overlap or be split across tools. Logging a single use won’t tell you who did which part.

Writing down the division of work can also point to a simpler design. The person making a draft wants to hand over a long meeting record. The person checking earlier email wants to read the thread in its original order. The tool can give them a draft with pointers to the source, and they can send and file the message where they already do that work. Once people actually want to hand over sending, you can look separately at recipients, permissions, and the record of what went out. A user can agree to change one step now and decide about the rest of the work later.

There’s one more thing that’s easy to miss. During a demo, somebody can find the old attachment and pass it to the tool, and the result still looks fine. Who’s going to find that attachment in daily use? If the tool does it, it needs the right permissions and a way to show the user which version it found. It can also make sense for people to keep doing it if there’s a convenient way for them to do so and they know it’s their job before they decide. Write down the spot where somebody helped out alongside the result. Then you have something specific to discuss when deciding if that help needs to continue.

## Try it on one job today

Here is a test I propose, not a procedure validated by the studies above. The next time someone makes a small tool or considers switching to one, they can pick a job the tester has done recently, expects to do again soon, and can undo if it goes wrong. The meeting action items will do. Use public material or make up something for the exercise. If you need to send a test email, send it to yourself. Keep the old way available so the work can continue while you try the tool.

1. Write down one step you want to save and one thing you already do that would still have to get done that day. You might want to stop copying out action items by hand and still need to retrieve the sent message with its attachment. Let the person trying the tool name that second job. Give them room to pick the one that matters to them.
2. Use the new feature for just that step, then finish the rest of the work the old way. Check the draft against the original. Look at the dates, owners, and open items separately. Record what somebody still has to correct and what it takes to copy things back and forth. A tiny saving is worth recording as such.
3. Try handing over the whole job with the same material. Keep the conditions for drafting the same and add the one old task you chose. Note which page people open, who they have to find, and what material they have to add. If a person has to step in, write down what they did and which step needed their help. If the tester heads back to the old tool, ask what they went there to finish.
4. Use what you saw to decide how much to take on. If the draft saves effort both times and the old job still has to happen where it always did, keep the new tool at the draft step. If both approaches work and switching the whole job does cut out some back-and-forth, extend the test to one more related task. If the draft needs a lot of rework either way, fix that one step first.

Knowing the material better can make the second run faster on its own. So I’d treat timing as a clue and pay closer attention to whether the old job got done and what it took. If another tester is available, have them try the two versions in the reverse order. If they start with different habits, write those down separately. An average might hide which person ran into which problem.

At the end, I’d ask this. What did your old tool stop doing for you today, and at what step did you bring it back? If you can name the action, you can decide whether to cover it or keep the two tools working together. Another result is easy to act on, too. If going back and forth takes more effort than it saves, close the new tool and get on with the work the old way.

Try that one job first.
