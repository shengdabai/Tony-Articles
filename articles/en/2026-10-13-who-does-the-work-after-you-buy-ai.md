# When You Buy AI, Who Does the Remaining Work?

> Publication date: 2026-10-13 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-13-买了%20AI，谁来做剩下的活.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-13-who-does-the-work-after-you-buy-ai.md)

---

Some AI products get smarter while their users get busier. A new capability arrives, but keeping it useful may require charging a device, connecting an account, granting access, checking the result, and fixing whatever goes wrong. A feature list tells us what the product does for us. It rarely tells us what we must now do for the product. To decide whether a tool actually makes us more capable, I want the whole account: which important work does it return to us, and which small chores does it quietly hand back?

## Mistake one: more features mean less work for the user

An AI summary attached to a clipping about AI hardware relays a pointed complaint. An ordinary object had already handled a small job; its smart replacement added charging, connectivity, and an app. This is a product observation relayed by a summary, not proof that a whole category of devices is worthless. It does expose a question that a polished demonstration can hide. The maker shows the new action. The user keeps that action available. How much work does each side do after purchase?

Suppose an AI pin can turn a meeting recording into key points. In a demonstration, someone presses a button and a neat summary appears. In ordinary use, the path is longer: check the battery before leaving, check that recording has started, wait for a sync afterward, inspect names and assignments that may have been misheard, then decide which parts may be sent to other people. I am not claiming that any particular product follows this workflow. This is one hypothetical device for doing the accounting. Every recurring step assigned to its user must be deducted from the time it promises to save.

Think of a dishwasher. You would not estimate its value solely from the minutes it spends washing. You would also count loading it, adding supplies, cleaning the filter, and dealing with dishes that came out dirty. The shared mechanism is simple: automating the obvious middle step can create work on either side of it. The analogy has a limit. A dirty plate is usually visible. A meeting summary that omits a condition may look perfectly clean. AI therefore adds a cost of checking for errors that are hard to spot.

This account is easiest to get wrong when a product is new. The striking capability fills the screen; setup, verification, and recovery are scattered across the day. One extra tap seems harmless. When small actions pile up and recur every day, they can become a second job. I am happy to put effort into learning a useful skill. I do not want to confuse a product's unfinished reliability work with my own development. Both take effort, but they leave different things behind.

Here is a useful distinction. Does an action improve the user's judgment, or does it patch a hole in the product? Replaying one section of a recording to decide whether a crucial decision was omitted may be a necessary human judgment. Reopening an app repeatedly because a recording will not sync is maintenance the maker should own. If a feature works only when the customer serves as daily technical support, adding more features will not necessarily reduce that customer's workload.

## Mistake two: every action left to the user is a defect

Removing every user action sounds considerate. It can also remove the part of the job through which a person stays capable. Our hypothetical pin could write the decisions and send them to everyone automatically. That would eliminate steps. Who would check whether it understood a disagreement? Who would answer for a message sent in error? Keeping those decisions with a person is not necessarily a sign of primitive technology. A sound division of labor lets the machine handle repetition while the person spends attention where responsibility matters.

A [2026 experiment by Contractor and Reyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849v2) makes this distinction more concrete. The researchers randomly assigned 211 undergraduates to a learning task with or without access to generative AI. Students studied an unfamiliar subject and wrote an essay under a time limit. They then took an immediate test without the tool and another assessment about a week later. The group allowed to use AI scored higher on the immediate knowledge test. A week later, the researchers estimated that its scores were still somewhat higher, but the gap was smaller and the result uncertain. This was a particular student sample, a short task, and a one-week follow-up; it is no manual for every use of AI.

The researchers also examined how students used the tool. Those who asked for explanations showed a different pattern of later performance from those who had AI draft their work. But students chose their own usage pattern, so this comparison does not establish a causal effect of one pattern over the other. What I take from it is modest. Having a product produce today's text for you and having it help you understand how to handle the next task are different services. The first can save time. The second may help you carry a skill away when you leave the tool. A design cannot treat fewer taps as its only measure of success when those are different goals.

An earlier [study by Butler](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf) ran four experiments in which people read passages and then either reread them or tried to retrieve what they had learned. A week later, they answered old questions or new questions requiring inferences. Here, “transfer” means using what was learned to answer a new question. Repeated testing led to better results on the retention and transfer questions measured in the study. Those experiments concerned passages and questions, not every complex real-world skill. They still remind me that some effort is the practice. Remove it, and the task may get finished faster without the ability following the person home.

Checking a summary can therefore mean two very different things. If the system marks uncertain names and missing decisions for me to resolve, the action is short, the responsibility is clear, and I may get better at recognizing what matters. If it hands me a long, vague paragraph and asks me to find every defect, I have become its unpaid quality-control department. Both designs can be described as requiring “one more user action.” The useful question is who keeps the value of that action afterward. If the user keeps the fatigue while the feature list gets all the credit, something is wrong with the division of work.

## Mistake three: a working prototype is already a deliverable product

Another note from today is an AI-generated summary of a recorded product talk. It describes a coffee robot shifting from a display of mechanical skill toward questions of serving speed, reliability, cost, and daily operation. A summary of a recording may omit qualifications, so I will not treat its sales or cost figures as verified public facts. It raised a worthwhile question: how much invisible work lies between a machine getting something right once and other people depending on it to run a business every day?

A prototype needs to show that a technical route is possible. A product people can rely on has a harder task. It has to deliver the promised result across different operators, places, and days. When a demonstration fails, its operator can run it again. When a product fails in use, a customer may have to change plans, explain a delay, and do the work personally. The relevant cost therefore includes more than manufacturing, model calls, and service fees. It includes the user's time spent waiting, checking, and recovering. Omit that time, and the product's numbers may look lovely while the customer's day does not.

A [randomized study of surgical training](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4481178/) offers a useful warning about what we choose to measure. It recruited 20 junior doctors; 14 completed the study. Both groups practiced in a simulated environment. The group given feedback on specific weaknesses and targeted practice received higher ratings for the quality of simulated procedures than the control group, while the groups did not differ significantly in final speed or dexterity. The researchers also assessed work on a porcine tissue model, but they did not test patient outcomes. This study cannot test a coffee robot or an AI pin for us. It reminds us to assess “faster” and “better” separately; the feedback in this study addressed specific weaknesses.

Back to the hypothetical pin. Measuring only the seconds needed to produce a summary can yield a wonderful number. If a user must spend a long time verifying that summary, the speed mostly moved work to a different point. If an omitted word of negation happens to reverse the most consequential decision, an average accuracy score may also miss the burden the user faces. The negation is an example of the failure mechanism, not a report of an incident involving a real device. At minimum, I would measure the total time for a normal use, who has to recover from an error, and whether the same recovery becomes less frequent after the product learns from it.

A concrete story in [Basecamp's chapter on setting product boundaries](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03) starts with a customer requesting a calendar view. The team asked when she needed it. She described needing to see available meeting-room slots while working from home rather than driving to her office to inspect a calendar on the wall. This is the company's account of one customer, not evidence that every customer has the same need. It demonstrates a valuable move: walk back from a feature name to the work a person was trying to complete. Build “a calendar,” and you may create a large new interface to maintain. Solve “see open slots from home,” and the useful part becomes clearer.

The same move matters for AI. Someone requesting an automatic summary might want less work after a meeting. Someone else might chiefly fear losing a decision. A third person may need a record that an absent colleague can use with confidence. Each need calls for different choices about checking, permission, and sending. Find the stuck point before choosing the technology, and a product has a chance to reduce total labor. Start with the most general demonstration, and users may spend their days teaching it what counts as correct in their particular moment.

## Mistake four: scaling requires removing all human work at once

The purchaser and the person who does the recovery work may be different people. A manager sees the purchase price and the demonstration. Someone else checks the output every day, explains exceptions, and enters lost information again. The first person may report that time was saved; the second may disagree. I would ask both to keep an account: who received a better result, and whose work increased? When the benefit and the burden land on different people, asking only the buyer whether the product works can make shifted labor look like efficiency.

There is a fair objection. Early adopters accept some fiddling, and new devices require new habits. Counting every preparatory step as a burden might blind us to a genuinely new capability. If a tool helps someone do what was previously impossible, or if its appearance, sense of companionship, or ritual is part of what they value, a few extra steps may be worthwhile. Some people enjoy tinkering for its own sake. I would not erase that value on their behalf.

The person using the product has to make that trade, though; its seller cannot make it for them. A tinkerer may love debugging. Someone who only needs the decisions from a meeting should not have to learn how to repair a sync failure. The barrier to use is not confined to the purchase. It appears again on the first use, the first error, and the next time the customer picks up the device. Counting only the first success is a reliable way to mistake an enthusiast's extra effort for a product's general ability.

In his [2013 essay on doing things that do not scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes early Airbnb founders visiting hosts and helping improve their listings. This is his account and startup advice, not a controlled test. One lesson is still practical: when a team has few customers, it can take on work that has not yet been automated and learn what people actually need. If the makers themselves would refuse to handle a recurring nuisance but expect each new customer to handle it, they should pay attention to that mismatch.

There is a limit to the approach. Doing manual work early can reveal problems; doing it indefinitely to hide a defective product can understate its cost. Record each rescue: what triggered it, whose time it consumed, and whether the next version can remove its cause. If a team manually prepared recordings for customers and discovered that what people truly needed was a short set of confirmable decisions, automation should focus on identifying, marking, and confirming those decisions. Turning every recording into polished prose might increase the work of checking it. This extends our hypothetical pin; real users would have to validate the direction.

I propose keeping a very thin labor ledger for any new tool. This is my suggested way to check your own work; none of the studies cited here tested it. On one side, write down the work the tool did for you. On the other, write down the preparation, checking, and recovery you did for it. Record time actually spent, not time promised in advertising. Add one more column: did this action make you better able to judge the next case? If so, it may be learning. If not, and it keeps recurring, it is a candidate for a product fix or a different tool. The ledger needs no elaborate system. It only needs to stop an easy bit of self-persuasion: a beautiful result appeared on screen, therefore the whole job must have been saved.

Try this check today. Pick one small task you already do with AI and follow it all the way from preparation to a result you could actually hand to someone else. Mark every action you take as either judgment or recovery. Then ask: would another person using this tomorrow have to do as much of the same work? Repeat the task a week later. If recovery has clearly shrunk while judgment remains yours, that is a clue that the tool is helping. If you still carry the recovery behind every polished result, the savings are sitting on the other side of the ledger.
