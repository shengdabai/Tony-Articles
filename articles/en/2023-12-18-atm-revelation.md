# Lessons from the ATM

Li Xiaolai has a brilliant insight regarding ATM machines, which I am quoting from her course on the Chaos platform: *2016.05.29 - The Essential Course for Product Managers*.

Li Xiaolai uses the ATM machine as an example. If you were to design an ATM machine, what functions would you focus on? Completing the core functions of depositing and withdrawing money—would that be just buttons and a screen? What kind of resources and costs would be required behind the scenes?

The ATM machine provides cash withdrawal services, and it has various explicit characteristics, such as screen color, the order in which cards appear, and the location of the camera.

However, the amount of cash stored in the ATM machine and its management become a crucial part of its core service, because banks need to place a large amount of cash outside for the ATM to use. This is also one of its core resources.

Banks need to ensure that the use of this core resource is valuable. Part of providing a complete service is to ensure that this large sum of cash circulates as quickly as possible, to maximize profits.

Which departments and personnel would need to support this?

First, there needs to be a strategic department to determine the strategic value of introducing ATM machines. The strategic value of ATM machines mainly includes placing them next to banks to alleviate the pressure of customer traffic at counters, and placing them in densely populated areas to make it convenient for users and increase corporate exposure and brand familiarity.

Under the operations department, there needs to be a plan for the distribution of ATM machines, along with reporting data for each machine, such as the daily cash withdrawal volume. If a particular branch has a very low daily cash withdrawal volume, it indicates that the foot traffic is low and the brand exposure goal hasn't been met. The machine should be removed and considered for placement elsewhere.

In terms of ATM maintenance, hardware management is required, including maintaining the normal operation of the ATM machine, such as power outages, hardware failures, paper and ink for the receipt printer. Customer service support is also needed to handle user issues, such as swallowed cards or incorrect cash dispensation.

Throughout the entire operational process, although users only need to input their ID to withdraw cash, at least seven positions are required to provide various services behind the scenes. Therefore, when designing an ATM machine, cost considerations must be taken into account, including the cost of the machine itself and the ongoing cost of the seven positions providing services.

Thus, as the simplest product, an ATM machine needs to meet strategic requirements, provide continuous service, and have effective cost budgeting. A standalone product is meaningless; the key is to provide continuous, consistent service within a cost-covering range.

In other industries, the same considerations apply: ensuring that users can continuously receive the same quality of product and service, and how to achieve this within cost control.

Li Xiaolai's example is very enlightening, but as the teacher mentioned, an ATM machine is still a simple product, because its target users are everyone, regardless of age or gender, and there's no need to focus on user personas or demographic distribution. Most products, however, can only focus on and serve a specific group.

Product design shouldn't only look at the surface; it also needs to consider organizational costs.

In product design, we often focus most of our attention on the appearance and functionality of the product, while neglecting the underlying organization and costs.

However, a good product must not only meet user needs but also consider organizational efficiency and cost control.

Organization is the core of successful product design. Excellent product design not only meets customer needs through appearance and functionality but also improves efficiency and reduces costs in the organizational aspect.

A well-designed organization can reduce unnecessary repetitive work and workload, avoiding waste of human and resources. For example, through reasonable process design and automation technology, production efficiency and quality can be improved, costs reduced, and product competitiveness enhanced.

An efficient organization also has a positive impact on the user experience. Because excellent product design helps improve organizational efficiency, allowing customers to receive faster and more accurate responses. When product design and organizational structure are well aligned, product delivery times are shorter, error rates are lower, and customer satisfaction is higher.

Product managers should consider all aspects of the product, including organizational costs, in product design. To design high-quality products, product managers must be very familiar with organizational costs and efficiency. In the early stages of product planning, product managers should understand the product's organizational structure and costs to better provide value for the product. Additionally, product managers should always pay attention to interactions between upstream and downstream organizations to ensure smooth processes and information flow.

We cannot ignore organizational design in product design. Excellent products not only meet customer needs in appearance and functionality but also improve efficiency and reduce costs through organizational design. This kind of design not only helps reduce internal costs but also enhances customer satisfaction, improves product quality, and increases competitiveness.

In the process of product design, we need to comprehensively consider the product's attributes, target users, usage scenarios, and efficiency. These factors are closely related and influence each other, and are crucial for achieving product success.

Understanding the needs and expectations of target users is essential in product design. By deeply analyzing the user group, collecting user feedback and suggestions, we can better understand their preferences and behavioral habits.

Based on this data, we can adjust the product's attributes and functions to make them more aligned with user needs, thereby improving user satisfaction. For example, Apple, through in-depth research into user usage habits and needs, created a humanized user interface and smartphone functions, successfully attracting a large number of loyal users.

Users use products in different scenarios, so product design should fully consider these different scenario requirements. A successful product should be able to adapt to use in different environments and provide a good user experience.

For instance, Google Maps can provide the best route based on traffic conditions during navigation, ensuring users can smoothly reach their destinations in busy cities. Contextual design can enhance users' reliance on and satisfaction with the product.

Efficiency is a crucial aspect of product design. An efficient product can help users complete tasks more easily and improve productivity.

For example, Microsoft's Office suite provides fast and efficient tools, helping users handle office tasks more effectively and saving time and effort. In product design, we should reduce unnecessary steps and operations, simplify processes, and improve user efficiency.

Successful product design requires a comprehensive consideration of product attributes, users, scenarios, and efficiency. Only when all these aspects are excellent can we meet user needs, provide an outstanding user experience, and enhance product competitiveness.

During the design process, we should listen to users' voices, pay attention to user feedback, and continuously optimize the product to pursue better user experience and efficiency.

With the development of technology, more and more users are choosing online payments, while offline banking services face some challenges.

A few days ago, I wanted to deposit cash into my bank account. I went to a nearby bank's ATM machine and started the deposit process. However, the ATM machine malfunctioned and swallowed my 2000 yuan.

I immediately went to the bank for help and was told the processing would take a week. I was surprised because even on weekends, it still took a week to process, and they said it required a fingerprint to open the ATM machine.

There were very few users inside the bank, which explains why the ATM machine malfunctioning didn't affect much—after all, most people don't need to deposit or withdraw money.

I also realized that the low efficiency of the bank's processing could damage its reputation. After four trips to the bank to urge them to process it, my money was finally retrieved, and I had to sign and fill out forms to deposit it back.

This situation is very familiar to many customers: users expect the ATM machine to handle deposits and withdrawals, yet they still need to go through many procedures. In contrast, the emergence of online payment methods has greatly simplified these issues.

Users can operate anytime and anywhere, avoiding the hassle of procedures and the risk of ATM card swallowing. Additionally, online payment methods allow users to better control their funds. They can check balances, transaction details, and account changes in real time, making them increasingly popular among customers.

Product design needs to keep up with environmental changes to better meet user needs. In the current context of rapid technological advancement and an increasing number of users choosing online payments, banking services need to adjust in this direction.

If banks want to retain customers, they must strengthen the integration of online and offline services, improving customer experience and efficiency.

Starting from the ATM machine, I shared my understanding of products and my experience with depositing money, hoping to bring a little inspiration.
