As the old saying goes, failure is the mother of success. So why is success also the mother of failure in the investment world?

Success itself carries the seeds of failure, and failure itself carries the seeds of success. Success makes people proud, and pride leads to arrogance. They become overly confident, swaggering around, no longer disciplined, no longer striving hard... and thus lose the essential qualities needed for success, and so they no longer succeed. A person's failure is not necessarily a bad thing; at times, it allows them to reflect, become more humble and cautious, realize they are not omnipotent. When they start being disciplined again, turning their past failures into positive progress, success will once again reach out to them. Success, too, has a cyclical nature.

Why is success not always a good thing for most people?

Success changes people, and usually not for the better. Success makes people feel smart. To a certain extent, feeling smart can boost confidence, which is a good thing. But once you become too smart, thinking you're good at everything and always smart, it becomes a bad thing. At the same time, success makes people richer, and when people become rich, they tend to be satisfied, which reduces their motivation to strive and work hard.

Why is timing (luck) so important in investment success?

You can't learn valuable lessons from success. Successful people often attribute their success to themselves, which blinds them to the real reasons behind their success. In fact, many people's success is simply due to luck, because they received help from a mentor. Successful investing often teaches only two dangerous misconceptions: that making money is easy, and that you don't need to worry about risk. These are two particularly dangerous false beliefs.

Why is it optimal for investors to choose passivity actively? Why is adopting a regular investment strategy, particularly the two-cycle eggshell regular investment strategy, the most friendly and anti-fragile approach for ordinary investors?

In the face of a complex world, everyone needs some simple principles. Sardines are also known as herring. Each sardine is small and weak, so they must act in groups. Even when attacked by large fish, the entire school of fish remains as a single unit, changing direction flexibly to avoid the attack. "Each fish is an independent entity, with no leader, yet the school can act as one without scattering. This is the form that future business units should take."

> Their principles are also simple: stay close, align, and separate. Each fish follows the one in front (self-organization), stays in sync with the fish beside it (self-adaptation), and maintains a distance from the fish behind to avoid being caught (dual feedback mechanism). In summary: follow the one in front without leading, stay in sync with the ones beside you without standing out, and keep a distance from the ones behind to avoid being overtaken! Our purpose in doing things is usually to achieve success. Therefore, before taking action, we should seriously consider whether this matter depends more on luck or more on ability (skills). If we understand this early on, we can then act with purpose and direction in the process.

The same applies to investing. Complex and profound theories are not necessarily correct. We must never underestimate the simplest content, which can be practiced long-term. For things that depend more on luck, we should not do them. For things that depend more on ability, we can confidently proceed, staying grounded. One key point in investment strategy is that we must deeply understand: nothing can be effective forever; no investment method, rule, or process can consistently beat the market. First, most securities and investment methods are effective in certain investment environments during specific phases of the cycle, but they become ineffective in other environments or when the cycle moves to different stages. Second, past success itself makes future success less likely. Additionally, as previously mentioned, focusing on short-term gains is a form of short-sightedness, and expecting to make money every day is unrealistic. No one, even the most skilled investors, can predict every cycle adjustment accurately. Therefore, actively pursuing short-term gains, chasing highs and selling at lows, is a waste of time and energy, and may even lead to greater risks or losses. Compared to actively buying and selling frequently, accidentally buying at peaks or selling at troughs, or even accidentally exiting the market and suffering greater losses, it's better to "actively choose passivity," hold long-term, and regularly invest, increasing your ability to earn outside the market, focusing your attention on more important matters, and letting time speak for itself.

What role does human emotion play in the cycle?

Emotion plays two roles in the cycle: first, it amplifies the power of objective factors, leading the cycle to extreme levels, and eventually requiring correction. Second, emotion causes market participants to ignore the cyclical nature of things, and at this point, recognizing the overextension of the cycle is crucial—it is also the most likely time to profit from investment or avoid losses, such as in the third stage of a bear market or a bull market.

Why is it said that as long as there are people, the cycle will always exist?

People have a tendency to go to extremes, and this tendency never ends. Therefore, these extremes must eventually be corrected, not the occurrence of the cycle itself. People always bring their emotional and personality weaknesses into their economic and investment decisions. They often become overly excited at times when they shouldn't, and overly discouraged at times when they shouldn't, because people tend to overestimate the potential of an upward cycle when things are going well, and overestimate the risks of a downward cycle when things are not going well. Therefore, human participation pushes the trend to the extremes of the cycle.

Human psychology and emotions determine the magnitude of the cycle's fluctuations. Objective factors influence the cycle, such as quantitative relationships, global events, environmental changes, technological development, and corporate decisions. However, when people analyze these objective factors, their psychology and emotions play a role, leading to overreactions or underreactions from investors, which determine whether the cycle fluctuates too much or too little.

When you understand the role of human emotions in the cycle, how can it help you with your investments and your life?

People have a tendency to go to extremes, and this tendency never ends. Therefore, these extremes must eventually be corrected, not the occurrence of the cycle itself. The economy and markets have never followed a straight line—neither in the past nor in the future. This means that investors who understand the cycle can find profitable opportunities. Human emotional fluctuations are natural and inevitable. It is precisely due to human emotions and psychology that the cycle exists permanently and fluctuates in magnitude. Human emotional fluctuations are natural and understandable; although inevitable, we can try to be more peaceful, objective, and rational by understanding emotions and the cycle. Building a more objective and rational understanding of the cycle can help us better grasp its trends and find more and better profitable opportunities.

The fundamental reason for the existence of cycles in our world is human participation. A deep understanding of emotions and the excessive behaviors they cause is a key point of this book. Cyclic deviations from the long-term trend line mainly arise from excessive human behavior and the subsequent corrections that follow when these behaviors reach extremes. People tend to go to extremes, and this tendency never stops, so these extremes must eventually be corrected. The cycle does not change.
