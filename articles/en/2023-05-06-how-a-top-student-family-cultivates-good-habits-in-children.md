Today’s article comes from Mia, a member of our community, who shared her insights at a tea gathering on the topic of "How to help children develop good habits."

Mia comes from a medical family; she has over a dozen family members who are medical professionals. Among her relatives, two of her cousins, four uncles, and two uncles-in-law all hold doctorates.

She is also a top student, with a TOEFL score of 110, a German language proficiency of level 8, and she is currently a graduate student in German medical health management, a registered nutritionist, and an internationally certified fitness coach.

In her family, the development of habits and the cultivation of self-directed learning seem to be a natural outcome.

**It's often said that habits shape character, and character determines destiny. Mia's family seems to validate this saying.**

So what exactly is it that this family environment has done right?

Let’s take a look at her insights.

**01**

**Unwavering Repetition**

Li Xiaolai, in our course *The Truth About Coaching*, emphasized that the core of habit formation is:

> **"Habit formation doesn't require reasoning; it just needs enough repetition to form a habit."**

This aligns perfectly with what we discussed in *The Truth About Learning* regarding the essence of learning—whether it's acquiring new knowledge or forming good habits, only through sufficient repetition can new neural connections be formed in the brain's cortex. Only through repeated reinforcement and review will these connections become stronger and more solid.

**Forming good habits boils down to seven words: unwavering repetition.** The challenge here is not the repetition itself, but the "unwavering" part.

In *The Road Less Traveled*, author Scott Peck says:

> **"A person's maturity is measured by their ability to exercise self-discipline. And the foundation of self-discipline is the recognition of one's self-worth."**

We all know that procrastination is a bad habit, but not prioritizing important tasks and instead choosing to play games or scroll through phones is it just a lack of self-discipline, or is it simply not having developed the habit of valuing time?

Actually, there's a deeper reason: **procrastinators often don't see their time as a valuable resource in their subconscious.**

Digging deeper—**procrastination is actually rooted in a lack of self-worth, a form of self-doubt, and a fear of facing the pain of solving difficult problems.**

Therefore, whether we are trying to form good habits ourselves or helping our children, the fundamental logic is: **unwaveringly believe that we/our children are capable of doing it, without anycounting on luck (counting on luck meanscounting on luck, which translates to "counting on luck" or "taking chances").**

True perseverance is doing good deeds and waiting for the flowers to bloom.

My eldest daughter is now 6 and a half. After her birth, I focused most on two habits: **reading and physical activity**, because I've read a lot of research on early childhood education, and I know these are the two most important skills for preschoolers.

**02**
**Key to Developing a Reading Habit: Seizing Every Moment**

**When My Daughter Was Born**
From the moment my daughter was born, I would buy around 30 age-appropriate bilingual picture books every month. The strategy for scheduling reading time was "seizing every moment"—carrying books with me, and using any spare time for reading.

**When My Daughter Was Half a Year Old**
I would read to her in both Chinese and English every day, spending an average of 1 to 2 hours on shared reading each day. The sheer number of books I bought for her filled two large bookshelves in our home. I also placed two bags of books in the trunk of my car, and whenever we had waiting time, like during outings or while waiting for something, I would pull out a few books to read with her. Reading had become her main indoor activity, and as soon as she woke up in the morning, she would sit by the bookshelf to start reading.

**Before She Turned 2**
I worked with my mother-in-law to speak to and read to the child using full sentences, which aligned perfectly with the approach Li Xiaolai taught in his courses on reading and writing for children: **"Speaking Well."**
My husband, due to his busy work schedule, was mainly responsible for shared reading, and I would often discuss with my mother how to communicate more effectively with the child. Since my mother is also a senior nutritionist with over 30 years of experience, her understanding of scientific parenting is very advanced. We had very little resistance in educating the child together, and she fully supported me. When I needed to handle work, I could confidently leave the child with her to be accompanied. Li Xiaolai has repeatedly emphasized in his courses that communication within the family is most important: **"Being patient and not sparing time to communicate the main educational strategies with other family members."** If family members are not aligned, it's impossible to form any good habit—**because we all know that habit formation requires long-term, unwavering repetition.** If other family members have different educational philosophies, the child will be exposed to conflicting behavioral habits. Plus, if many parents are busy with work, they can't guarantee absolute time for companionship, which will greatly reduce the repetition of good behavior.

**After She Turned 2**
After my daughter turned 2, I saw the results of the previous two years of "speaking well": her speech was very clear, her pronunciation was accurate, and she learned to speak faster than her peers in the nursery. The kindergarten teacher said her vocabulary was astonishing, and she often used complex words that other children didn't know. This small success further solidified my **confidence and determination in shared reading.**

As Li Xiaolai said in *Time's Friend*: **Long-termists are often quietly watering the seeds, and it may take a long time before they see any results.**

But during this period of no immediate feedback, believing in the correctness of the method and persistently repeating the right actions is the necessary condition for one day finally witnessing the sprout of a seed breaking through the soil.

**3–4 Years Old**
From 3 to 4 years old, I officially started the systematic Englishfirst introduction (first introduction meansfirst introduction, which translates to "first introduction" or "early education"). I chose a "semi-outsourced" approach for my child's Englishfirst introduction: ensuring two hours of full English immersion with an English teacher each week, and guaranteeing daily 40 to 60 minutes of English reading and exposure to English songs at home. This time may seem not much, but in practice, it was very challenging. **The key was still "seizing every moment"—never expect a block of learning time, but try to prepare books in all possible places.** I had two large bags of Chinese-English books in the trunk of my car, and during any waiting time, like when we were out playing, I would take out a few books to read with her. I also bought some audiobooks, carrying the child's favorite music and stories with me. **Children naturally prefer familiar stories, and the same story can be listened to or read dozens of times without getting bored.** After two years of this, my child's English conversation is now mostly fine, and her spoken and listening abilities are close to those of native speakers.

**At 6 Years Old, Writing Ability**
In terms of writing, it wasn't until the past few months, after she started kindergarten, that I began to practice. Many parents have encountered resistance from their children when they start learning to write, whether in Chinese or English, because writing is so tedious. In this regard, I still used the shared reading strategy: seizing every moment. After kindergarten, I would spend ten minutes, sometimes even coaxing her to write one character, usually writing it twenty times. I would carry paper and pen with me, and during waiting times, like when we were out or waiting at a restaurant, I would encourage her to practice writing one Chinese character or a few English words. About half the time, she would get frustrated or angry because she couldn't write a character or a word quickly. I fully understood her feelings, and **the method to deal with an angry child is simple: "gentle but firm."** No matter what excuse she used to avoid the practice, I would only repeat to her: **"Mom knows you find writing boring, but learning is a process that's inherently boring. No one has a shortcut."** Her parents also started off not being good at writing or doing things well, but through millions of repetitions, they became skilled. **By persistently repeating this about four or five times, she gradually calmed down, or even managed to finish the practice with some emotion.** But I've also noticed her attitude changing. Now she can actively ask to practice, and she also pays attention to how long she spends practicing.

**03**
**Key to Developing a Physical Activity Habit: Leading by Example**
Physical activity is straightforward—everyone is a long-termist, and we all know that exercise is a fundamental ability for survival. At the beginning, I also introduced that I come from a medical family, my father is a surgeon, and my mother is a senior nutritionist with over 30 years of experience. I've always had a good awareness of health and a solid exercise habit. Therefore, the emphasis on physical activity for my child was a natural progression. I've already developed the habit of exercising every day, and even though I'm now pregnant with my second child and will give birth in a month, I haven't stopped exercising. If you haven't yet developed an exercise habit and want to help your child develop one, start by moving yourself first—**believe in your child's eyes, and they will want to try what you do every day.**

From "Little Fat Girl" to "Energetic Girl"

When my daughter was born, she was delivered naturally, weighing 6 pounds 4 ounces, which is normal. She was exclusively breastfed for the first year, without any formula. However, due to my personal constitution, the milk had a higher fat content, and during her 1-2 year check-ups, her weight was always in the overweight range, with her weight index consistently above 90% of her peers. Because I had studied a lot about child growth and development, I wasn't worried at all. I just followed the correct methods, encouraging and guiding her to crawl, taking her outdoors for sun exposure whenever possible, and arranging physical activity whenever possible. When I was at the gym, I would bring her along to play nearby. During my aerobic and strength training at home, I would also involve her in some simple exercises. Even when she couldn't walk yet, I would carry her in a carrier while going for walks. Whenever possible, I would encourage her to crawl and explore her body. Here, I want to emphasize to parents: **Never be afraid of "messiness."** It's completely normal for children to get their hands and clothes dirty while playing outside. Parents, especially mothers, who are overly clean and over-sanitize can be detrimental to children's physical development and may also affect their gut microbiome, making them more prone to allergies and illness. As long as we ensure they wash their hands before meals and change into clean clothes after coming home, it's fine.

Around the age of 2, I would take her out for outdoor activities daily at the nearby community activity center, encouraging her to climb the monkey bars, do cartwheels, and climb the ropes. She started to engage in various ball sports like basketball, taekwondo, and American football around the age of 3. By the time she was almost 4, her weight had returned to the standard range, and by the time she was 5, her physical fitness score reached 98, with her muscle mass exceeding 90% of her peers. Since all family members, including myself, intentionally scheduled time for fitness, my daughter has come to see exercise as an essential part of life.

Now, my daughter has developed an interest in more sports, such as roller skating, martial arts, and football. She is willing to try them all, and due to the continuous foundational physical training from a young age, she picks up any sport quickly. **The key to growing tall and beautiful:** Outdoor activities not only ensure sufficient exercise but also guarantee children's growth and development. As long as it's not extreme weather like heavy rain or snow, I make sure my daughter has at least two hours of outdoor activity every day, whether it's riding a scooter, a balance bike, a bicycle, playing ball, or simply taking a walk. **One reason is that sufficient sunlight promotes eye development and prevents myopia.** **The second reason is that only sufficient outdoor sunlight can promote the synthesis of vitamin D.** Many parents may not know that vitamin D is difficult to obtain directly from food. Over 90% of vitamin D synthesis in our bodies occurs on the skin, and the precursor for vitamin D is cholesterol from food. Therefore, **nutrition, sunlight, and exercise are all essential for children's physical development.** My daughter is now 6 years old, standing at 122 cm, which is among the top in her kindergarten class. I'd like to take this opportunity to share a common misconception with parents: the belief that children who don't grow tall need to take calcium supplements. Although calcium is essential for children's growth, calcium supplements are not a direct way to "target" the bones.

Bone growth requires not only calcium but also two other essential conditions: vitamin D and growth hormone. Vitamin D, as mentioned, is best obtained through sunlight. Studies show that the effectiveness of vitamin D supplements without sunlight decreases by 50%—that is, **no amount of supplement can replace real sunlight.** The normal secretion of growth hormone depends on two factors: **sleep and exercise.** 1. **Sleep** Children in the growth phase need to ensure 10–12 hours of sleep, but "regular sleep patterns" are even more important. Because the human body only produces deep sleep in the first 90 minutes after falling asleep in the time that the body's biological clock is used to. Deep sleep is the time when the most growth hormone is secreted, and the amount of growth hormone secreted during deep sleep is more than the total amount secreted at all other times. Deep sleep is the most critical sleep phase for the body's growth and repair. This physiological characteristic also applies to adults. That is, **if children often stay up late with adults and sleep in the morning, with irregular sleep patterns, even if they get 12 hours of total sleep, deep sleep won't occur.** Therefore, children won't get enough growth hormone, and such children may not grow tall. 2. **Exercise** Bones only deposit calcium, protein, and vitamin D when they "feel" the pressure from exercise. If you only supplement calcium but the child doesn't have enough exercise, the bones have no "need" to grow, and the child won't grow tall. **04**
**Breaking Bad Habits: Simple, Yet Difficult**
After listening to Li Xiaolai's lecture on habits, I had two most profound feelings. **One, I'm grateful to my parents and elders for doing many things right, which helped me avoid many detours on the path to learning.** **Two, the difficulty of breaking bad habits is far greater than we imagine.**
The teacher emphasized that bad habits are almost impossible to eliminate; the only way is to replace them with new good habits. Once neural connections are formed and stabilized, wanting to weaken or break them **requires stopping further repetition and reinforcing new behaviors to gradually replace old ones.** If parents want to help their children break certain bad habits, the only challenge is estimating the difficulty of the task and maintaining a long-termist mindset through consistent psychological reinforcement. Here's a brilliant analogy I want to share with parents, hoping it will help you adjust your state and continue to be a qualified coach when you feel your child is "unreasonable" or your patience is about to break. This analogy is from Stephen R. Covey in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*:

> **"To escape gravity and fly into the sky, a spaceship consumes more energy in the first few kilometers of its ascent than it does in the next several hundred kilometers of its journey."**

Habits are also like gravity. To eliminate deeply rooted bad habits such as procrastination, lack of patience, nitpicking, or selfishness, only a little willpower and a little change are not enough. **"Taking off" requires a huge effort, but once you're free from the pull of gravity, you'll enter a vast world of freedom.**

Now we know that, whether good or bad, habits are created through millions of repetitions. As parents, we naturally hope that our children will not make mistakes from a young age and help them develop good habits. But if, in the past, due to oversight, the child has already developed bad habits, we need to be mentally prepared for **a massive amount of patience to help the child gradually escape the "gravitational pull" of bad habits**, helping them rebuild the neural connections for good behaviors and develop habits that will benefit them for a lifetime. **This is true for children, for family members, and for ourselves.**
