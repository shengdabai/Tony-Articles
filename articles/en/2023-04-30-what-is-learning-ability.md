I don't know when it started, but we've come to expect clear and specific instructions on what to do and how to do it. As for why we do it, we rarely care. Gradually, we've accumulated a lot of unnecessary worries, perhaps even self-inflicted ones, and our complaints about life have increased.

During my student years, exam scores were everything. If you did well, anything you did was justified. Conversely, poor grades became the basis for being useless, and everything you did was attributed to your poor performance. This is similar to the situation after entering the workforce, where performance is measured by KPIs.

From my observations in teaching training, common types of evaluation include process-based and result-based, formal and informal, as well as formative, summative, and diagnostic evaluations. In reality, most teaching ends up focusing only on results, tests, and written reports, ignoring the value of the process.

When I participated in an international teacher training program, one class left a deep impression on me, as it was the last time I was rated as Outstanding. (So, I do care about evaluation results and focus on the final score.) But do you know how awkward it was? That class was actually one of the most failed ones I've ever had, because I didn't finish it.

After the class, when I reviewed it with my mentor, they firmly told me, “This time, you did it.” What did I do? I focused on the teaching process and genuinely addressed the learners' problems. I spent a few minutes listening to their concerns and guided them to identify and express their issues themselves.

I've always been influenced by lesson plans and standard procedures, always hoping to complete the lesson as planned and ensure that all learners understood. But this is just my illusion. Sometimes, when balancing overall understanding with individual differences, I consider it sufficient if most people understand, but this is not what my mentor focuses on.

It's not only because personalized teaching and individual guidance are essential parts of our teaching, but also because our lack of effective guidance leads to learners' confusion. A typical example is when a teacher asks, “Do you understand? Did you get it? Are there any questions?”

These questions are ineffective. Teachers need to use different methods to assess whether learners truly understand, such as having them restate the content, apply what they've learned, or teach it to others. Simply asking Yes/No questions, regardless of whether they understand or not, often results in Yes answers.

Let me share an example from my training. Most of the time, we only focus on the result and care about completion. But taking a cable car to the top of a mountain is different from climbing there. As we often hear, the process is more important than the result.

In fact, I don't think the process and result need to be compared in terms of importance. The key is to focus on what your goal is at this stage. For example, getting an acceptance letter for studying abroad is the goal before enrollment. To achieve this, you need to work on various aspects. But once you're actually studying abroad, the experience itself will shape you in many ways, offering diverse experiences and growth along the way — this is the value of studying abroad.

Last night, while walking with my younger brother, we discussed the issue of learning ability. My brother, from his perspective as a middle school student, shared his observations about the sense of aimlessness and lack of direction he sees around him for his future.

I have friends with high academic qualifications, but I don't think having a high degree necessarily means having strong learning ability. This brings us to how we define learning ability.

Learning, which is composed of “学” and “习,” is often translated as learning or studying in English. We often say “practice makes perfect,” which refers to practice. My definition is not precise or representative enough. I would define learning ability as: the ability to acquire information through certain channels and form knowledge or understanding through practice. It's a skill that can be developed.

Learning ability is not just about learning knowledge or going to school. It's a core ability that allows us to acquire what we need in an uncertain era and produce content or products that benefit us.

For example, earning money. In today's world, information is abundant and channels are diverse. If one lacks the ability to earn money, they can't meet their financial needs — this is caused by insufficient learning ability. Even if we can find various reasons to explain why we haven't earned money, in the end, it's still because we haven't been able to change our situation through learning ability.

Through learning, we can gain information, skills, or insights, which can help us earn money. Instead of regretting for missing opportunities, missing trends, or blaming lack of luck, we should focus on improving our learning ability.

This is a rough understanding of what learning ability is. The next step is to ask: why do we need learning ability?

I don't like creating anxiety or panic — it doesn't fit my personality. I'm just an ordinary person, ordinary enough that you might not even recognize me. I don't want to express strange or outlandish views. I only share what I believe in or can do.

Learning ability must be improved — for a better life, for family and friends, and for a brighter future. Everything is possible, so why not study hard?

Of course, to explain why we need learning ability, analogy is the best tool. Let's try this analogy:

Just like athletes need continuous training to improve their physical and skill abilities, learners also need to keep learning new knowledge and exploring new areas to enhance their learning ability. Moreover, just like muscles need gradually increasing loads to grow stronger, learners also need to gradually increase the difficulty and expand their cognitive boundaries to continuously improve their learning ability.

Or, learning ability is like a chef's cooking skills—attention to seasoning, constant experimentation with new recipes, and the ability to create delicious dishes. Just as a chef needs to keep exploring and learning to master more techniques and flavors, learners also need to accumulate knowledge and experience, master more learning methods and techniques, and ultimately achieve a state of ease and confidence in learning. After discussing what and why, the final question is how—how to improve learning ability. In fact, this question is complex, involving multiple disciplines, and it's something I've been exploring and thinking about continuously.

I must recommend Li Xiaolai's series in the Family Growth Community, *The Truth of Learning*, which has genuinely helped me understand learning systematically and provided me with more learning models and methods. The teacher himself believes he only briefly explained the truth of learning, and he will continue to iterate the course. So there's really no such thing as a one-time, effortless creation. It's all about continuous development and ongoing iteration.

From my perspective, here are the methods I'm currently using:

1. Read a lot, especially across disciplines and non-fiction.

2. Share often and communicate frequently. I've found that sharing and exchanging ideas often brings unexpected surprises and also accumulates more material for me.

3. Solve problems—try consulting. Don't think consulting is difficult. Start with people around you, help them solve problems. Of course, you'll encounter problems you can't solve, and being there is also a method.

4. Review. We forget more than we know, so a review plan is truly a learning plan.

5. Build an external brain. A note-taking system and a search system. Don't rely on memory alone; make sure there's a place to look things up, even if it's well-organized.

6. AI mentor. Let artificial intelligence help you learn, such as having it act as your mentor, constantly asking you questions.

7. Share through multiple channels. Provide more people with places to give you feedback and suggestions, keeping communication channels open.

It's said that seven points are already quite a lot. After all, what you say may only be remembered by three. I'm sharing these seven methods I'm using with you.
