# Build Yourself an Apprenticeship That Doesn't Grade Itself

> 发布日期:2026-10-24 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-24-给自己造一份不放水的实习.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-24-build-yourself-an-apprenticeship-that-doesnt-grade-itself.md)

---

Who will trust a beginner with real work? For a long time, the answer depended on getting an internship, finding a mentor, or joining a team willing to absorb mistakes. Today, the small irritations on your own computer can supply real assignments. AI can help you make a first version, and you can try again when it fails. But the person who sets the assignment can also be far too generous when grading it. I think you can build your own apprenticeship, provided the thing you make eventually faces a test you do not control. A mentor points out errors you missed. Without one, you must deliberately build that job into the practice.

## The assignment is close at hand

01. Start with something that repeatedly interrupts you. Perhaps you rename the same kind of file every day, hunt for missing subtitles, or search through notes for the same passage. These small frictions make better first assignments than a grand product idea. You can describe when the problem occurs and what a successful fix would let you do. A passing annoyance is weak material; a recurring obstacle lets you compare what happened before and after a change. Keep the first goal small enough to see that difference. Frequency alone does not make automation worthwhile: changing the tool may take longer than continuing with a method that is already fast enough. Look first for the friction that repeatedly causes mistakes or breaks your train of thought.

02. Turn a complaint into an action. “This software is bad” invites AI to produce a handsome interface. “When I pause a video, I want to find the word I just heard and return to the same second” gives you something you can check. The difference is not the length of the prompt. It is whether you understand your own work. An AI summary of a course recording proposes using unsatisfactory software on one's computer as material for learning to build with AI. That suggestion leads me to a harder question: how do you avoid being both the customer and the judge of your own practice? At a minimum, state what triggers the task, what action must succeed, and where failure would be acceptable. Leave those blanks open and AI may decide what you need on your behalf.

03. Your own need is a useful starting point. In a [2013 essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham says that people solving their own problems often have a way to find others with similar problems. He also describes Stripe's founders helping early users install their product immediately, in person. These are startup examples from his experience, not a rule for every product. What I take from them is the value of getting close enough to a problem to stop guessing about its details. “I can use it” settles the question of whether you found an assignment. It does not settle whether your solution holds up. You know your own habits and are exceptionally good at working around a tool's defects. That familiarity helps you begin, then hides the obstacles another person will meet first.

04. Make the assignment small enough to fail today. Suppose you want a tool that helps you look up words while watching a video. Begin with one local video you have permission to use. Make pausing, looking up a word, and returning to the playback position work together. Leave cross-site support, phone sync, and the beautiful home page for later. Your first failure should point to a specific step, rather than disappear inside an entire system. A small task still exposes you to permissions, formats, habits, and unusual inputs. Those details turn “ask AI to build it” into “understand why it works.” Small is no excuse for sloppiness: if these few actions do not connect reliably, extra features will only bury the fault more deeply. With a narrow scope, you can tell whether the word was not recognised, was recognised but not explained, or could be looked up only at the cost of losing your place in the video. Each calls for a different repair and teaches a different lesson.

05. A low cost of trying is not a zero cost. The AI summary of that course recording treats software as a way to create your own opportunities to practise, because many mistakes on a computer can be changed and tried again. I like that advantage. I would still refuse to treat the machine as a consequence-free playground. Overwriting an original file, uploading private material, or connecting a paid service makes the stakes real. Start with copies, sample data, and actions you can reverse. The easier it is to try again, the more boldly you can experiment without gambling with something important. Reducing the cost of a mistake protects the number of attempts you can make. It also makes it easier to return to the difficult part tomorrow.

## Practise without giving yourself a free pass

06. Predict before AI acts. You do not have to write every line yourself. Before handing over the task, though, name at least one place it could fail and how you will detect the failure. For the word-lookup tool, ask whether subtitle timestamps will match the video and whether playback will resume at the right place. Those predictions become checks when AI says the job is done. If every session ends with the model announcing success, you may experience a steady stream of surprises without learning which parts you actually understand. Write the prediction before seeing the result, so a later “I knew that” cannot masquerade as knowledge. A wrong prediction still shows what you need to understand next.

07. Doing something often can be another form of repetition. In their [2019 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), Ericsson and Harwell explain that the original, strict meaning of “deliberate practice” involves a skilled teacher diagnosing an individual's weakness, assigning a goal, and helping the learner use feedback for repeated revisions. Practice that you direct toward a goal can be valuable, but it does not become that teacher-guided practice merely because you spend many hours on it. Their work reviews research on expert performance; it cannot calculate a beginner's rate of improvement in software. It gives me a better question than “How long did I chat with AI today?”: “Which gap was I trying to close?” Without a teacher, test results, a user's hesitation, and instructions you cannot understand on rereading can all supply some feedback. None automatically tells you what to practise next.

08. Feedback should point to your next action. If the lookup tool fails once, “When a subtitle timestamp is missing, the tool returns to the wrong position” is a useful record. “AI is unreliable” is not. The first lets you make a sample input, change one part of the handling, and try again. The second tempts you to switch models and roll the dice. You need not write a long report. Keep the input, the expected result, and the actual result. When a similar failure appears later, you will know whether you solved an old problem or dug the same hole again. Keep the sample that failed, even after the fix. Run it again when you add a feature; if the old defect returns, you can see the cost of the new change.

09. Finding faults is not the same as improving. A [2025 survey by Lee and colleagues](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) gathered 936 examples of AI-assisted work from 319 knowledge workers. In their self-reports, greater confidence that AI could do a particular task was associated with less reported critical thinking. The authors also observed that thinking often moved toward verifying information, integrating outputs, and overseeing the task. This is an association in reported behaviour; it does not prove that AI causes a decline in anyone's ability. My practical reading is narrower: when an answer feels unusually smooth, turn “it might be wrong” into a check you actually perform. The parts you understand least deserve particular care: a fluent explanation can make you feel safe before the result has been independently checked.

10. Let the failure choose the repair. Some problems come from a vague assignment, some from missing material, and some from the tool's limits. Rewriting a prompt will not conjure a subtitle file that does not exist. A stronger model cannot decide for you whether that file should be read. Ask where the failure happened, then decide whether to supply material, change the process, or narrow the goal. Separating causes gives you something you can use next time. Otherwise each success feels like a lucky draw. If the cause is unclear, ask AI for competing explanations and use the smallest sample you can make to rule them out one by one. Test the cause; do not let it choose whichever story sounds most reassuring.

11. Change the material to see what carries over. By “transfer,” I mean using what you learned on a problem that looks different on the surface. In [2016 experiments](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full), van Eersel and colleagues had psychology undergraduates study passages and then answer questions from different knowledge domains. In their second experiment, practising recall beat rereading alone, but the advantage became smaller when rereaders were also shown the key information. The researchers studied learning from passages, not programming with AI. Their result keeps me from giving all the credit to “figuring it out myself”: noticing the relevant clue matters too. Trying another video with the word-lookup tool is a check I propose, not a software-learning result tested by this study. A tool that still works on another video is more persuasive than a polished demonstration using the one familiar sample. Change too little and you are just repeating; change too much and you may be testing a different skill. Keep the goal, then change the format or content of the input. If the new material fails, do not declare the earlier work worthless. Ask whether the rule you learned still applies or whether you missed a clue in the new material; those failures call for different next assignments.

12. Change the person as well as the input. You know every quirk of your own tool and may work around it without noticing. Someone else will not. Ask a willing person to complete one clear task, watch where they stop, and ask what they expected at that point. A refusal to try is information as well. You are not trying to prove the tool can sell. You are checking whether the way to use it lives in the tool or only in your head. If you cannot find another user, put the tool aside for a few days and follow the instructions you originally wrote. That is more honest than silently supplying missing instructions as you go. Resist coaching during the attempt; once you speak, you are testing your ability to teach as well. One person's pause does not represent everyone, but it identifies a place worth examining.

13. Do not accept every feature request. In a [2009 first-person account](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/), Steve Blank recalls gathering customer demands for both byte orders at MIPS. Byte order means which part of data goes first when a computer stores it in memory. For example, a number split into parts can be stored with its leading part or its trailing part first. Both orders went into the chip, while Blank had underestimated the burden this would put on later software work. It is a retrospective account of one project, not a controlled experiment. When someone asks your tool for another feature, first ask which action they could not complete. If the real problem is an unclear entry point, another button may make that entry point harder to find. Feedback should test your understanding of the problem, not make the choice for you. Record the obstacle behind the request and ask whether it recurs and whether removing it would help the original task. If you cannot answer yet, postpone the code.

## Take the assignment into the world

14. A self-made apprenticeship has a firm boundary. Your private tool can be slow, ugly, and repeatedly rebuilt. Once you handle someone else's information, charge for delivery, or enter a team's systems, permission, maintenance, and the cost of mistakes change. A personal project cannot teach every part of working with others, and it does not automatically qualify you for a profession. Someone could reasonably say that a real apprenticeship needs a mentor and an organisation to correct your blind spots. I agree with that part. A personal project should aim for verifiable starting skills, then gradually expose the work to other people's use and review. If testing involves someone else's data, get their consent and use only what they permit. If a failure requires immediate support, do not present a personal practice build as a delivered product. There is a less flattering risk, too: when you choose the assignment, you may select only the parts you already enjoy and avoid what you most need to learn. A user's failure or a piece of feedback you cannot explain may then be worth practising more than another feature you would enjoy adding.

15. Do not end the record with “finished.” Leave one page you could return to: what was inconvenient, what you tried, which change worked, when it still fails, and what the next user needs to know. That page separates two gains. The tool may work for now; you may also understand why it works. If you cannot understand your own explanation after some time has passed, the experience is not yet ready to travel to your next assignment. Write for the person who will have to change the tool later. Quite often, that person is you. Record the features you chose not to add, too, and why. Weeks later you can judge the choice you made, instead of treating everything absent as something you forgot. If another person can reproduce one success and one known failure from that page, it becomes more than a learning diary: it is the starting point for the next repair.

16. Give yourself a short apprenticeship today. Pick one irritation that appeared more than once this week and can be tested on a copy. Write down the action that would count as done and two likely failure points. Ask AI to build only the smallest part. Once it works on the old material, change the material and the input; if possible, let another person follow your instructions. Finally, record the one gap most worth fixing. None of this guarantees you will become an engineer. It does give “I'm learning AI” a piece of work you can inspect again. If time is short, stop after defining the failure conditions and running one sample; leave yourself the next input to test. You do not owe anyone a huge project finished overnight.

I would now answer the opening question this way: your computer can assign the work, and AI can help you revise it, but neither should stamp its own apprenticeship certificate. The value of the practice appears when you put aside the familiar sample and let someone else inspect your explanation. The next time you say “I know how to use this,” ask yourself: with different material and a different person, can I still explain where it might break?
