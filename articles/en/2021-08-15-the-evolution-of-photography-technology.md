Technological Development:

In 1826, Joseph Nicéphore Niépce, a Frenchman, used his self-made optical camera obscura and the "sunburn etching" method, exposing for 8 hours and then developing with lavender oil to capture the world's first photograph, *The Birdcage*. In 1835, Louis Daguerre discovered that latent images on silver iodide plates could be made visible using mercury vapor, a process that significantly reduced exposure time, allowing for the recording of bright-light scenes in just 20 to 30 minutes, making photography practical for everyday use. However, his surviving works are few, only the *Paris Street, At Dusk* from 1838. On August 19, 1839, France officially announced the "Daguerreotype" process, a day now universally recognized as the birth of photography. This marked the beginning of a new era of continuous development, progress, and refinement in photography. While Daguerre was refining his photographic method, in 1835, the Britishman F. Talbot created the world's first photographic negative paper, allowing for the production of countless prints and greatly reducing the cost of photography.

In 1851, the Britishman F. Archer invented the "wet plate" photographic process, which had shorter exposure times and allowed for handheld cameras. In 1888, the American G. Eastman manufactured the first "Kodak" camera and introduced Kodak film, with the advent of transparent film laying the groundwork for the widespread use of movie cameras and projectors. German manufacturers, leveraging their strengths in design and optical technology, pushed cameras into mass production. In 1936, the birth of color film brought vibrant colors to photography, allowing people to appreciate the colorful world through photographs without the regret of losing vivid hues.

In the 1950s, Japanese engineers introduced electronic technology into the field of photography, utilizing concepts from American computer and aerospace industries, such as microcircuitry and computer chips, to develop high-sensitivity metering systems, automatic exposure systems, and autofocus systems, leading to the birth of modern digital cameras. The emergence of digital cameras disrupted traditional photographic norms, and the various advantages of digital photography were embraced by professionals and individuals alike, making photography more engaging. Since its invention by the French in 1839, cameras have undergone nearly 170 years of development, evolving from black and white to color, from purely optical and mechanical structures to a triad of optical, mechanical, and electronic systems, and from traditional silver halide film to today's digital storage media. The advent of digital cameras marked a leap into a new digital era for the camera industry, fundamentally transforming people's visual lives.

Summarizing the above development, it includes three major disruptive shifts:

1. From Daguerreotype to Wet Plate Photography

Daguerreotype required careful protection after exposure, with the negative needing a frame and a glass cover, making each negative both a negative and a photograph, unique and irreplaceable. Once damaged, it had to be retaken, but some photographs could not be retaken, so this photographic technique was eventually replaced. The Daguerreotype process was only popular for about ten years, and by 1850, photographers using this technique had already decreased, and by 1860, almost no one was willing to continue using it. It was replaced by the "wet plate" process. The Daguerreotype required long exposure times to capture the subject clearly, a habit that many photographers retained, so most photographs were of static subjects. However, when photographing subjects with lines, such as buildings, the shortcomings of the Pechenik lens became more apparent, with dark corners, distortion, and deformation troubling most people. A lens capable of fast shooting and minimal distortion was needed. By 1860, the demand for camera lenses and the market potential attracted astronomical lens manufacturers, who drew inspiration from astronomical lenses and decided to use a symmetrical design.

The first major disruption in photography was driven by the imperfections in the technology and the evolving commercial demands. With a focus on convenience and practicality, the wet plate process became the dominant photographic method, and wooden cameras transitioned into more durable metal cameras. At this time, the main customers were still professional photographers, and the technology was not yet widely accessible to the public. As new photographic technologies met the needs of professionals, they gradually replaced the first commercial camera.

2. From Film to the Digital Age

The disruptive innovation of digital cameras was astonishing. Courses like *Chaos* and Professor Christensen have both discussed Kodak's disruption, which was the creator of digital cameras but also fell due to film. The destruction was so complete that even today, it's hard to find a roll of film. But the disruptors have now become the disrupted. Disruptive innovation is the innovation that ultimately replaces existing products or technologies in the market. When a new innovative product is introduced to the market, disruption begins. However, it does not target existing customers through established demand and performance standards, but rather through usability, portability, and affordability, targeting new users. New users widely adopted the new product. Existing competitors ignored it, as the new market seemed uncompetitive and low-profit. However, over time, the disruptors improved overall performance and captured existing customers, leading to the decline of existing technologies. This is exactly what digital cameras did to film cameras. Initially, digital cameras had poor image quality, so existing camera companies and most photographers ignored them. Instead, they gained popularity among people who didn't care much about image quality but appreciated the convenience of digital formats and LCD screens. However, as time passed, image sensors improved, and the combination of good image quality with digital convenience allowed digital cameras to fully dominate the market.

Over the 180 years since the invention of photography, the number of photographs taken has been growing, and in recent decades, it has grown exponentially. In a blink, the 800 billion photos shot on film have vanished, replaced by digital photos that have multiplied.

3. From Digital Cameras to Mobile Photography

Integrating cameras into smartphones eliminated the need to carry separate devices, allowing people to take photos effortlessly wherever they were. Initially, this didn't interest camera owners, as the image quality was too poor (sounds familiar?). However, over time, things improved. Innovations like back-illuminated (BSI) image sensors significantly enhanced image quality even as pixel counts shrank. Yesterday, Apple demonstrated how multi-touch technology and wireless connectivity could further disrupt the digital camera market. Through this combination, the cameras on devices like the iPhone can now provide excellent image quality and unmatched convenience. You simply touch the part of the image you're interested in, and it automatically sets exposure and focus. Then, you can instantly perform simple edits and share the image in any way—via WeChat, QQ, Weibo, Facebook, Twitter, and so on. This is a photographic experience designed for the masses, and for most people, it will soon make separate cameras unnecessary. Smartphones are not just replacing cameras—they are more than that. In fact, before smartphones became widespread, only a small number of people used cameras, especially in China. Whether it was the simple and convenient film point-and-shoot cameras of the past or the later digital compact cameras, neither truly achieved mass adoption. Smartphones, however, are different. Without exaggeration, smartphones have truly turned billions of Chinese people into photographers. Today, it's rare to find someone in China who hasn't taken a photo with their phone.

Mobile photography may one day be replaced by another technology

Today's smartphones are already portable super-intelligent platforms, capable of handling almost anything. However, technological development is so fast that what seems obvious today may be replaced by something new tomorrow. In the short term, smartphones may still be difficult to be completely replaced by another type of intelligent device. However, the evolution of smartphones has never stopped, and no one can predict when a smartphone might suddenly undergo a qualitative change, becoming a product we've never seen before. Photography is also evolving alongside the evolution of smartphones. What will the next generation of photographic tools be? We don't know yet. But one thing is certain: it will be a tool that is even easier to use, more portable, and more intelligent than a smartphone, and it will inevitably bring about new lifestyles and industries.
