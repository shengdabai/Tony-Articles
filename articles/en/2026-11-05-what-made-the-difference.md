# It Works. What Made the Difference?

> 发布日期：2026-11-05 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-05-改好了，哪一步起了作用.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-05-what-made-the-difference.md)

---

An AI-generated summary of an interview about working with AI agents—tools that can carry out tasks in steps from a user's instructions—mentioned creating a consistent tool for repeated operations, reducing the differences between improvised scripts. That detail brought a question into focus for me: AI can change many things at once and finally get a task done, while leaving you unsure which change helped.

Here is my judgment: if you want to learn from working with AI, make some of your attempts small enough to distinguish possible causes. A polished result can demonstrate that you have an outcome. It may tell you much less about how that outcome happened.

So this is the question I want to answer: once AI has improved something for you, how do you turn that success into judgment you can use next time? The work needs to be delivered. The learning needs a little arrangement of its own.

## The result improved. The reason got lost.

Consider a public example. In his [2011 account of building Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/), founder Joel Gascoigne described a restrained sequence. First, he put up simple pages to see whether people would leave their email addresses. Next, he added a pricing page to observe whether they would click on paid plans. Only then did he build a working product. This was one founder’s account of one company, without results from a randomized comparison or a guarantee of success.

All these actions advanced the same business, but they answered different questions. Leaving an email address indicated an interest in learning more. Clicking a paid plan suggested that the price had not immediately extinguished all interest. Actually paying added another layer of evidence. An earlier signal could not simply answer the next question.

What interests me goes beyond the sequence. Once you separate the questions, you can say more clearly what you have learned. Even a disappointing result can help you decide whether to investigate the usefulness of the product or the suitability of its price.

AI makes another approach tempting. You can rewrite the introduction, adjust the price, change the process, and add a set of promotional materials, all at once. Then the numbers rise. Good. But so many things could explain the increase that you still do not know which action to repeat.

There is an easy confusion here. Reproducing an artifact and explaining why a particular change helped are different abilities. Keeping a complete record of the steps helps you repeat them. Identifying the contribution of an individual factor requires arranging a comparison. A detailed record does not automatically close that gap.

Think about adjusting a pot of soup. Add salt, pour in water, and simmer it longer at the same time. It tastes better, but that tells you little about how much extra salt the next pot needs. Making fewer changes and tasting before the next adjustment makes it easier to connect an action with a change in flavor.

Still, the changes interact. Adding water affects saltiness; simmering affects the amount of water. Trying adjustments separately can help you understand them, but their effects may not simply add up. AI-assisted editing has a similar complication: a sentence’s persuasiveness often depends on what comes before and after it.

Making fewer changes narrows the range of possible explanations. It does not reduce the world to a single cause.

Checking the output can also create a misleading sense of involvement. You read every sentence, accept some suggestions, and reject others. It feels like serious learning. But can you explain your reason for accepting a particular change? Or does the revised version simply seem smoother?

A [2025 study of generative AI and critical thinking](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) surveyed 319 knowledge workers and analyzed 936 examples they provided. Generative AI means tools that produce text or other content in response to a request. Knowledge workers are people whose jobs largely involve handling information, such as writing reports or analyzing material. Critical thinking here means examining whether a claim holds up against evidence and the requirements of the task. Participants described thinking that shifted toward verification, integration, and overseeing the direction of a task. Greater confidence in AI’s ability to perform a particular task was associated with less reported critical thinking. These were self-reports; they do not establish that people’s abilities had declined.

For me, the reminder is straightforward. Keeping a hand on the approval button does not mean you retain the reasons behind the decisions. Accepting the finished work can close a task. Understanding the changes takes another step.

## Give each attempt a question to answer

That extra step can be small. Write down exactly what you want this attempt to clarify.

Buffer’s pricing page is useful to examine because it did not bundle every uncertainty into the question of whether the product would work. Interest in the idea and willingness to consider its price had separate opportunities to become visible. A founder’s retrospective cannot supply a causal demonstration for us. We can still use that distinction between questions to organize our own attempts.

Your target should be specific enough to point to an action, too. Improving a product introduction is broad. Helping a reader accurately explain what the tool is suitable for gives you something observable. Judgment is still involved, but you now know what to ask the reader to do and whether the feedback addresses your question.

Ask only whether someone likes it, and the answer may combine reactions to the subject, wording, layout, and personal preferences. The more you try to learn from a single evaluation, the harder it becomes to identify what needs changing. That is my inference about feedback, not a research finding that supplies a standard answer for every creative task.

Research on practice offers a useful distinction. In their [1993 paper](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/1_-_the_role_of_deliberate_practice_in_the_acquisition_of_expert_performance_2.pdf), Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer discussed doing work separately from practice specifically designed to improve performance. Such practice targets particular weaknesses, provides feedback, and allows methods to be adjusted. Repeating familiar work does not automatically produce the same improvement. This framework drew on research into expert performance in fields including music; it is not direct evidence for the effectiveness of training with AI.

I find the distinction particularly useful now. AI can help complete a whole task. It does not decide which part of your judgment you intend to develop during this attempt. If learning is the goal, you need to preserve a difference that you must assess yourself.

Holding conditions steady for a comparison does not mean committing to one approach forever. During exploration, you can ask AI for radically different options. When you are ready to assess a particular difference, keep the other conditions as consistent as you can. One activity helps you discover choices; the other helps you understand them. Mix both into the same attempt, and you can examine many options without knowing why you selected one.

There is another shortcut to watch for: treating more variation as more learning.

In a [2010 study of learning transfer](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf)—using something learned to answer a new question—Andrew Butler compared repeated studying with repeated testing. Experiment 1b involved 24 university students. One week later, they answered new inferential questions related to the passages they had studied. On factual inference questions, accuracy was 60% in the repeated-same-question testing condition, compared with 30% in the repeated-reading condition.

Another testing condition used rephrased questions and reached 57%, with no significant difference from the same-question condition. Tests included feedback with correct answers, and the outcome was performance on reasoning questions related to the passages. These percentages cannot be converted directly into gains in writing or business ability.

The absence of an additional advantage is worth remembering. A more varied format does not necessarily teach you anything extra. Rewriting a prompt—the instructions you give AI to perform a task—in increasingly elaborate ways may simply make the process busier. You need to know what problem the added variation addresses.

These studies did not test the complete AI workflow I am proposing. The lesson I take from them is narrower: give learning a clear task, preserve conditions that allow comparison, and check whether the feedback addresses the part you want to improve.

Otherwise, you may credit a newly learned prompt for better results when the useful change was the background information you added. Or you may blame the model for failure when you also changed the delivery requirements. Misidentify the cause, and you can end up diligently repeating an irrelevant action.

Of course, observing a change before and after an intervention does not settle causation. A different group of readers or a different time could produce different results. A small personal trial need not pretend to be a rigorous experiment. But its conclusions should stay within its evidence. One success is a thin basis for inventing a universal rule.

## Bundle the delivery. Separate some of the learning.

You might object: who has time for all these comparisons? If several changes can solve the problem together, why separate them?

When delivery is urgent, I support making them together.

Some problems require coordinated changes. A different promise in the introduction may require a different user process. Separate them too rigidly, and an individual version may not even work. Delaying a needed outcome to understand every change can be a poor trade.

There is another practical complication: readers and users change. In his [2013 essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham described close contact with early users as an important source of feedback for improving a product. A footnote also points out that users’ needs can change in response to what you build. This is an account of startup experience, not an experiment covering every industry. Even when you identify an action that helped this time, you cannot assume its conditions will remain fixed.

So I would narrow the requirement. For a task you intend to master over time and expect to encounter repeatedly, choose one important uncertainty and arrange a comparison specifically for it. Let the rest of the work continue. Your entire working day does not need to become a research project.

Buffer’s retrospective includes another plain detail. The first working version took seven weeks to build, and its first paying customer arrived within four days of launch. That timetable belongs to that startup; it is not a schedule for yours. At least the account preserves what happened at different stages, rather than compressing everything into a declaration that the product succeeded.

Recording stages still does not identify causes. We can take an additional step: after delivery, select one change you most want to understand and make a separate comparison, keeping the original. The delivered version can remain intact. The version used for learning can have a narrower scope.

The arrangement of the comparison can affect the result. If the same reader sees the original first and the revision second, the revision may benefit from a second reading: the reader already learned something about the tool from the first version. Using two different readers introduces differences in understanding and needs. Varying the order and recording those differences can help reveal problems. It does not automatically turn a personal trial into a rigorous experiment.

Write down the criterion for success before making the change, too. Choose a criterion after seeing the result, and almost any version can look like progress: less content becomes concision, more content becomes completeness. First, specify what misunderstanding you want the reader to avoid. You can revise the criterion after discovering a problem, but changing the criterion and testing the edit should be separate attempts.

Here is a small exercise I plan to try. You can try it on a low-stakes task already in front of you:

1. Save the current version and write down one judgment to test. Specify the change, the performance you hope it will affect, and what result would make you acknowledge that it did not help. “Make it better overall” is too broad.
2. Ask AI to produce a comparison version, keeping the material, requirements, and other parts as consistent as possible. Change the part you care about. Treat changes that must work together as a group, and limit your conclusion to that group rather than assigning the credit to one component.
3. Compare the versions using the performance you specified in advance. Ask a reader to perform that concrete action, or examine both versions against the same requirements. Where feasible, vary their presentation order and record changes in participants or conditions. Preserve disagreement when it appears.
4. Explain the result yourself before asking AI to identify other possible causes. Keep just one conditional conclusion: under these materials and requirements, this change appeared to help, while these other explanations remain unresolved.

The exercise takes extra time, and I do not yet have personal results from using it. Its value needs to be tested in subsequent attempts. Whether to continue should depend on whether it reduces blind revision next time, rather than how tidy your records look.

Be particularly careful about shrinking the task into something trivial merely to obtain a clear answer. If you want to improve judgment, the comparison needs to exercise judgment. Spending a long time establishing which punctuation looks nicer may do little to help you explain a product clearly. A careful comparison can still be wasted effort when it addresses the wrong question.

You can also leave an unclear result unclear. If a change helps only in combination with another change, record that condition. If readers differ widely, resist presenting an average impression as something that works for everyone. Knowing where your conclusion stops gives you information for your next action.

If you had to do the same task again tomorrow, which change would you keep, and on what grounds? If you cannot answer, today’s success is still worth enjoying. You just cannot yet be certain what you learned.

Next time, leave room to find the cause.
