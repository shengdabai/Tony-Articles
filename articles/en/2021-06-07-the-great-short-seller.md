# The Great Short Seller

*The Big Short* is based on a true story from the 2008 U.S. subprime mortgage crisis, showcasing human greed, the madness of capital markets, and how even strippers got swept up in the frenzy of a booming real estate market. It's truly a group of clueless masses—Wall Street's financial elites were no exception to the crowd's euphoria. The term "black swan" perfectly captures the outbreak of the subprime crisis. Yet, there were always people who sensed something was wrong, braving immense pressure to short the financial crisis and making a fortune in the process. While Wall Street elites indulged in their paper wealth, others were already on the front lines, conducting real estate market research and discovering that banks were even willing to lend to a dog. They not only sensed danger but also recognized a massive opportunity. As default rates rose, bond prices remained stable—how would Standard & Poor's respond? If I don't give an A, they'll just go knock on Moody's door. That's the rule of the game in our industry. A great movie, worth watching repeatedly!

The three stages of a bull market: the first stage, only a few particularly insightful individuals believe that fundamentals will improve; the second stage, most people realize that fundamentals have indeed improved; the third stage, everyone concludes that fundamentals will only get better and will always be better. The three stages of a bear market: the first stage, only a few deep-thinking investors realize that although the situation seems great and the market is generally optimistic, fundamentals won't always be smooth sailing; the second stage, most people recognize that fundamentals are getting worse; the third stage, everyone believes fundamentals will only get worse.

The smart ones act first, the foolish ones act last. In the first stage of a bull market, there's almost no reason to be optimistic, and only a few particularly insightful individuals dare to buy, with extremely low entry costs and the potential for significant future appreciation. These early adopters, by definition, are only a small group of people with both insight and courage: first, they have insight, seeing the future more accurately than others; second, they have courage, daring to buy even when the majority doesn't agree, because it's the courage to seize the unclaimed profit potential and to buy assets at an extremely low price. However, in the third stage of a bull market, many investors follow the crowd to buy in, as the market was overly optimistic, and they had to pay a high price to enter, leading to significant losses later. Every investment trend eventually goes too far and pushes prices too high, so when the bull market reaches its peak, the last person to buy in pays a price that's too high for the overestimated potential. The last person to buy in receives the punishment of capital, not the appreciation of capital.

Currently, the market is likely in the second stage of a bull market. After an initial surge, more and more people have become optimistic, fearing they might miss out on opportunities, and their attitude is gradually shifting toward risk tolerance, which is characteristic of a bull market. Based on the current overall situation, it's in the second stage, where most people believe fundamentals are improving. Newton once said, "I can calculate the movement of celestial bodies, but I cannot calculate the madness of humans." This reflects how human nature, psychology, and emotions influence investors beyond imagination. Investors find it extremely difficult to remain rational throughout the entire investment process, as their nature swings between risk tolerance and risk aversion, leading to investment outcomes that fluctuate between risk compensation and risk premium. Even the great Newton was no exception—he bought assets that had already risen a lot at the peak of the bull market and sold assets that had already fallen a lot at the bottom of the bear market, either buying at the top or selling at the bottom. This serves as a reminder that investing is not an easy task. The long history of market cycles, with inevitable ups and downs, has always existed. In the realm of human effort and progress, few fields are as dominated by history as finance, where past experience is merely a part of memory, yet it is often dismissed by the young as a refuge for old fuddy-duddies, because those old fuddy-duddies lack insight and can't appreciate the incredible miracles of the modern financial world.
