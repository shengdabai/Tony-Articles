# The Three Scorecards of a Finished Piece of Work

> Published: 2026-09-23 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-23-一份作品的三张成绩单.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-23-three-scorecards-for-a-finished-work.md)

---

Picture an ordinary evening. You give AI a difficult English interview, and minutes later it returns a smooth Chinese article. You read it, nod, and save it to your library. The next day, someone asks why the speaker reached a particular conclusion. You cannot quite explain it. The article is finished. Your understanding is still catching up.

Now picture a small tool you have made. People enjoy the demonstration. Some save the link. But when it is time to use the tool repeatedly, or spend time and money on it, the response grows quiet.

These scenes raise the same question for me: how often do we receive one impressive score and assume the whole undertaking has passed? I think a finished piece of work deserves at least three separate scorecards. Did it produce a useful result now? Can I still do the relevant thinking when the tool is gone? Did anyone else change what they actually do because of it? The answers are related, but none can stand in for the others.

## The first scorecard: what did we accomplish today?

AI can make an immediate result much better. A translation arrives faster. A layout is easier to read. A rough draft begins to look like an article. Those gains are real. Someone facing a deadline may need a usable document, rather than a full language lesson every time.

The trouble begins when we treat the quality of the artifact as proof of our own ability. A Chinese draft reads well, so we assume we understood the English interview. A web page runs, so we assume we understand its users. A report identifies mistakes in a chess game, so we assume we will avoid them next time. Each move crosses a threshold that has not actually been tested. The artifact tells us that a process produced an outcome. It does not necessarily tell us who supplied the judgment behind it.

Think of eating an excellent dish in a restaurant. Its quality can tell me that this meal was worth having; it cannot show that I know how to cook it. The reverse is true as well: knowing how to cook does not tell me whether customers would come back to this restaurant at this price. The analogy helps separate an output, a skill, and a demand. It has limits. Learning rarely follows a fixed recipe, and writing, software, and human needs keep changing. The analogy tells us which kind of question to ask; it cannot answer that question for us.

A [study of an undergraduate physics course](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6), published in 2025, is a good reason to take the first scorecard seriously. Researchers compared a carefully designed AI tutor with an active learning classroom covering the same material. In that classroom, students discussed and worked on problems instead of only listening. Students using the tutor learned more on the immediate postlesson tests while spending less time on the material. This supports a claim about immediate learning in a particular course with a particular design. It does not license claims about an ordinary chatbot in every class, or prove better long-term retention.

So I do not want to dismiss fast completion as shallow by definition. The useful question is what this task was meant to achieve. Do I need an accurate, reviewable deliverable today, or do I need to handle a similar task on my own next time? If the deliverable is the goal, the first scorecard matters enormously. If growth is part of the goal, I need a second one.

## The second scorecard: did any ability stay with me?

When I read an English interview, I can ask AI to organize the argument, explain unfamiliar context, and point out a leap in reasoning. The issue is not how much it helped. The issue is whether, after it has helped, I can explain the argument and its limits without it. Saving fluent Chinese is easy. Closing the page and stating the speaker's premise, conclusion, and a possible counterexample in my own words is much more revealing.

This is not a ritual designed to make learning needlessly hard. An ability we value should usually travel to a new setting. Today's source may be a podcast; tomorrow's may be an unfamiliar report. Today someone else has arranged the reasoning; tomorrow I may have to decide what deserves another question. If I perform well only while the original prompt, original task, and original tool are present, I would call that a successful collaboration. I would hesitate to call it my own understanding. Applying knowledge elsewhere simply means that when the question and the hints change, I can still recognize the relationship that matters.

The [authors' abstract of an experimental paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721), revised in 2026, reports a warning from trials that assigned participants to different conditions. Across 1,222 participants and tasks including mathematical reasoning and reading comprehension, AI help improved immediate performance, but participants subsequently performed worse without AI and were more likely to give up. The reported difference appeared after brief interactions. I am relying here on the authors' abstract; this was not a long-term follow-up, and it cannot establish that all routine AI use weakens people. It identifies a risk worth checking for ourselves: an answer that arrives easily may conceal patience and methods we have yet to develop.

The physics study offers a serious counterargument. Its tutor asked students to respond, let them work at their own pace, and gave timely feedback. The researchers also structured multipart problems and supplied prepared solutions to reduce the risk of inaccurate explanations. The result is a reminder that struggling alone is not the only path to learning. A tool that makes me answer, shows me where I misunderstood, and then lets me try a new problem independently may serve me better than getting stuck by myself.

The two studies do not cancel each other out. One measured immediate outcomes from a designed tutor in one course. The other observed independent performance and persistence after AI assistance across several short tasks. The questions, tools, time spans, and measures differ. My takeaway is not a universal slogan about whether to use AI. It is a practical distinction: doing well with help and doing well once the help is removed are different things to measure.

My simplest check is to close the answer for a moment. After reading a passage, I write down what problem it addresses, why its conclusion follows, and a situation in which that conclusion might fail. If I cannot do that, I return to the evidence and the missing connection. If I can, I ask AI to challenge my account. The tool supplies explanations, feedback, and objections. I must supply the final reconstruction. This is not necessary for every rushed delivery. When the purpose is to learn, it turns "I think I got it" into something I can test.

## The third scorecard: did anyone else need it?

The second scorecard still measures only what happened to me. Once I intend a piece of work for someone else, another question appears. My ability to explain or build it does not mean that another person will make room for it in their life. A chess analysis tool might identify errors accurately and never be opened for a second game. A learning note might be beautifully written and change none of its readers' decisions. More polishing may not address the real problem. I need to learn what the other person was already doing and which step my work changes for them.

Public accounts of early products offer a plain reminder. In his 2013 [essay on doing things that do not scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes Stripe's founders setting up the product on a willing user's laptop right away. He also describes Airbnb's early efforts to visit hosts and improve their listings. These are practitioner accounts and examples, not controlled proof that personal service always works. They matter because the makers had to face actual points of friction, rather than a user they had imagined.

Buffer founder Joel Gascoigne separates signals even more clearly in his [2011 firsthand account](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/). He began with a small page to see whether people would leave an email address. He then inserted a pricing page to see whether anyone would keep clicking. Only after a working version launched did the first paying customer appear. An email suggested interest. Continuing past the price page suggested willingness to explore further. Payment was a different commitment again. None of those steps proved that a large market would exist; even one payment could not guarantee lasting use. Each step, however, told him more than his own belief that people ought to want the product.

That changes how I read feedback. Praise, a bookmark, a signup, a trial, repeat use, and payment ask different things of a person and answer different questions. Someone may say they like an idea out of politeness. Spending time to complete an inconvenient task is a stronger signal. Returning later and making the work part of an existing routine is closer to evidence of continuing value. Payment is not the only valid measure. A public-interest tool, a learning project, and a favor to a friend each have different aims. The point is to name the thing I am trying to establish, then look for an action that could actually establish it.

A lack of use does not immediately prove a lack of value, either. The entrance might be hard to find, the explanation confusing, the timing wrong, or the intended user unable to get permission. Conversely, a brief burst of attention does not prove stable demand. The third scorecard needs context. In particular, I want to know where the path breaks between "that looks good" and "I used it again."

## When the scorecards disagree

The hardest case is often not three bad scores. It is one very good score. AI returns polished prose in minutes, so the immediate result looks excellent; the writer still cannot explain its central argument. Someone studies carefully and builds a tool independently; users keep using their old, clumsy method. Or a work attracts users for a while, but its maker must keep patching it manually, without learning a repeatable way to maintain it. One or two visible successes can hide the missing scorecard.

It is tempting to say simply that something "worked." I would rather ask which part worked. If we fail to make that distinction, today's smooth delivery may hide the ability we will need tomorrow. This is especially easy for creators, because success is easier to display than its limits. Screenshots, seamless demos, and attractive numbers travel well. The ability to explain an argument without AI, or to see real users return over time, is often less photogenic. That less visible part tells us what to practice next.

There is a strong objection here: if the final work is useful, why should anyone care whether its maker can reproduce it independently? In many situations, I think the objection is right. A translation needed in a hurry, a one-off cleanup, or a clearly specified repetitive job can be handled mostly by a reliable tool. We need not slow every piece of production just to prove that we could do it ourselves. If the result is checked and someone remains responsible for it, the first scorecard may be the main standard for this job.

But the objection is not enough when the aim is to develop judgment, or when the work must survive in a changing environment. Today's AI answer may be sound. Tomorrow the source changes, a reader objects, or the tool makes a mistake. Someone still has to notice and decide what follows. In the same way, someone opening a page today does not tell us whether it will help next month. The second and third scorecards check two different futures: whether I can handle a new problem, and whether the work can keep helping real people.

I use the three scorecards as three places to measure, not as a demand for perfect marks on every tiny task. For a learning task, independent explanation and use in a new setting matter most. For a delivery task, accuracy and reviewability come first. Work meant for others also calls for observation of actual use. Goals can change the order and the standard. What cannot change is the logic: an easy signal cannot certify a different event that has yet to happen.

## A small experiment you can do today

Choose one thing you have already finished today and care about: a passage you understood with AI, a description you wrote, or a small tool you plan to share. Write its purpose in one sentence. If the purpose is learning, put away the source and the AI response, explain the central claim on a blank page, and reread the place where your explanation breaks. If the purpose is delivery, have someone check the result against an explicit standard and note what needs reworking. If the purpose is to help someone else, ask a suitable person to try it in a real situation. Watch where they continue and where they leave.

You do not have to do every step or invent an elaborate scoring system. Before the next round of work, ask: which scorecard am I looking at now? What action would give me evidence for the one that is missing? Once the question is specific, the tool has a clearer job. It might explain, produce, check, or remind me to go and watch how someone actually uses the work.

I return to that smooth Chinese article from the opening. I would still ask AI to help organize a difficult interview, and I would welcome the time it saves. After I press save, though, I want to close the page and explain the argument. Before I share it, I want to know what a reader could do with it. The finished artifact is on the table. Learning and value still have their own tests to pass.
