# When Judgment Outruns Skill

> 发布日期:2026-09-16 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-16-当判断力跑在能力前面，你会误以为自己退步了.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-16-when-judgment-outruns-skill.md)

---

Today I reread a learning note and stopped at a small, frustrating detail.

A learner had practiced pronunciation almost every day for months. Earlier, scores near 100 appeared often. Then something changed. Reaching 95 became difficult. Sometimes the score fell to 92. One morning, a single word consumed an hour and still refused to sound right.

The obvious conclusion was: I am getting worse.

But there is another possibility. The mouth may not have regressed. The ear may simply have improved first.

Once you hear a distinction that used to be invisible, the same performance no longer feels acceptable. Yesterday's “good enough” becomes today's detectable error. Nothing outside has changed. The person doing the judging has.

This is one of the strangest moments in serious learning: progress can first arrive as disappointment.

## One score can hide two learning curves

We often talk about “ability” as if it were a single number. It is not.

At least two abilities are developing whenever we learn a craft.

The first is production: Can I write the paragraph, pronounce the word, build the feature, or solve the problem?

The second is discrimination: Can I tell a strong result from a merely plausible one? Can I locate the error? Can I explain why it is wrong and what a better version would require?

These two curves do not rise at the same speed.

At the beginning, production often grows faster. A few techniques create a visible jump. The learner feels powerful because the output looks dramatically better than yesterday's.

Later, discrimination catches up. The learner starts seeing weak evidence, hidden assumptions, false positives, awkward rhythm, brittle code, and solutions that work only in the example. The work has not necessarily become worse. The inspection has become sharper.

When discrimination rises faster than production, confidence falls. That fall can feel like regression even though it is the beginning of expertise.

Learning science gives us a useful warning here. Research on “desirable difficulties” distinguishes performance during practice from durable learning. Conditions that make practice look smooth can produce weak retention and transfer; conditions that slow visible progress can sometimes produce stronger learning later. In other words, today's score is not a reliable verdict on what will remain tomorrow. ([Research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6353106/))

The word *sometimes* matters. A falling score is not automatically proof of growth. We can also decline because we stopped practicing, changed the task, received noisy feedback, or formed a bad habit. “My standards improved” can become a flattering excuse.

So the real question is not whether the score fell. It is whether our ability to diagnose the fall became more precise.

## AI can raise performance while hiding learning

AI makes this distinction urgent because it can lift the production curve almost instantly.

A rough email becomes polished. A blank file becomes working code. A confused thought becomes a neat outline. The task score goes up before the person has had time to build the judgment that normally grows through repeated attempts.

That is genuine value. I do not think we should deliberately return to slower tools just to preserve a romantic image of struggle.

But a danger appears when we use the improved artifact as evidence that *we* have improved by the same amount.

Researchers at Aalto University examined this gap in logical-reasoning tasks. In one study, 246 participants used AI to solve 20 LSAT problems. Their performance was three points higher than a norm population, yet they overestimated their own performance by four points. A second study with 452 participants replicated the broader pattern. The study is specific to these tasks and does not prove that AI makes everyone less self-aware, but it shows that better output and better self-knowledge can move separately. ([Aalto University publication](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/3b3fcbba-72ed-476f-be59-6ec892b9dd96))

This is why “I finished faster” is an incomplete measure of AI-assisted learning.

If AI handles the production while I lose the ability to predict failure, spot a weak answer, or transfer the method to a new situation, my performance improved but my learning system did not.

The opposite is also possible. I may become more demanding after working closely with AI. I notice that a fluent paragraph has no evidence. I see that a feature passes the demo but fails at the boundary. I hear that a sentence is grammatical but not natural. My satisfaction drops because my diagnostic resolution rises.

That is not a reason to use less AI. It is a reason to measure a second curve.

## Keep two ledgers, not one

I now think every serious AI-assisted practice needs two ledgers.

The first is the completion ledger. It records what the tool helped us finish: the article shipped, the bug fixed, the concept explained, the workflow shortened.

The second is the judgment ledger. It records what we learned to see: the unsupported claim we caught, the edge case we predicted, the misleading metric we rejected, the reason one version works better than another.

You can start today with a small three-step routine.

Before asking AI, write a short prediction. What should a good answer contain? Where is it most likely to fail? Two or three lines are enough. The point is to make your current judgment visible before the machine improves the result.

After AI responds, do not only save the answer. Save an error map. Mark what you can independently verify, what remains uncertain, what the first draft missed, and why you changed it. The goal is not to collect more criticism. It is to make discrimination trainable.

Finally, run a small transfer test. Once a week, take one similar task and work for ten minutes without AI. You do not need to finish it. Try to outline the approach, predict the likely mistakes, or explain the key decision. Then bring AI back and compare.

The three records answer different questions:

- Did the work get done?
- Did I become better at seeing what could go wrong?
- Can I carry any of that judgment into the next task?

This is not merely a personal theory. A randomized study of educational chatbots found that students who received metacognitive feedback—prompts that made them assess and regulate their own thinking—showed better transfer and greater metacognitive sensitivity than groups receiving affective or neutral feedback. The interaction design changed what students practiced: not only answering, but monitoring how they answered. ([Study in *npj Science of Learning*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00311-8))

The same idea applies outside a classroom. Ask AI to challenge your confidence before revealing its solution. Ask it to withhold the final code until you predict the failure mode. Ask it to compare your explanation with the evidence, not merely replace your explanation with a cleaner one.

The tool should not only move the work forward. It should leave the worker with a finer instrument for seeing.

## Disappointment needs receipts

There is a comforting version of this essay that says: whenever you feel worse, celebrate, because you must be improving.

I do not believe that.

Some difficulty is useful. Some is merely confusion. Some lower scores reveal a sharper standard. Others reveal actual decline. We need evidence to tell them apart.

If your judgment really is improving, it should leave receipts. Your criticism becomes more specific. Your predictions fail less often. You can distinguish uncertainty from error. You transfer a principle to a new case. You need fewer random retries, even if you demand more revisions.

If none of these things changes, the falling score is probably not hidden progress. It may be time to change the method, seek better feedback, or return to the basics.

AI will keep making finished work easier to produce. That is precisely why self-knowledge becomes more valuable. When a machine can raise the visible score in seconds, we need another way to know whether the person behind the score is growing.

Sometimes progress feels good because our hands finally catch up.

Sometimes it feels terrible because our judgment has run ahead.

Do not confuse that temporary distance with failure. Measure it. Train inside it. Then let your hands catch up to what your eyes have learned to see.
