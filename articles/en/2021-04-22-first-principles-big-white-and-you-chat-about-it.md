The "first principles" in every industry and every field of knowledge are different. Our actions and thinking are often swayed by temporary events and situations, leading to a tendency to forget the first principles. Whether we call it "original intention," "original purpose," or "first principles," what we're emphasizing is the awareness of first principles.

*Waiting for Godot* is a famous absurdist modern play. On stage, two people say some boring things and do some boring things from beginning to end. But every time a paragraph is about to end, one of them says, "What are we doing?" The other replies, "We are waiting for Godot." "Who is Godot?" "We don't know." Then they start talking and doing boring things again, repeating the same dialogue until the next segment.

At first glance, *Waiting for Godot* seems boring, but by the end, it's very moving: it's exactly our survival state, a description of our behavior and thinking. From a local perspective, doing something may seem meaningful, like you hit me, I hit you, you squeeze me, I squeeze you... competing for superiority. But when someone asks, "What are we doing?" you say, "We are waiting for Godot"—a pre-set answer, but no one knows who Godot is. Godot is said to be a variation of "God" in French, but whether it's a person, a god, or a train, no one knows. *Waiting for Godot* vividly portrays the survival state of modern people: forgetting the first principles and priorities, being busy but without meaning.

Are we really making mistakes based on the first principles? For example, my mistake is based on a principle or assumption I believe in. I tried doing this thing, and even though the facts proved I was wrong, it was meaningful because it proved that something was wrong. On the other hand, you might commit a mistake without even knowing the purpose—when we suffer a loss, we say, "This is paying the price," "This is a cost," but you need to figure out *why* you're paying the price or making the payment. Otherwise, you'll always be paying the price, and the mistake is meaningless. Is the mistake worth it? Are you paying for *what*? Are you paying the price for *who*? When you understand *why* you're paying and *for whom*, you need to look at that *what*—entering the second level of thinking. Through thinking, you'll gradually realize: it's not worth it, and you can end this mistake. A "correct mistake" is one where you know why you're wrong, and it gives you a warning mechanism and valuable data; a "wrong mistake" doesn't provide substantive data.

Elon Musk proposed that rockets shouldn't be so expensive; their price should be reduced tenfold. Musk's tenfold thinking comes from first principles. What does that mean? If you're making small improvements, like a technology that can reduce costs by 18%, we're not interested. We want to do something that can reduce costs by tenfold.

> "Six methods of tenfold thinking": try to avoid outsourcing, and produce 80% of the components yourself. Procurement should be more flexible. Connect with the big market. This might be a key point. Recycle and reuse as much as possible. Don't rely only on specialized aerospace talent; also look for people in the broader market. "Occam's Razor"—simplify the manufacturing process as much as possible.

First principles are most suitable for places like NASA, where things are deeply entrenched, the market is not competitive, and the system is far from equilibrium. Take Tesla's newly released truck product as an example. Jerome Guillen, Tesla's global sales and service vice president, explained the logic behind Tesla's truck manufacturing: "We just thought: what do people want? They want reliability. They want low cost. They want driving comfort. So we rethought everything. What is a truck really?"

Neil Strauss explained this way of thinking in his own words: "In other words, if you want to create or innovate, start from a blank page. Don't just accept any idea, convention, or standard just because everyone else is doing it. For example, if you want to make a truck, it must be able to reliably transport goods from one place to another. It must follow only the laws of physics; everything else is negotiable, even government regulations. Just remember, the goal is not to reinvent the truck, but to create the best truck, regardless of whether it looks like the original one or not."

Neil Strauss said: "This way of thinking allows Elon Musk to view the industry more objectively than those who have spent a lifetime in a particular field." Indeed, Elon Musk has achieved remarkable results in both the automotive and rocket industries using this way of thinking.

**Tips for Establishing First Principles**

Socratic questioning: Socratic questioning can be used to establish primary principles through rigorous analysis. This is a disciplined questioning process used to establish truth, reveal underlying assumptions, and distinguish knowledge from ignorance. The main difference between Socratic questioning and regular discussion is that the former aims to systematically identify the first principle.

> Socratic questioning typically follows this process:
>
> Clarify your thoughts and explain the origin of your thoughts (Why do I think this way? What exactly am I thinking?)
>
> Challenge assumptions (How do I know this is true? What if I believe the opposite?)
>
> Find evidence (How can I back this up? What is the source?)
>
> Consider other perspectives (What would others think? How do I know I'm right?)
>
> Examine consequences and implications (What if I'm wrong? What are the consequences if I'm wrong?)
>
> Question the original question (Why do I think this way? Am I right? What conclusion can I draw from the reasoning process?)
>
> This process prevents you from relying on your intuition and limits strong emotional reactions. This process can help you build something lasting.

Children instinctively think in first principles, just like we do. They want to understand what's happening in the world. To do this, they intuitively break through the fog by playing games that parents often find annoying.

"Why?"

"Why?"

"Why?"

This is a very interesting example:

"Now it's time to brush your teeth and go to bed."

"Why?"

"Because we need to take care of our bodies, which means we need sleep."

"Why do we need to sleep?"

"Because if we never sleep, we will die."

"Why does it make us die?"

"I don't know; let's look it up."

Children are simply trying to understand why adults say certain things or why they tell them to do certain things.

Your child's first time playing this game is cute, but for most teachers and parents, it eventually becomes annoying. Then the answer becomes something my mother used to tell me: "Because I say so!" (Mom, I love you.)

Of course, we don't always have this patience for our children. For example, when we're late for school, or we've traveled for 12 hours, or we're trying to spend our own time, I often feel tired. But I still try not to say, "Because I say so."

People dislike the response "Because I say so" for two reasons, which are also common in the corporate world. The first reason is that we feel it slows us down. We know what our goal is, and this response creates unnecessary resistance. The second reason is that after one or two questions, we often get lost. We actually don't know why. Faced with our own ignorance, we resort to self-defense.

I remember asking people in meetings why we were doing something or why they thought something was right. Initially, this approach had some tolerance. But after three "why" questions, you usually find yourself on the other side of the "we can use this offline" version.

First principles (English: First principle), a philosophical and logical term, is the most basic proposition or assumption that cannot be omitted, deleted, or violated. First principles are equivalent to axioms in mathematics. The concept was first proposed by Aristotle.
