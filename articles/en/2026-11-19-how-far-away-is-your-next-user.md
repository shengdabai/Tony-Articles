# How Far Away Is Your Next User?

> 发布日期:2026-11-19 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-19-下一位用户有多远.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-19-how-far-away-is-your-next-user.md)

---

Is the department next door the easiest place to find your next group of users? I'd look first at whether they run into the same problem and would deal with it in the same way. Two teams a floor apart can work very differently. Two small businesses that have never heard of each other might spend part of every day wrestling with the same thing.

Here's how I think about that distance. In the hands of the next group, can the part the first users found useful do its job again? It's gratifying when the first few people take to a little tool you've built. The tricky part comes when you decide whom to approach next. Familiar names and requests that sound alike can get bundled up with whatever actually made the tool useful. Pull those apart and the next step gets clearer.

## What's different next door?

It's how the work gets done. A summary in today's notes compares two ways of getting a tool into use. It comes from a clipped interview. Some tools need an engineer to set them up alongside the customer. Others can be used as soon as you open them. That got me thinking. Even with the very same tool, if two groups need different kinds of help, we owe each group an explanation of why it would be worth using.

Suppose you've built an AI support assistant for small online shops. A customer asks where a package is. The assistant looks up the order and drafts a short reply for a support agent to check and send. At the first shop where the tool works well, staff get a lot of shipping questions every day and keep switching between the chat window and the order system. They come back to the assistant because skipping a lookup gets an answer to the person waiting in front of them sooner.

The shop's returns team replies to customers too, and they'd also like to save time. With refund disputes, though, the hardest step is different. First you have to establish what the platform's rules say and hear both sides. Then you have to find the person who can decide how much to refund. The assistant can write a smooth reply and the agent will still be waiting. If all you hear is that both teams want to type less, it's easy to decide drafting is the benefit they share. Meanwhile, another little shop may use different chat software and still be fed up with looking up packages. If the assistant can reach its orders, the two shops could use the tool in much the same way.

Think about taking the bus. Two places can look close together on a map. One might be on your route, while the other takes a transfer. The route here is the set of steps between a customer's question and the agent being able to move things forward. Switching chat windows is like changing stops. The question is whether you can still get to the order. A refund that needs someone else's decision adds a transfer. Bus routes are printed on maps. At work, you often have to see a case get stuck to find out where the transfer is.

One published study makes the difference visible. The researchers studied a chat assistant rolled out in stages, mainly from fall 2020 to early 2021, using data from 5,172 support agents at a software company and its contractors. Issues resolved per hour increased by about 15% on average, with larger gains for less experienced agents. That measure was available only for the subset with the necessary resolution data. The tool offered suggestions and links to internal documentation, and agents could decide whether to use them. Even in similar jobs, the same assistant could help people to different degrees. [*Generative AI at Work*](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.11771v2)

I'd put that distinction in the way I describe the tool. Say agents can reply faster and people will naturally picture the messages they find hardest to answer. The messages I can make a clear case for are ones where the order is there and looking it up is a pain. That pitch might attract fewer people, but it makes it easier for someone to tell me which kind of message they deal with day after day. I can find out what they most want to solve before we sit down for a demo.

## What made it go smoothly?

Look at who did the work. In 2013, Paul Graham described a practice from Stripe's early days. When someone agreed to try it, the two founders set them up on the spot. He also wrote that merchant accounts seemed to open very quickly at first because the founders manually applied for traditional merchant accounts for users behind the scenes. These are early examples recounted by an investor. The essay gives no comparison of how much growth each way of setting up an account generated. [*Do Things that Don't Scale*](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html)

I find those two details especially interesting together. Helping someone set up lets you see whether they're willing to try the tool. Opening an account for them lets them get something out of it quickly. When you move on to the next group, it helps to know how much of that experience came from the work you just did. Someone can say yes because the job is genuinely a pain. It can also matter that you're right there to take care of the headaches at the start. Both can be true.

Back to the shop. While setting up the assistant, you might have quietly matched up two order spreadsheets. After that, whenever a customer asks about a package, the assistant can find the tracking number quickly. The time saved is real. The next shop has its own way of numbering orders, and some orders never make it into the spreadsheet. You thought you'd only have to switch chat apps. Now you have to sit down with the shop and figure out which sheet they trust. The detail worth keeping is that small job you did the first time around. Otherwise your trial starts with material that's already been cleaned up, and nobody can say how the customer is supposed to get it into that state.

That help is worth giving. Matching the orders yourself once may tell you more than ten conversations about whether they're easy to match. I'd record the time it took and whether I'd have to spend it once at setup or every few days. A one-off task could become a step a user follows when getting started. Recurring work belongs in the day-to-day costs. If the assistant saves one agent a step on every case and another colleague has to babysit a spreadsheet every day, the shop has to decide whether the whole new arrangement is worth it.

I'd also look at it the other way round. Sometimes setup is the same for both groups and the difference is what gets in the way of continued use. In a 2018 account, Superhuman founder Rahul Vohra explained how his team first looked for recent users and learned from those who would most miss the product that they valued speed, focus and keyboard shortcuts. The team then looked at users who also cared about speed and felt less attached to the product. Vohra reported the lack of a mobile app as one thing that held them back. The case shows how the team investigated obstacles among existing users. The survey left out people who hadn't started using the product. [*How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit*](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)

There's an easy detail to miss here. People can tell you they care about the same thing, but you still need to see when and where it helps. Someone who stays at a desk and someone who's often away from one can both genuinely like getting through email faster. For one, a mobile app is a nice addition. For the other, it might determine which tool they actually use on Tuesday. So when the next group says they like speed too, I'd ask what they'd most like to do faster and when. The answer tells me whether I need to give them another way in or whether I'm getting into a different business.

## Does urgent demand mean I can expand?

I'd treat those as separate questions. In 2011, Paul Graham published selected correspondence with investor Fred Wilson about Airbnb. In a 2009 email, Graham relayed what the founders had noticed after coming back from New York. Quite a few users genuinely needed their rental income to pay rent. Wilson had met the team and still wondered how big the business could get. He thought it could reach the bed-and-breakfast market and had doubts about whether it could take on hotels. The email exchange records one investment discussion. It leaves the eventual market size unanswered. [*Subject: Airbnb*](https://www.paulgraham.com/airbnb.html)

That difference of opinion is worth keeping in view. Helping pay the rent tells you the income mattered to those hosts. For people further afield, it would also matter how they already handled guests, what extra work they were willing to take on, and which parts of the job somebody else already handled. There's still work to do between finding people willing to make an effort for something and finding more people willing to make an effort for the same thing. The more precisely you can say what drew the first group in, the easier it is to ask the next group a question that gets somewhere.

Sometimes the smallest step is to change where you go and carry on as before. Other times, expanding would mean changing who you work with, how they do the job, and how you deliver the tool. You can change all of that. If it goes well, though, it can be hard to tell which change mattered. If it falls flat, the problem might be a single missing prerequisite at setup. When I have a choice, I'd hold a few things steady and change the one I most want to understand. Where two shops look up orders in the same way, switching chat software gives me a result I can read more easily than handing the whole trial to the returns team.

Someone might say that if you keep looking for people who work the old way, you'll end up with a little niche. You very well might. Once the shop is using the support assistant, somebody might have a new idea. What if the returns lead could review the conversations each day to see which product customers most often misunderstand? That might turn out to be more valuable than checking shipments. It also presents a different set of questions. Can you reliably spot a misunderstanding in a conversation? What can the lead change after seeing the results? After using it once, will they have a reason to look again next week?

So I'm willing to look further away, especially if the current group is too small to sustain the product or if people keep finding a new use and are willing to spend time testing it. I'd give the attempt an accurate name up front. Taking an existing benefit to more people is expansion. Using an existing tool to find a different benefit is exploration. There's a practical reason for keeping track. If the new use falls flat, I can go back to checking shipments and look at the shops it already served. If the new use works, I still have to learn how to deliver results for a different job.

## How can I measure the distance today?

Compare two potential users. Even if only one person has used your tool so far, you can try this. Find someone who looks close at hand and someone whose work may be more like your current user's. Put the demo away and ask each of them to tell you about the last time they did the job. Ask until you can write down what happened. Stick to things they've already done, so you don't mistake something that's easy in your demo for something they struggle with in real life.

1. Pick a specific instance from someone who already uses the tool. Write down what happened, which step the tool made easier, and what it needed to do that. At the shop, the trigger was a customer chasing up a package. The direct benefit was one fewer trip to the order system. The condition was that the assistant could retrieve the correct tracking number. If you helped organize the orders, write that down too.
2. Take the same three questions to each potential user and ask about that one occasion. Note what they open first, where they have to wait, and how the case ends. Two people waiting for shipping information could be waiting for very different things. One has to look it up in a system. The other needs the carrier to update it. The first might be able to skip a step. The second still has to wait for news from outside. It's enough to have them point to a recent instance. You can use an example with no customer information to confirm how the work gets done.
3. Pick one difference for a small trial. If you want to know whether different chat software gets in the way, look for similar work and similar order data. Agree in advance on something you can see happen. When a customer asks where a package is, for instance, the agent can retrieve the right tracking number from their usual chat window and reply. Record any step that still depends on you. If you matched up the spreadsheets again to get a smooth demo, that note will tell you more about what to investigate next than the smooth demo itself. Pick an order of the kind the shop actually sees and one the assistant can't find. In the second case, telling the agent what's missing is also an observable result. At least it keeps a guessed tracking number out of the reply.

All this trial can do is help you decide what to try next. If the workflow holds together and the benefit shows up, find someone else who works that way and check again. If the benefit is clear and somebody has to fill in missing information every time, see whether that extra work is worth the cost. If what really takes up the customer's time is a different job, give that use its own trial. One perfectly ordinary result is that you can't find two people whose differences you can tease apart. Write down what changed together and wait for the next real opportunity. A single success can't tell you which of several changes deserves the credit.

Write down the next user you most want to win over. Will the step that gave your last user a reason to keep coming back still happen in this person's hands?
