"Physical fitness is the vehicle for carrying knowledge and the dwelling for morality... without a sound body, there can be no morality or intellect." Good physical condition is the foundation that supports intellect and the practice of morality. Without a good body, everything loses its carrier.

How to bring up children through physical activity, to refine their minds and strengthen their bodies, has always been a topic of concern for many parents in the community.

This episode's tea-hall discussion shares the story of a mother who raised three children of different ages, cultivating exercise habits...

#

**01**

Good afternoon, thank you, Susan teacher, for inviting me to share on the topic of bringing children into physical exercise.

First, let me introduce myself. I'm Hai Xiang, based in Xinxiang, Henan, and a mother of three children.

Currently, I work as an accountant. My three children are 17, 10, and 8 years old this year.

I learned about Li Xiaolai's courses last year, and after that, I joined the Good Family Education Community. Learning from the teacher's courses has been truly worth it, and the impact on our small family can be described as "huge."

The Good Family Education course trilogy has given me direction in many aspects, established the concept of lifelong learning and development, and provided a long-term perspective. **Especially, it has helped me no longer feel anxious or pressured about my children's education. Everything is moving in a positive and upward direction.**

Currently, I'm practicing more in the areas of physical health and marital relationships. So, I especially admire the friends in the "Dedicated Investment in Life" class, who can often listen to Li Xiaolai's lessons. I'm also grateful that I happened to join the "Good Family Education" program.

Today, I will share in three parts.

Li Xiaolai mentioned in the course that according to a survey, less than 20% of parents truly value their children's physical training. The reason is that most parents believe intellectual ability is more valuable, and those who are physically strong are just simple-minded.

Frankly speaking, I used to be one of the majority of the 80% of parents. **For children's education, there was full anxiety, with no time for learning, let alone time for exercise?** This is a shallow understanding that leads to wrong decisions.

In fact, children are seriously lacking in physical exercise. Teachers at school worry about accidents, so during breaks, they encourage children to sit and study rather than go out and move around. Physical education classes are often replaced by cultural subjects.

Both of my children's classes have homework and exercise check-in, and the parents in the homework check-in group are very active, while the exercise check-in group is very small. There are many overweight and nearsighted children in school.

The correct concept should be — **physical ability affects intellectual ability**:

1. **Attention concentration and sustained focus require physical support.** People who achieve great things all have their own exercise habits.

2. Physical ability development and its limit are completed before the age of 20, which **supports the brain's lifelong learning and growth**; therefore, it's important to develop the habit early.

3. **Any form of exercise is a way to charge the body, extend its energy, and empower it.** It's not a drain on the body. So, it's worth setting aside specific time for exercise.

My initial reason for joining the running camp was to influence my eldest daughter. She is 17 now, and since middle school, she has become less active. On weekends or holidays, she stays in her room and doesn't go out.

At that time, she would catch a cold several times a year. What really upset me was that she stayed up late at night and slept in in the morning, rarely eating breakfast. **She lacked the vitality, energy, and spirit that should belong to her age.**

To get her moving, I tried all sorts of methods — emotional appeals, rational explanations, cash rewards, forced requirements, and even threats and persuasion. None of them worked.

Later, I realized that change must come from within. I thought **the only way was to do it myself and influence her.** Previously, I was just talking about it, but this time, I needed to take it seriously. **I had to focus on doing it.**

I like running, but I used to run randomly. To **run long-term without injury**, it's necessary to systematically learn about running.

So, in June last year, I signed up for a running interest camp. After that, I ran my first 10 km, 15 km, and half marathon. Currently, I'm studying for a full marathon and have developed the habit of going to bed and waking up early. This year, my goal is to participate in a marathon.

How can I influence them? I have to let them see.

During this time, I specifically asked them to film my running actions. Scientific running posture needs practice, and I would also specially report to them three about my running progress. I would tell them how far or how long I ran that day. For example, running 10 km is the distance from our hometown to school; a half marathon is the distance from school to school, which takes about two hours.

They would think, "Oh, that's how far it is."

There were two times when I ran a half marathon, and I specifically invited my eldest daughter to ride a bike with me. When I won the highest achievement award, I told the children that they had contributed half of the effort. So they were as happy as I was, taking photos with the certificates one by one.

"Mom is so serious about learning to run, I also want to be like Mom..."

My eldest daughter lives in a boarding school and isn't around me most of the time, so I train her on weekends.

She used to like playing badminton, and her father bought a new racket for her to play with. But it was difficult to wake her up in the morning on weekends, and once a month was already great. This experience made me realize, **if children don't develop good habits when they're young, it's hard to change them later.**

So, I need to start early with the second and third child, helping them develop good habits from a young age.

The teacher mentioned that **habits are the most direct and smooth connections between neurons. The earlier you form them, the better, and early on, you need to provide support and guidance.** Moreover, **habits should be built one at a time, not multiple at once. Therefore, it's crucial to determine which is more important and which is the most important.**

I chose exercise. Looking back, this choice was right. I want to help my children develop the habit of exercise by doing the same thing at a fixed time.

Initially, I wanted to run with my children, but it wasn't practical in terms of timing, and children of this age aren't suitable for long-distance endurance running. At the same time, the school had a requirement for students to jump rope and kick a shuttlecock, so I decided to start with these two activities.

Every day at a fixed time, the first thing I do after coming home from work is to accompany them in exercise, without fail. The second child is the older sister, and she didn't find it too hard, but she needed to keep it up every day. The third child is the younger brother, and he didn't know how to jump rope or kick a shuttlecock at first.

When the younger brother first started learning to jump rope, his arms and legs didn't coordinate well, and he clumsily moved the rope from behind his head, over his head, and then in front, with his feet jumping over it, then the rope again, then the jump again, and so on, one by one, one by one, and he would cry loudly several times...

Kicking a shuttlecock was also difficult. His feet couldn't catch the shuttlecock he threw up. He couldn't aim his feet correctly, sometimes it would go left, sometimes right, sometimes high, sometimes low, as if the shuttlecock was thrown by someone else. Sometimes he couldn't catch it even ten times, and he would throw the shuttlecock in frustration.

Later, we came up with a method: I held the shuttlecock still, and it would fall vertically onto his feet, one by one.

Now, looking back, those days of practicing together feel so far away.

During this time, I also tried many different methods, such as sticking to 21 days and rewarding each child with a watch, and they actually did it and got the watches they wanted. Sticking to 100 days, we bought LEGO, and we finally got it. Through a mix of encouragement and persuasion, they gradually kicked more and more.

Now, our daily routine is: jump rope 300 times a day and record a one-minute video; kick a shuttlecock 200 times and record a one-minute video. These two activities usually take about half an hour to complete. Their current performance is about jumping rope over 160 times per minute, and the best can reach over 180; for the shuttlecock, they can kick over 65, and the best can reach over 80.

As they kicked more, they also started to like these two activities, and they would occasionally pick up the shuttlecock and kick a few. If I come home too late on a day, they would remind me: "Mom, you haven't jumped rope or kicked a shuttlecock today."

**They went from being resistant to not opposing, and now they are actively participating.** This also verified a lesson the teacher mentioned — **interest isn't because of liking something, but because you do it well and then you become interested.** I completely agree.

Because of these two activities, the children's physical abilities are relatively strong in their class, and their self-confidence has increased a lot, and they have also grown taller.

Finally, I'd like to share the small results of our family's exercise after one year.

Last month on Mother's Day, May 14th. Early in the morning, I went downstairs to run as usual, and my eldest daughter stopped me and said, "Mom, please wait a moment, I want to run with you."

Wow, hearing those words, I was so excited. **Finally, I got my child to run with me.** Previously, I couldn't get them to move, but now they are actively joining me. **This is the best Mother's Day gift I've ever received.**

****

As for the second and third child, they learned to kick a shuttlecock and jump rope through repeated practice, gaining some sense of achievement. **They began to understand the importance of the word 'repetition' and realized that learning is about repetition.** This is exactly what Li Xiaolai said — **mastering one thing can be replicated to another, and through this understanding and experience, it helps children reduce their fear of difficulty in learning.**

Finally, I'm very grateful for joining the Good Family Education Community, which gave my family a clear direction. I'm also very grateful for joining the Running Interest Camp, as running helped me become a better version of myself, and exercise helped the children develop good character, patience, and resilience.

It's only been a year, and I still have a long way to go. I need to continue learning and improving. But I believe it will only get better. My sharing is here, thank you all.
