*Harvard Business Review*
*Three Ways to Prepare for the Future of Work*

By Jennifer Howard-Grenville and Laura Empson
September 28, 2023

**Summary:**

Today, many forces are converging in unprecedented ways, reshaping the nature of work. To understand and adapt to these forces, leaders need to start seriously considering three important areas of focus: the future of the worker (they are questioning the role work plays in their lives and are placing more demands on their organizations); the future of work (work is rapidly evolving due to hybrid and flexible arrangements, digitization, and automation); and the future of work itself (it is evolving in response to macro-level forces and social changes). The authors refer to these three areas as the *who*, *what*, and *why* of work. Each presents its own unique challenges. Leaders need to confront and address these challenges, and the authors offer suggestions on how to do so most effectively.

In recent years, we have seen political, economic, social, and environmental crises. Many things we once took for granted about the world of work have been called into question, and many once unimaginable realities have now become part of daily life. But the exodus of talent is far from over. As a new employee at a global consulting firm told us, “We are at the beginning, not the end, of a massive upheaval in work life.”

These transformations have raised numerous questions about the future of work. Where are we going? What will the future look like? How should leaders prepare for it? What should organizations focus on as they push forward? The rapid growth of digitalization and artificial intelligence? The many impacts of the shift to hybrid work? Employee benefits? The changing priorities of Millennials and Baby Boomers? Social and environmental responsibilities? The unstable geopolitical environment and our uncertain economic future?

Faced with these multiple, interdependent challenges, it’s understandable that leaders feel overwhelmed. “I’ve done everything I could to keep this company united during the pandemic,” said the CEO of the aforementioned consulting firm. “When I think about the scale of future challenges, I feel like I have nothing left to give.”

We are scholars of leadership, sustainability, and organizational change. In recent years, we have focused our attention on how leaders and organizations can best respond to these challenges. Through our academic research and professional consulting, we’ve learned that if leaders want to understand and adapt to the forces reshaping work, they will need to start thinking more specifically about three key areas of focus: 1) the future of the worker, 2) the future of work, and 3) the future of work itself.

In other words, *who*, *what*, and *why* we work.

**Three Areas of Focus**

The *who*, *what*, and *why* of work are closely intertwined. Thinking about their relationships won’t help us accurately predict the future, but it will help us prepare for it. Therefore, let’s consider each question in turn.

**The Future of the Worker (Who).**

The reflection sparked by the pandemic and the experience of breaking through lockdowns has led many to question the role of work in their lives. They have raised deep questions about the demands their organizations place on them and whether their organizations are worth their commitment. At the same time, they often demand that more of their “whole self” be brought into work, which forces leaders to pay more attention to topics such as gender, race, religion, and sexual orientation.

But leaders who become too focused on addressing diverse individual needs can lose sight of the bigger picture. Employees’ questions and concerns about work arise from a collision between two powerful forces: today’s macro socio-economic realities on one hand, and long-accepted understandings of what belonging to an organization and doing “good” work look like on the other. Any reflections on the future of work must take stock of this evolving status of the *who* and how it relates to the *what* and *why*.

**The Future of Work (What).**

Hybrid, remote, and flexible working are here to stay. Despite attempts to compel staff to return to the office, levels of remote working remain four to five times higher than before the pandemic. These new practices fundamentally alter how people work, where they work, and when they work, but their consequences are not yet fully understood.

Leaders feel pressured to announce “standardized” policies to provide some reassuring certainty in an unsettling ambiguity. But in the current turbulent macro context, any sense of certainty about how we should work is only illusory and temporary.

At the same time, digitalization is rapidly reshaping the way people interact with machines, influencing the tasks we perform, the roles we fulfill, and the structures that support and guide our organizations. By the mid-2030s, up to 30% of jobs may be at risk of automation, and the rapid development of artificial intelligence is bringing the future into the present at a pace much faster than most people predicted. How can leaders shape a constantly evolving work environment and employee tasks driven by rapidly advancing technology?

**The Future of Work (Why).**

As we adapt to new energy infrastructures, an aging population, and a hotter, more volatile Earth, long-term macro forces and short-term shocks are placing a series of new demands on businesses. Supply chain disruptions are becoming increasingly frequent. Political events are also triggering rapid shifts in public sentiment—such as discussions on racial inequality or commercial relations with Russia and China. Alongside these macro changes, people’s desires and actions are also evolving, reflecting deepening differences in values, aspirations, and life choices.

Some leaders are responding with new mission statements that define how they will meet societal needs and the increasingly diverse expectations of shareholders. But these long-term efforts are filled with short-term ambiguity, as redefining a company’s role in society has sparked strong opposition. This is to be expected, as we are undergoing a cultural shift and beginning to develop a new understanding of what a “good company” looks like, and consequently, what constitutes “good work.”

We have more agency than we realize.

The transformations reshaping the object, content, and purpose of work are beyond our control to some extent, and this situation is likely to continue. Even if the direction forward is clear, no one can accurately predict when the next action or human crisis will occur, or whether it will be triggered by technology, war, climate, or social unrest. Regaining agency begins with recognizing our role within this complex system and noticing how workers, work, and the nature of work itself are intertwined. Ultimately, the future of work does not “lie out there.” It exists within us and our organizations, and we can help shape it together with our colleagues through the steps outlined below.

**Leading Together in Uncertainty.**

In complex environments and geopolitical challenges, people generally believe that localized actions can only yield limited results, and that solutions lie in collective action. Just as nations are powerless in the face of complex systemic challenges, so too are leaders within organizations. To address the challenges of the future of work, leaders need to understand that their responsibility is not personal but collective, bringing together the complex alliance of colleagues across the entire organization. Collective leadership is a core component of the future of work.

Remember the exhausted CEO of the consulting firm we mentioned at the beginning of this article, who felt, “I have nothing left to give”? He bears the responsibility of shaping the future of work within his organization. But he shouldn’t carry this responsibility alone. The burden is collective. His role is to bring together potential leaders at all levels of the organization to collaboratively solve this issue, expand collective insight, and contain emotional uncertainty.

**Expanding Collective Insight.**

Currently, the complexity of challenges in the work domain spans across levels, generations, functions, and regions. Only by bringing together multiple groups of stakeholders with competing interests can these challenges be addressed, as these stakeholders collectively can generate broad insights and help the organization and its leaders successfully navigate uncertainty. By definition, senior leaders suffer the most in the short term, so they may be reluctant to raise the most challenging questions about the future. At the same time, the youngest employees benefit the most in the long run, as the status quo holds little value for them. For this reason, many of the organizations we collaborate with are exploring ways to integrate new and unfamiliar voices into their leadership discussions.

**Managing Emotional Uncertainty.**

While leaders need to rally and sustain leadership initiatives across the entire organization, they also need to build some emotional safeguards. At any time, a leader’s key role is to explore how best to balance an individual’s need for autonomy with the organization’s need for control. Although younger colleagues especially crave the autonomy and opportunities to pursue passion in their work, they still need to believe in their organization’s collective identity and their place within it. Leaders should remind employees of the best aspects of their organization’s past, reaffirm their cultural identity, and lay a solid foundation for building a collective future.

* * *

In the end, we all hope to belong to something greater than ourselves—something worth committing to. But the organizations we belong to are changing, and so are we. Facing the accumulated overwhelming challenges, it’s easy to distance ourselves from them and try to carry on as “normal.” But when the old sense of normalcy no longer applies, as it does today in the workplace, we must learn how to clearly think about what is happening. We must recognize that only by creating a new sense of normalcy for ourselves—one that adapts rather than resists ambiguity and ongoing uncertainty—can we finally understand this difficult and exciting period of transformation. This is not something isolated to us. But within our organizations, by focusing on collective leadership, we can begin to do it together.
