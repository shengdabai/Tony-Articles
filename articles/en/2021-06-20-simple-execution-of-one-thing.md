> Simple or complex, that is the question?

In the face of a complex world, everyone needs some simple principles. The WeChat Official Account “Far Read ReOcean” recommended a book with the same name as its title: *Simple Rules: How to Thrive in a Complex World*, written by Donald Sull, a professor of management at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and Kathleen M Eisenhardt, a professor of strategy at Stanford University. They spent 20 years researching how to make more rational decisions when facing a complex world.

> The principles are as follows: the first principle is the timing rule: you should set a clear deadline for your tasks, and this deadline isn’t arbitrary—it should have some form of incentive or punishment. The second principle is the priority rule: only do what is essential. If something is neither important nor urgent, forget it. When facing a problem, we need to clarify the timeline, what to do and what not to do, return to the core of the matter, and use our most precious time to solve the most critical tasks.

Sardines are also known as *sardinops*, and each individual is very small, so they must act in groups. Even when attacked by large fish, the entire school maintains formation, flexibly changing direction to avoid the attack. “Each fish is an independent entity, with no leader fish, yet the school can act as one without scattering. This is the form that future business units should take.”> Their principles are also simple: approach, align, and separate.

> Each fish follows the one in front (self-organization), stays in sync with the fish beside it (self-adaptation), and keeps a distance from the fish behind to avoid being caught (a dual feedback mechanism). In summary: follow the one in front without leading, stay in sync with the ones beside you without standing out, and maintain distance from the ones behind to avoid being overtaken!

Is my summary any good? Haha, the meaning is right, but it’s not very rhyming! So simple principles can maintain a complex system. In many cases, simple to the extreme, its power is hard to imagine. Next time when investing, let’s think—have we, as a little sardine, broken any rules? Or should we just keep following the regular investment plan and invest together?

Regarding long-termism, I’m sharing what I’ve learned with everyone, hoping it can inspire you!

> Long-termism, in English, is called *Long-termism*. There’s a related concept called strategic loss. Many companies have been around for a long time but haven’t made a profit. They say we are willing to accept strategic losses, and we don’t accept profits without strategy. We don’t just pursue tactical profits. In short, long-termism is first and foremost a worldview—meaning it’s a way of looking at the world and how things develop. Long-termism is not only a worldview but also a personal spirit. Generally, people who feel more secure in their hearts often didn’t develop a pathological obsession with safety or money due to early life instability. Such people tend to have a spirit inclined toward long-termism, for example, Mark Zuckerberg. Long-termism is a good thing, but it’s also a very difficult thing. Long-termism is a worldview and a spirit.

> For learning, to become a good short-termist, you must first be a long-termist. For example, programmers: some technologies can be mastered quickly. If you learn a popular technology in a short time, you might land a great job if the opportunity is good. But what if that technology is no longer popular? If someone has a solid foundation in computer basics, especially in areas like algorithms and mathematics, they can adapt to any new trend quickly. Learning new things fast, working fast, and being a multi-talented person—this itself is a kind of skill, and this skill is built up over the long term.

Regular investing is something that long-termists must always maintain. You don’t need to deliberately chase long-termism—it’s already there, naturally. Let’s invest together and pay tribute to Li Xiaolai!
