117th Original Piece

As an English teacher, I'm sensitive to translation issues in original English books: where we are situated becomes the correct understanding of ourselves, and the expression of words is more accurate than the language itself. During the translation process, information is inevitably lost. So, is there a perfect translation? If there is, it's only relatively smooth after some processing. Reading original editions in English, or other languages, allows us to obtain first-hand information, understanding the author's native language. Just like many of our classic books, they are hard to translate. To improve reading ability and tackle an English book, just read what you want, without considering your foundation or anything else. After all, under the trend of motivation, learning anything is efficient.

When I first saw the title of *The Most Important Thing*, I thought it might be clickbaity. Is the author really saying that investing is that important? It seems like everything is important. After reading, it really was that important, and using the term "most important" wasn't an overstatement. This made me think of the logical traps mentioned in my logic class. How unreliable we are sometimes—like our understanding of dollar-cost averaging. When we realize that the best and simplest investment strategy is dollar-cost averaging, we might unconsciously fall into a trap, thinking that nothing else matters, and that we should focus solely on dollar-cost averaging. What about other things? Are they unimportant? Do we not need to pay attention? Have you ever had this thought? I did, which shows how shallow my understanding is. If we only know dollar-cost averaging without deeper thinking and understanding about investing, we'll still waver, and our analysis of the market will be biased.

In fact, many decisions in the investment process are related to logic. If we don't better understand our thinking process and the structure of logic, we'll get lost during reading. We're easily trapped by various kinds of biases. Whether it's one kind or another, there are always logical pitfalls waiting for us. Some people say that every book you read gives you a new mental model. I think this book provides more than one. These are mental models we can apply in our thinking and learning in the investment field. The value of a model isn't in its universality or ability to solve everything. It's dynamic and adjusts according to specific situations. Choose the right model for the situation—it still has boundaries and a range of use. What's scary is using the wrong model and sticking to our wrong views.

Stay open, always reflect, and see through the essence.
