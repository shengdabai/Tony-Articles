# When Compute Is Scarce, Clarity Is Leverage

> 发布日期:2026-09-01 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-01-算力越稀缺人越要清醒.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-01-when-compute-is-scarce-clarity-is-leverage.md)

---

The shallow question about AI is whether it is a bubble.

The better question is what kind of scarcity AI is creating.

For the past few years, many people have described AI as if it were a machine for making intelligence free. You type a question, wait a few seconds, and receive a page of fluent language. You ask for code, outlines, summaries, strategies, images, translations, and plans. From the surface, intelligence looks like running water. Turn the tap, something comes out.

But the surface is misleading. What is becoming cheap is not the whole thing we call intelligence. What is becoming cheap is the first visible layer of output. Under that layer sit chips, power, memory, cooling, capital, data centers, engineering taste, context management, and human verification. These things do not disappear because the interface becomes friendly. They often become more important exactly because the interface is friendly.

This is the part we should take personally.

If AI were truly making thinking free, the best user would be the person who asks the most and delegates the most. But if AI is moving scarcity from output to judgment, the best user is different. The best user is the person who knows what deserves compute, what deserves attention, what deserves verification, and what deserves to be ignored.

That is a very different kind of person.

According to the [2026 Stanford AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/research-and-development), global AI compute capacity has grown 3.3 times per year since 2022, reaching 17.1 million H100-equivalents. That sounds like abundance. It is abundance, in one sense. But the same report also points out that the most capable models are becoming less transparent, and that frontier compute continues to rise even as disclosure falls.

The [IEA's 2026 report on energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) tells the same story from the physical side. Energy use per simple AI task has dropped dramatically, but reasoning, video generation, and agentic tasks can consume hundreds or thousands of times more energy than simple text generation. Data center electricity demand grew 17% in 2025, while electricity consumption from AI-focused data centers surged 50%. The IEA's central projection has data center electricity consumption roughly doubling from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030.

So the paradox is simple: each unit becomes more efficient, and total demand still explodes.

This is not unusual. When roads improve, people travel more. When cameras become digital, people take more photos. When writing becomes easy, people publish more text. When intelligence becomes available through an interface, people do not merely replace old tasks. They invent heavier tasks.

At first, we ask AI to summarize a meeting. Then we ask it to rewrite the strategy. Then to inspect the codebase. Then to run agents. Then to generate video. Then to compare markets, simulate customers, manage workflows, and maintain systems. The machine gets better, so our imagination gets hungrier.

That hunger is not wrong. It is the beginning of a new literacy problem.

The people who fall behind will not only be those without access to AI. Many people with access will still waste it. They will spend compute the way people spend attention on a bad feed: constantly, reactively, without memory. They will ask twenty versions of the same vague question, collect twenty plausible answers, feel busy, and end the day with no clearer judgment than before.

This is how AI can make a person weaker while making the person more productive on paper.

The output goes up. The ownership goes down.

I do not think the answer is to use AI less. That is too simple. A person can avoid AI and still avoid thinking. The stronger answer is to use AI with a budget.

A budget is not just a financial limit. A budget is a declaration of priority. It says: this matters enough to spend on; that does not. It forces you to put your real belief on the table before the machine starts producing options.

Before you ask an AI system to solve a problem, ask yourself three questions.

What am I really trying to decide?

What would count as evidence that changes my mind?

What part of this answer must I personally be able to defend?

These questions feel slow, but they save time because they prevent fake work. If you do not know what decision you are making, the model will happily generate materials for a meeting that should not exist. If you do not know what evidence would change your mind, it will decorate your existing preference. If you do not know what you must defend, you will become a distributor of language you do not own.

This is also where AI changes learning.

In the old world, many learners treated notes as storage. Read something, highlight something, save something, maybe summarize it. In the AI world, storage becomes almost free. The hard part is no longer keeping information. The hard part is turning attempts into a system that changes how you act next time.

One idea from recent AI research feels useful as a metaphor here: when an agent repeatedly improves an answer, the history of its failed attempts can be more valuable than a compressed summary of the final answer. The lesson for humans is not that we should hoard every word forever. The lesson is that premature compression often deletes the most expensive part of learning: why the previous path failed.

People love summaries because summaries feel clean. But growth is not clean. A useful memory contains friction. It remembers not only the conclusion, but the misunderstanding that made the conclusion necessary.

This is why the next personal productivity system should not only save prompts or outputs. It should save decisions, failed assumptions, corrections, and the reason a better question emerged. Otherwise, every AI session starts from zero, even if the folder is full of generated text.

The same rule applies to small products and solo businesses. Revenue can appear quickly in AI. Stripe's report on the AI economy says the top 100 AI companies on its platform reached $1 million in annualized revenue in a median of [11.5 months](https://stripe.com/ae/guides/indexing-the-ai-economy), faster than earlier SaaS cohorts. That is exciting. It also creates a trap: speed can disguise weak economics.

Traditional software taught many people to think that once the product is built, the marginal cost of serving one more user is low. AI applications are different. Each active user may bring model calls, retrieval, generation, evaluation, storage, and support. Growth is not automatically proof that a machine works. Growth is a stress test on the cost structure.

So the founder's question changes from "Can I get users?" to "Can I serve the next hundred users without selling my future to the runtime?"

That sounds like a business question, but it is also a personal question. When you use AI every day, you are building an internal cost structure. Some habits compound. Some habits create debt.

If every hard question becomes an immediate prompt, you may become faster and shallower. If every first draft comes from the model, you may publish more while losing contact with your own taste. If every uncertainty is resolved by asking another model, you may become dependent on external confidence.

But the opposite path is also available.

You can use AI as a thinking gym. You bring a rough position, ask the model to attack it, improve the position, test it against evidence, write down what changed, and then act. You can use AI as a microscope for your own assumptions. You can use it to make your ignorance visible earlier. You can make the system remember what you learned so the next loop starts higher.

This is what I mean by becoming stronger rather than lazier.

The lazy use of AI removes effort before it has produced understanding. The strengthening use of AI moves effort to a higher level. You stop spending human energy on formatting, repetitive search, boilerplate, and first-pass comparison. But you spend more human energy on framing, taste, evidence, constraints, and responsibility.

It is like moving from digging with bare hands to operating a machine. The machine reduces physical labor, but it does not remove the need for direction. In fact, bad direction becomes more expensive because the machine can dig faster.

This is why clarity becomes leverage when compute is scarce.

Clarity decides which problem enters the machine. Clarity decides how much context is enough. Clarity decides when to stop sampling and start building. Clarity decides whether a beautiful answer is useful, whether a benchmark matters, whether a task should be automated, whether a product should exist, whether a piece of writing says something true.

Without clarity, more compute creates more fog.

There is a quiet danger in the phrase "AI will make everyone more productive." It may be true in aggregate and false in the life of a specific person. A person can produce more artifacts, attend more conversations, generate more plans, and still fail to become more capable. The scoreboard moves; the person does not.

The real gain is not output per hour. The real gain is better judgment per loop.

A loop means: notice, decide, act, observe, update. AI can accelerate every step, but acceleration alone is not progress. If the loop has no judge, it will only repeat your confusion faster. If the loop records nothing, it will forget the lesson. If the loop has no contact with reality, it will become theater.

The new divide may not be between people who use AI and people who do not. It may be between people who let AI multiply their impulses and people who let AI refine their judgment.

The first group will look active. The second group will become dangerous in the best sense: harder to fool, faster to learn, calmer under novelty, more able to build from small beginnings.

That is the person I want to become. Not someone who asks the machine to live on my behalf. Someone who uses the machine to make my own choices clearer, my own practice tighter, my own learning less forgetful.

Compute is scarce. Attention is scarce. Trust is scarce. Time is still scarce.

AI does not abolish scarcity. It reveals what kind of scarcity you are made of.

So spend compute the way you would spend a serious life. Do not worship thrift, and do not worship abundance. Spend on the questions that can change your actions. Spend on the work where a better answer becomes a better system. Spend on the loops that leave you stronger after the output is gone.

The future will not belong to the person with the most prompts.

It will belong to the person whose questions keep getting better.
