# Does Your Interest Include the Everyday Work?

> 发布日期：2026-11-08 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-08-这份喜欢包含日常吗.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-08-interest-includes-the-work.md)

---

What do we mean when we say we like a particular line of work? Here is the standard I find more useful now: when you use AI to explore a new direction, do a small part of the core work you would still be responsible for, such as choosing a question, checking the evidence, or deciding what to leave out, before deciding to commit. The finished result can attract you. The everyday work gives you information for the choice.

You read a good article and want to write. You see a useful tool and want to build one. That spark matters; there is no need to interrogate it immediately. But once you are considering a continuing investment of time, or even a change in your work, the spark alone is a little thin. You need to know what you are willing to deal with repeatedly.

A clipped summary about doing great work connected choosing a direction with actually doing the work: before trying it, you can hardly know what it involves. That prompted a further question for me. AI can make a first attempt go smoothly. Could that smoothness conceal precisely the parts you need to understand?

The question you need to answer can be quite concrete: once I start doing this, which parts make me want to return, and which parts are merely being kept out of sight by a tool? Your answer affects both the next attempt and whether you continue.

## Do you like the result, or want to keep doing the work?

Suppose you want to try producing a weekly science-reading newsletter. AI recommends topics, condenses articles, drafts the text, and formats it. A credible-looking issue appears. You are pleased, and you begin to think this might be a good fit for you.

The pleasure is real. The question is which parts of the work you have tried. Choosing a question worth explaining, deciding what the original source actually supports, and removing an appealing but unsupported statement may all remain untouched. The finished product has arrived. The information you need to choose a direction may not have.

I find it helpful to separate two layers of interest. One is wanting a particular result. The other is wanting to keep working on the problems that produce it. The first is enough to get you started. The second deserves closer observation before a long commitment. You may have both, or for now only one.

Trying on shoes in front of a mirror and walking around in them tell you different things. Appearance tells you whether you like how they look. Walking reveals whether the heel slips or the toes are squeezed. An interest needs a little walking, too: let the actual work present a problem, then see how you respond.

Still, discomfort at work does not always mean a poor fit. When you are starting out, difficulty finding a source or understanding a sentence may simply mean you do not yet know how. And a shoe that rubs is hardly a reason to prove your willpower. You need to distinguish unfamiliarity, unsuitable conditions, and a responsibility you really do not want to carry over time.

For that newsletter, struggling with an original source may call for a little background learning. An inaccessible source may call for a different article you can verify. But if you persistently do not want to investigate whether a statement has support, it is worth asking: do you want to make a reading newsletter, or do you mainly like the feeling of having a polished issue finished?

Do not rush to a verdict about yourself. Record the conditions of the attempt as well. Did you have an uninterrupted stretch of time, or were you skimming between messages? When your attention never gets a chance to settle, it is hard to tell whether you dislike the work or have never properly entered it.

[A randomized experiment published in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11834938/) used a random assignment to decide who would block internet access on their phones first and who would do so two weeks later. During the block, participants could still make calls, send texts, and use the internet on computers. Among participants who completed the main measurements, the researchers reported improvements in sustained attention—the ability to stay focused over a period of time. Of the 467 unique people who committed to taking part, only 119 kept the block active for at least 10 of 14 days, meeting the researchers' advance standard—about a quarter. Most already wanted to reduce their phone use. The experiment did not measure career interests.

It makes me more inclined to include the setting when evaluating an attempt. An experience of constant interruption should not immediately become a lasting judgment about yourself. Give the work at least one more stretch of serious attention. What you need here is more information, not another label about lacking discipline.

One other detail: wanting to return need not feel like excitement. Sometimes an unresolved question simply stays with you after reading, and you want to investigate it. That thought is closer to the work itself than applause for a finished product. Record it, without immediately declaring it a lifelong passion.

## Which difficulties should you hand to AI?

You can reasonably turn this around: why do something by hand if a tool can already handle it? What is wrong with enjoying a newsletter but delegating its formatting? Authors can hire editors; surely every author need not relearn typesetting.

Fair enough. Tools change work. We should understand a possible direction in terms of the actual division of labor between ourselves and AI. Requiring a taste of every old procedure would turn an exploration of interest into an unnecessary endurance test. I care about the parts that would remain your responsibility.

[A working paper updated in 2026 by Contractor and Reyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849) studied undergraduates learning unfamiliar material and writing an essay under the same time limit, followed by unaided assessments immediately and roughly a week later. The researchers reported learning gains for the group permitted to use AI, with smaller gains remaining at the later assessment. The study concerned learning within a time limit; it did not test whether someone was suited to a line of work.

Making an attempt easier can therefore have real value. AI can explain difficult sentences and help locate information, letting you engage with questions that would otherwise be beyond your reach. The paper also distinguished students who sought explanations from those who had AI write for them. But students chose their own uses, so differences between those groups cannot directly establish the causal effect of a particular way of using AI.

My view is that, when exploring an interest, the key question is whether the tool gives you more contact with the work or less. Receiving a summary might send you back to the original to investigate something you do not understand. Or you might stop there and place the summary in the finished issue. Those processes leave you with very different grounds for choosing.

Return to the hypothetical newsletter. Having AI organize citation formats will usually do little to obstruct your assessment of whether you want to explain scientific questions. Having it locate a relevant passage that you then read in context may make the attempt more substantial. But if you delegate what to choose, what to trust, and what to remove as well, your main experience is receiving a result.

There is no permanent boundary between the human and AI roles here. You can design the newsletter around editing and organizing material rather than writing every sentence yourself. Then give editing a serious try: select, verify, revise, and decide whether something is ready to go out. You need to understand the work you are actually choosing.

[Another experiment, published in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/), compared mathematics practice using a standard chat-style AI, an AI with teacher-designed hints, and no AI at a high school in Turkey. The standard version improved assisted practice performance but was followed by poorer performance on an unaided exam. The version with hints and other learning safeguards largely avoided that loss. The experiment used the GPT-4 available at the time and measured short-term mathematics performance.

That study makes me wary of treating a finished submission as proof of the process behind it. It did not establish that an interest becomes more authentic with every additional task done by hand. The requirement I draw from it is narrower: to learn about your relationship with a kind of work, retain some actual contact with that work. Checking whether the finished result looks good is insufficient.

How much contact? Enough to encounter a real choice you must handle. The original conclusion is weaker than the headline: will you remove that sentence? The evidence is incomplete: are you willing to acknowledge that you do not yet know? You need not already be an expert to make these choices. They can still tell you whether you want to keep taking responsibility for such problems.

And do not deliberately retain the most mechanical part as a test of passion. Disliking the copying of citations or the adjustment of spaces says little by itself about an interest in explaining science. The task you keep should represent the judgments you would actually own. Otherwise you have merely tested a different job more thoroughly.

## How can you try enough to decide whether to continue?

One attempt will usually tell you too little about a lasting fit. You may have picked unusually difficult material or happened upon an easy topic. Record what you encountered, where you got stuck, and whether you want to return. That is more useful than rushing to announce a calling or dismissing yourself as someone who never sticks with anything.

Continuing has costs, too. In [a 2015 investment memo](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-06-08-risk-revisited-again.pdf), Howard Marks discussed both the risk of loss and the risk of missing opportunities: excessive avoidance of one can expose you to the other. This is an investor's conceptual argument, not an experiment on choosing interests. I use it as a reminder that you need not wait for certainty before trying, nor continue indefinitely simply because you have started.

Never trying can cost you a chance to learn about yourself. Trying every direction too superficially can leave you without enough information to choose. Set a small scope for an attempt, then decide what the next one needs to reveal. That helps connect what you invest with what you learn. You do not need to commit a long stretch of your life at once.

I suggest setting aside half an hour today for a small exercise. This is the author's proposal, not a research-tested measure of interest. Keep using the tools. For the science-reading newsletter, retain one part of the work you expect to own over time:

1. Spend two minutes writing down your expectations. Which action do you think you will enjoy? What will AI handle? What will you personally judge this time? Describe actions that will happen, rather than only the wish to produce a newsletter.
2. Spend twenty minutes checking one specific conclusion. Let AI help locate material or explain a difficult sentence. Read the relevant context yourself, then decide to keep the claim, remove it, or mark it for further checking, and write down why. If you do not finish, record where you got stuck.
3. Spend five minutes leaving a short passage another person could read. It can be brief; the point is to preserve a trace of your decisions. Do not make the issue look complete by having AI turn an unresolved point into a confident statement.
4. Use the final three minutes to record your response. Which step made you want to continue? Which would you want to delegate permanently? Would you return to tackle a similar problem next time? Record yes, no, or not yet clear.

These minute counts are simply a way to get started. They have no scientific significance as a measure of interest. After today's attempt, find two more occasions, in different states of mind or circumstances, to encounter the same kind of core work. Do not repeat only the easiest part, or deliberately choose the hardest material to prove you can suffer through it.

When reading your notes, separate the reasons you got stuck. If you lacked a skill, learn one small piece and try again. If the setting prevented engagement, find an uninterrupted stretch of time. If repeated contact still leaves you unwilling to own this kind of judgment, seriously consider a different division of labor or a different direction. These reasons call for different next steps. Lumping them all under “I don't like it” loses useful information.

AI can make trying things less costly. That is welcome. Use some of the saved effort to understand yourself more specifically: what you want to own, what you want to delegate, and which question deserves another visit. Leaving a direction can yield something valuable, too, provided you leave with a clearer understanding rather than simply a new label for yourself.

Start by writing down the small question you will personally judge today. Once you have done it, see who wants to come back tomorrow: you, or only the polished result.

- [Experimental Evidence on the Learning Impact of Generative AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849), Zara Contractor and Germán Reyes, 2026, updated October 4. Used to discuss how AI can help people engage with and understand unfamiliar material. The experiment took place at Middlebury College in spring 2025, with a common time limit and self-selected ways of using AI. This article does not use statistical values affected by obvious display errors in the captured text.
- [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/), Hamsa Bastani et al., 2025. Used to distinguish assisted performance from engagement with the underlying process. Conducted at one Turkish high school in fall of the 2023–2024 academic year; findings concern that tool design and short-term mathematics tasks, not career interests.
- [Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11834938/), Noah Castelo et al., 2025. Used to explain why the conditions of an attempt deserve recording. Participants were iPhone users in the United States and Canada, most already motivated to reduce use. Primary analyses depended on participants completing all measurements; compliance and attrition limit generalization.
- [Risk Revisited Again](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-06-08-risk-revisited-again.pdf), Howard Marks / Oaktree Capital Management, 2015. Used to highlight the costs of loss and missed opportunities. It is a conceptual argument in an investment memo; its application to exploring interests is the author's extension.
