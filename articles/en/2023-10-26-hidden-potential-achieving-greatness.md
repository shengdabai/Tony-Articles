**Preface: "Roses Growing from Concrete"** _Have you ever heard of a rose growing from the cracks in concrete?_ _It proves that natural laws are wrong,_ _It learned to walk without feet,_ _It looks funny, but it keeps its dream,_ _It learned to breathe fresh air._ _--- "Roses Growing from Concrete" by Tupac Shakur_ That was in 1991, on a cold spring weekend, when some of the smartest young people in America gathered at a hotel just outside of Detroit. As they walked toward their assigned seats, the chatter in the lobby was loud and lively. The clock struck, and the lobby fell silent, only the ticking of the second hand could be heard. At that moment, everyone's eyes were fixed on a row of black and white squares---this was the U.S. National Junior Chess Championship. In those years, this tournament had been dominated by private schools and elite institutions. These schools had the resources to make chess part of their curriculum. The reigning champion of this year's tournament was Dalton Academy, a prestigious preparatory school in New York City, which had won the national championship three times in a row. Dalton Academy had built an International Chess Olympiad Training Center. Every kindergarten child learned chess for a semester, and every first-grader learned it for an entire year. The most talented students had the opportunity to receive instruction from the best chess coaches in the country before and after class. Dalton's crown jewel was Josh Weitzman, a child prodigy whose story would later become the basis for the popular movie *Searching for Bobby Fischer*. Although Josh and another star player didn't compete in the tournament that year, Dalton had a strong chess team. No one considered the Raging Rooks (Rook is a chess piece, also called a castle, which can move infinitely in both horizontal and vertical directions, so according to Chinese chess conventions, it's usually translated as "rook") to be a contender. When they nervously entered the hotel, their members were eye-catching. The children had little in common with their wealthy white opponents: the Raging Rooks were a group of poor students of color---six Black boys, one Latin American child, and one Asian American child. They lived in communities ravaged by drugs, violence, and crime. Most of them grew up in single-parent households, raised by mothers, aunts, or grandmothers, whose annual incomes were not enough to cover the tuition at Dalton Academy. The Raging Rooks were eighth and ninth graders from JHS 43, a public school in the Harlem district of New York City. Unlike their opponents from Dalton Academy, they had not undergone a decade of training or years of competition. Some of them had only learned the game in sixth grade. Captain Kassion Hall learned chess by chance at age 12 and practiced with a drug dealer in a park. In the national championship, each team only took the best scores, discarding the lowest. A team like Dalton, with so many talented players, could discard up to six players' scores. But the Raging Rooks had trouble gathering enough players to compete. Every point mattered---they had no room for error. To win, every player had to perform at their peak. The Raging Rooks initially made a strong impression. Earlier, their weakest player defeated an opponent with a much higher score. The rest of the team was encouraged and went on to defeat more experienced opponents. A total of 63 teams participated, and by the semifinals, the Raging Rooks were ranked third. Although the children lacked competition experience, they had a secret weapon---their coach, a young chess master named Maurice Ashley. Maurice was a 20-something Jamaican immigrant who was dedicated to breaking the stereotype that children with dark skin were less intelligent. He believed, based on his own experience, that while talent is evenly distributed, opportunity is not. He could see potential where others overlooked it, and he wanted to grow roses from concrete. But in the penultimate round of the competition, Maurice watched his team start to decline. After gaining the lead, the captain Kassion made a mistake, almost failing to draw the game. Another player, on the verge of victory, was checkmated by his opponent and defeated. He cried, ran out of the room. Another game started too poorly, and Maurice turned away from the competition hall. It was too painful to watch. By the end of this round, the Raging Rooks dropped from third to fifth. Maurice reminded the team that they could only control the decisions they made, not the results they wanted. To win the championship, the Raging Rooks had to win their next four games and hope the strongest team lost theirs. But no matter what happened, they were already the best in the nation. They didn't need to win to win people's hearts. They had already surpassed all expectations. Chess is considered a game of genius. Top young players are often child prodigies, with extraordinary mental abilities, able to remember sequences, quickly analyze situations, and predict many moves ahead. If you want to build a championship chess team, the best way is to recruit a group of child prodigies and give them intensive training from a young age. Maurice did the opposite: he started with a group of high school students who just happened to be interested in chess and willing to learn. One of these students was the class bully. They were mostly B-grade students, and they were selected for the chess team not because they had any special chess talent. Maurice recalled, "Our team had no stars." However, when the final round began, the Raging Rooks still managed to maintain their ranking. Two players checkmated their opponents and won decisively, while the captain Kassion remained strong in his match against a higher-ranked opponent. Although he had the potential to pull off an upset, the Raging Rooks knew this might not be enough. Their first match in that round ended in a draw. A few minutes later, Maurice heard a shout from the corridor, calling his name: "Mr. Ashley, Mr. Ashley!" In the final stage of the intense battle, the captain Kassion surprisingly defeated the top player from Dalton Academy. This shocked everyone---the leading team made a mistake, opening the door for the Raging Rooks to tie for first place. The players cheered, hugged, and celebrated. "We won! We won!" In just two years, these poor kids from Harlem had grown from chess novices to national champions. But the most surprising thing was not that the underdogs won, but the reason they won---the skills they developed would eventually give them more than just a chess championship title. Everyone has hidden potential. This book is an introduction to how we unlock it. People generally believe that greatness is innate, not created. This leads us to celebrate talented students in school, talented athletes in sports, and child prodigies in music---but you don't have to be a prodigy to achieve greatness. My goal is to explain how **all of us** ordinary people can achieve greater success. As an organizational psychology researcher, most of my career has been spent studying the forces that drive people forward. What I've learned may challenge some of your basic assumptions about personal potential. In a landmark study, psychologists investigated the origins of extraordinary talent in musicians, artists, scientists, and athletes. They conducted extensive interviews with 120 Guggenheim Fellowship-winning sculptors, internationally renowned concert pianists, award-winning mathematicians, pioneering neuroscientists, Olympic swimmers, and world-class tennis players, along with their parents, teachers, and coaches. They were surprised to find that only a small number of these high achievers were child prodigies. Among the sculptors, not a single one was identified as having special talent by their elementary school art teachers. Some pianists won major competitions before the age of nine, but others only appeared talented when compared to their siblings or neighbors. Although these mathematicians and neuroscientists performed well in elementary and high school, they were not standout students in their classes. Almost none of the swimmers set records early on; most won local competitions but never reached regional or national championships. And most tennis players lost in the early rounds of their first matches, taking years to become local champions. If they were selected by their coaches, it wasn't because of extraordinary talent, but because of extraordinary motivation. This motivation is not innate; it often begins with a coach or teacher who makes learning fun. "Anything that anyone can learn, almost everyone can learn," the project's lead psychologist summarized, "as long as the right conditions for learning are provided." Recent evidence emphasizes the importance of learning conditions. A student usually needs seven to eight practice sessions to master a new concept in math, science, or foreign language. This number is based on thousands of students, from elementary school to college. Of course, some students performed exceptionally well after fewer practice sessions. But they weren't learning faster; their rate of progress was the same as their peers. What made them different was that they had more foundational knowledge when they started. Some students had already mastered the relevant knowledge before they began. Others were taught by their parents early on, or they started learning on their own. What appears to be differences in talent are often differences in opportunity and motivation. When we assess potential, we often make a focus error, overemphasizing the starting point---those immediately visible abilities. In a world obsessed with talent, we assume that those who immediately stand out are the most promising. However, high performers vary greatly in their initial abilities. If we judge a person solely by what they can do on their first day, their potential will remain hidden. You don't know where they started or where they will end up. With the right learning opportunities and motivation, anyone can build skills to achieve greater success. Potential is not about where you start, but how far you go. We need to focus less on the starting point and more on the distance traveled.

Every time a Mozart-like prodigy makes headlines, there are multiple Bachs slowly rising and blooming. They are not born with invisible superpowers; most of their talents are cultivated and developed by themselves. Those who achieve major progress are rarely natural-born monsters; they are usually monsters nurtured through effort. Ignoring the impact of upbringing has serious consequences. It leads us to underestimate the progress we could achieve and the various talents we can learn. As a result, we limit ourselves and those around us. We cling to narrow comfort zones and miss out on broader possibilities. We fail to see others' potential, close the doors of opportunity, and deprive the world of greater things. This book is not about ambition. It's about desire. As philosopher Agnes Callard emphasized, ambition is the result you want to achieve, while desire is who you want to become. The issue is not about how much money you earn, how many "beautiful" titles you get, or how many awards you accumulate. These status symbols do not truly represent progress. What matters is not how hard you work, but how much you grow. And growth requires more than just a mindset; it begins with a series of skills we usually overlook---**the right things**. In the late 1980s, around the same time the Raging Rooks were learning chess in Harlem, Tennessee launched a bold experiment. In 79 schools (many of which were low-income schools), they randomly assigned over 11,000 students to different classes from kindergarten to third grade. The initial goal was to test whether small-class teaching was more beneficial for learning. But an economist named Raj Chetty realized that since students and teachers were randomly assigned to classrooms, he could look back and analyze other classroom characteristics that might have an impact. Chetty is one of the most influential economists in the world. He is a MacArthur Fellow. His research shows that excellence is not as dependent on our talent as we imagine. Tennessee's experiment had an astonishing result. Chetty could predict students' adult success simply by observing their kindergarten teachers. By the age of 25, those who had been lucky enough to have more experienced kindergarten teachers earned significantly more than their peers. Chetty and his colleagues calculated that replacing an inexperienced kindergarten teacher with an experienced one would increase each student's annual income by over $1,000 in their 20s. For a class of 20 students, an above-average kindergarten teacher could bring an additional lifetime income of $320,000. Kindergarten is important in many ways, but I never imagined that a teacher could leave such a lasting mark on students' future earnings. Most adults don't even remember what they were like at age five. Why do kindergarten teachers leave such a long-lasting impression? The intuitive answer is that good teachers help students develop cognitive skills. Early education lays a solid foundation for understanding numbers and words. It's not surprising that students with more experienced teachers scored higher on math and reading tests at the end of kindergarten. But in the years that followed, their peers would catch up. To understand what students carried with them from kindergarten to adulthood, Chetty's team turned to another possible explanation. At fourth and eighth grade, their teachers evaluated some other qualities of the students. Here's an example:

  * Proactiveness: Did they often ask questions, answer questions, seek information from books, and participate in extracurricular learning with teachers?
  * Sociability: How did they interact and collaborate with their peers?
  * Self-discipline: Did they effectively focus and resist impulses that disrupt classroom order?
  * Willpower: How did they consistently accept challenging questions, do more work than assigned, and persist in the face of obstacles?

When students were taught by more experienced kindergarten teachers, their fourth-grade teachers gave them higher scores on these four attributes. Their eighth-grade teachers did too. Proactiveness, sociability, self-discipline, and willpower lasted longer than early math and reading skills and were ultimately proven to be more powerful. When Chetty and his colleagues predicted adult income based on fourth-grade scores, these behavioral scores were 2.4 times the standardized test scores in math and reading. How surprising this is. If you want to predict a fourth-grader's income potential, you should focus more on your teacher's subjective assessment of their behavioral patterns than on their objective math and language scores. Although many people believe these behaviors are innate, they are taught in kindergarten. No matter where students start, learning these behaviors will lead them to success decades later. **The Power of Character** When Aristotle wrote about qualities like self-discipline and sociability, he called them virtues of character. He described character as a series of principles people acquire and develop through sheer willpower. I used to see character this way too---I thought it was a matter of commitment to clear moral principles. But my work is to test and refine the various views philosophers are passionate about debating. In the past twenty years, the evidence I've collected has led me to rethink this view. I no longer see character as a matter of willpower, but rather as a set of skills. Character is not just about having principles. It's the ability to live according to principles. Character skills enable a chronically procrastinating person to complete a task on time in front of someone they care about, enable a shy introvert to stand up against injustice, and enable a class bully to avoid fighting with teammates before an important match. These are the skills great kindergarten teachers cultivate, and they are also the skills great coaches nurture. When Maurice Ashley formed his chess team to compete in the national championship, a student named Francis Idhan was not among the top eight players. Maurice still chose him because of his character skills. "Another child was an excellent chess player," Francis told me, "but he didn't develop the emotional self-regulation skills Maurice considered important." When the Raging Rooks fell behind in the penultimate round of the national championship, Maurice Ashley didn't pull out a secret tactical book. He didn't even talk to them about strategy. "I reminded them to be disciplined," he said---this was a skill they had practiced for two years. Their character skills caught the attention of legendary chess coach Bruce Pandolfini, who had coached many students to national and world championships. After watching "Raging Rooks" win, Pandolfini was amazed: "Nothing can confuse them. Most children under pressure start to rush or show their emotions, but they don't. They take their time, and they have no expression on their faces in front of the chessboard. I've never seen children of this age so calm. They're like professionals." If a knight on the chessboard were a Trojan horse, Maurice had secretly brought an army of character skills into the game. They helped the Raging Rooks rise quickly when facing opponents who were flustered. "He always taught life lessons without putting pressure on people," Francis said. "It's more about understanding oneself and mastering oneself than executing a chess strategy. It was crucial to my life." Maurice saw the value of character skills in his own life. During his growth, he saw his mother sacrifice everything for the dream of immigrating to America, while his grandmother stayed in Jamaica to raise him and his siblings. When they finally arrived in New York ten years later, they knew opportunities wouldn't come automatically; they had to create them. After accidentally discovering a book about chess in the high school library, Maurice decided to join the team. But he quickly realized he wasn't good enough. He fully dedicated himself to improving his level and later became the captain of the university team. When he got a job teaching chess in a Harlem school for $50 an hour, he happily accepted the invitation. Today, if you ask anyone in the chess world about Maurice, they'll tell you he's an outstanding strategist. In the game, if you move your bishop instead of your rook, he can tell you how many moves it will take for you to be checkmated and whether you'll lose your queen in the process. He simultaneously played ten different games against ten opponents, all while blindfolded. But he believes character is more important than talent. Evidence shows that although children and beginners who are smarter may learn chess faster, intelligence is almost irrelevant in predicting the performance of adults and advanced players. The early cognitive advantage in kindergarten chess competitions tends to fade over time. Becoming a chess master on average requires 20,000 hours, and reaching the level of a grandmaster requires 30,000 hours. To maintain progress, you need proactiveness, self-discipline, and determination to study old games and new strategies. Character skills not only help you reach peak performance but also push you to higher peaks. As Nobel Economics Prize winner James Heckman summarized in a review of research, "character skills predict and create life success." But they won't grow in a vacuum. You need opportunities and motivation to cultivate them. **If you build the scaffolding, they can climb up** When people talk about parenting, they usually refer to the continuous investment parents and teachers make in nurturing and supporting children and students. But to help them fully realize their potential, we need something different. It's a more focused and temporary form of support that allows them to guide their own learning and growth. Psychologists call this scaffolding. In construction, scaffolding is a temporary structure that allows workers to climb to heights they couldn't reach on their own. Once the building is complete, the scaffolding is removed. From then on, the building can stand independently. In learning, scaffolding works similarly. Teachers or coaches provide initial guidance and then remove the support. The goal is to transfer responsibility to you so you can develop your own independent learning methods. This is exactly what Maurice Ashley did for the Raging Rooks. He built a temporary structure, providing them with opportunities and motivation. When Maurice started teaching chess, he saw other coaches set up all the pieces and teach standard opening moves: the king's pawn moves two steps forward, then the knight moves one step up and one step diagonally. But he knew learning the rules could be boring, and he didn't want the children to lose interest. So when he first introduced the game to a group of sixth graders, he took the opposite approach---he placed some pieces on the board and started from the endgame. He taught the students different ways to checkmate, helping them understand how to win in chess---this was the first scaffolding. People often say, "Where there's a will, there's a way." But we often ignore a fact: when people can't see the path forward, they stop dreaming about the destination. To inspire their will, we need to show them the path. This is what scaffolding can do. By teaching the game in reverse, Maurice ignited the fire of determination. Once the students knew how to corner the king, they had a path to victory. Once they had a winning method, they had the will to learn. "You won't tell children, 'Okay, you need to learn patience, determination, and perseverance,' because they'll fall asleep immediately," he laughed. "You say, 'This game is fun. Let's play. I'll beat you.' It ignites their spirit, their competitive fire. They sit down, start learning the game, and when they fall in love with the game, when they lose the game, they want to win." Soon, Kassion Hall would lie in bed every night, imagining 64 squares on the ceiling, and play chess in his head. Maurice also introduced other scaffolds to support the players' mutual development. He taught them creative ways to share skills: they drew cartoons about chess strategies, wrote science fiction stories about chess games, and recorded rap songs about controlling the center of the board. They learned to see a solitary game as a socialized team exercise. When a player cried during the national championship, it wasn't because he lost, but because he felt he had let his teammates down. As they gradually became a team, the players began to take control of their learning motivation and opportunities. They demanded from each other to record every move in the game so the whole team could learn from individual mistakes. They didn't worry about being the smartest player in the room; their goal was to make the whole room smarter. In the previous year's national competition, due to budget constraints, the Raging Rooks still managed to achieve a top 10% ranking despite having fewer participants. When Maurice set the goal for them to win the next year, the team members actively created a plan. Now, they had skills and the will. They organized a temporary chess training camp, practicing and reading related books all summer. They convinced Maurice to join their training and let him give up his summer vacation. They had already taken the driver's seat of the battle. In an ideal world, students don't need a coach to get these opportunities. Maurice's scaffolding replaced a broken system. A parent told Maurice that when she saw her son playing chess, she realized she had never believed her child could. Maurice not only helped his team unlock their potential but also helped their parents and teachers recognize it. We rarely have coaches like Maurice Ashley. We don't always have access to ideal mentors, and our parents and teachers don't always provide the right support and guidance. My purpose in writing this book is to make it that scaffolding. This book is divided into three parts. The first part explores specific character skills that drive us toward higher levels. You'll learn about these skills from a self-taught professional boxer who taught himself architecture; you'll also meet a woman who escaped poverty through intense learning; and you'll meet two students who struggled with a particular subject in school but are now among the world's top talents. The second part discusses building structures that support motivation. Even with strong personal abilities, no one is immune to fatigue, doubt, or stagnation. But achieving significant success doesn't require you to be a workaholic or push yourself to the brink of exhaustion. To illustrate the scaffolding that maintains motivation, I'll introduce a musician who built a temporary structure to overcome a permanent disability; a coach who helped turn an average athlete into a superstar; and a group of overlooked officer candidates who proved everyone was wrong. You'll discover that practice without real participation is incomplete; spinning might be the best way to move forward; and a shoelace isn't just someone's responsibility. The third part focuses on building systems to expand opportunities. Society should open doors to those with great potential, but often closes them to those facing the greatest obstacles. For every person who was underestimated or overlooked and then broke through, there are thousands who never had the chance. You'll learn how to design schools, teams, and institutions that cultivate and don't waste potential. By visiting a small country that has one of the most successful education systems in the world, you'll see how we can help every child make progress. Studying one of the most miraculous rescues in human history will reveal how groups can surpass the sum of their individual parts. To find out how we can fix the broken selection process, I'll take you inside NASA's astronaut selection process and the behind-the-scenes admissions at Ivy League schools. By changing the systems that exclude people too early, we can increase the chances of the disadvantaged and those who come later. I care about unlocking potential because I've experienced it firsthand. My most meaningful achievements were in areas where my initial talent was severely lacking. Thanks to excellent coaches, I grew from the worst swimmer in school to the national champion, from failing in small lectures to receiving standing ovations at TED. If I had judged my potential based on early failures, I would have given up. What I learned in this process helped me build my own scaffolding for future leaps. This made me determined to uncover the mystery of how we can surpass the limits we call our own. As a social scientist, I start with data: randomized experiments, longitudinal studies, and meta-analyses (studies of studies) to quantify cumulative results. Then, I begin personal reflection and look for stories to bring the research to life. I met people who far surpassed their starting points in various environments and uncovered their hidden potential---from underwater and underground to the tops of mountains and outer space. I want to understand how they walked long paths by changing themselves, others, and sometimes even the world around them. This is exactly what the Raging Rooks did. Their success played a role in changing the face of chess. Coaches estimate that since they entered the chess world, the percentage of minority participants in national championships has increased fourfold. Maurice has become an advocate for chess, viewing it as a tool for cultivating personal character, and the movement he helped start now takes place in low-income schools across the United States. A non-profit organization dedicated to chess has already taught over 500,000 children. There's no reason to believe this magic is limited to chess alone. If debate happens to be Maurice's passion, he would guide students to anticipate their opponents' rebuttals and help them refine their responses together. What matters is not the activity itself, but the lessons you learn. As Maurice said, "Achievement is in growth." Due to the opportunities and motivation Maurice provided, the Raging Rooks applied their character skills beyond chess. The self-discipline they showed in resisting short-term moves helped them resist gangs and drugs. The determination and enthusiasm they showed in memorizing patterns and predicting moves are equally applicable to exam preparation. The social skills they developed through collaborative practice and mutual criticism helped them become excellent collaborators and mentors. Most players managed to surpass their environments. Jonathan Nock came from a high-crime community, was robbed while passing by a basketball court during a successful season, but is now a software engineer and founder of a cloud solutions company. Francis Eden once dodged a stabbing and a shooting on his way to school, but eventually earned a degree in economics from Yale University, an MBA from Harvard University, and became a financial director at one of the largest public utility companies in the United States and a chief operating officer at an investment company. Kassion Henry was once homeless and recruited by a gang member, but now has three master's degrees and is an award-winning film producer and composer. "Chess developed my character," Kassion recalled. "Chess improved my focus and attention... Chess ignited me. Someone lit a star, and it will continue to burn in every moment of my life." In 1994, the principal of another school three blocks away from JHS 43 asked Maurice to coach their Dark Knights team. Over the next two years, their men's and women's teams won national championships consecutively. By then, Maurice was ready to take on his next historic mission. He paused his coaching work and began focusing on improving his chess skills. In 1999, Maurice became the first African American chess master in the United States. That year, under the guidance of a new coach, the Dark Knights team won their third national championship. Their assistant coach was Charu Robinson, who later taught chess in schools across the city, planting seeds in countless children. The Raging Rooks were not just solitary roses growing from concrete cracks. They cultivated the soil, paving the way for more flowers to bloom. When we admire great thinkers, doers, and leaders, we often focus only on their performance. This leads us to praise those who achieve the greatest success while overlooking those who achieve the most with the least. The true measure of your potential is not the height of the peak you reach, but how far you climbed to get there.
