How does the investor's psychological and emotional pendulum swing? What are the characteristics of this swinging?

Emotional fluctuations in the securities market are like the movement of a pendulum. This pendulum swings back and forth, forming an arc, with the center of the arc perfectly describing the pendulum's "average" position. The investor's psychological and emotional pendulum swings from excitement to depression, from celebrating good news to worrying over bad news, from overvaluation to undervaluation. This phenomenon of swinging from one extreme to another is the most certain feature of the investment world. Investor psychology is like a pendulum, often swinging to extremes, either one extreme or the other, rarely staying at the center point, rarely achieving the happy and fulfilling equilibrium state that our culture emphasizes as the middle way.

The pendulum always swings, moving toward one extreme or the other. Then it swings to an extreme, stops there, and begins to reverse, turning back toward the center point. The force that pushes the pendulum back to the center is the reversal of kinetic energy. One could also say that the pendulum often swings back to the center point or returns to the average, but like most cycles, the pendulum often overshoots, passing by the center point and continuing to swing toward another extreme, completely opposite to the extreme it started from.

Why does the investor's psychological and emotional pendulum swing?

Humans are unreliable, subjective, and not rational. Our emotions are easily influenced. Few people can consistently remain calm and not be trapped by emotions. Because of this, only a few investors can set benchmarks, define the position of the center point, and remain in a balanced state between greed and fear, no matter how the future develops. They can always maintain balance, neither too greedy nor too fearful. In contrast, most investors swing dramatically between greed and fear. When emotions are optimistic, they become overly greedy; when emotions are pessimistic, they become overly fearful. Most investors' psychology and emotions swing to the wrong positions at the wrong times. When good news appears, the market becomes overly excited, and they rush to buy, pushing prices to overvalued levels. At this point, most investors become even greedier, wanting to buy more. Conversely, when bad news appears, the market becomes overly pessimistic, and they rush to sell, pushing prices to undervalued levels. At this point, most investors become even more fearful, wanting to sell more.

How to Accumulate Your Emotional Capital?

Observe your own psychology and emotions, and objectively view yourself and the market. Strive to cultivate yourself to become mature and rational, skilled in analysis, objective, and not swayed by emotions. Work to thoroughly analyze the fundamentals and environment of investments. Strive to maintain an appropriate balance between fear and greed. Strive to resist external influences and maintain emotional balance, taking rational actions, seeing both favorable and unfavorable events, and remaining calm and objective when considering these events, without emotional bias.

> Everyone finds that the more they buy, the more it rises, so they buy even more, forming a virtuous cycle that allows tech stocks to keep rising, as if they can rise forever.

Market prices will respond continuously due to investors' "self-fulfilling prophecy." The more you believe it will rise, the more you buy, and the more you buy, the more it really rises. This is why good years tend to follow one another. This phenomenon will continue for some time until all the bearish parties join the bullish side, and the market begins a sharp reversal.
