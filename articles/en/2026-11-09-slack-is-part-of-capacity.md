# Slack Is Part of Your Capacity

> 发布日期:2026-11-09 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-09-日程里的空档，也是产能.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-09-slack-is-part-of-capacity.md)

---

The faster AI works, the less sense it makes to schedule your day around its fastest performance. If several outputs still need the same person to assess, revise, and deliver them, the time saved on drafting cannot simply be exchanged for an equivalent number of new commitments. When rework arrives, the deadlines you promised other people may already be crowding together, before you have enjoyed any of the time you supposedly saved.

I would count slack—the time you deliberately keep unassigned—as part of a person's capacity. By capacity, I mean how much acceptable work someone can reliably deliver within an agreed period. That is a different question from how many drafts they can generate. Unassigned time does not create finished work by itself. It gives work that goes wrong somewhere to be repaired, without always delaying the next task to make room.

This matters for anyone using AI to do more than they could alone. We can let machines work on more things at once while keeping time available to handle surprises ourselves. Those two decisions belong together.

## Does starting more mean taking on more?

It depends on who finishes the work.

One of today's notes is a saved summary about collaboration among multiple AI agents. Its discussion of task division, handoffs, and acceptance checks brought a neglected step to mind: once several AIs have started working, who handles the results they return at the same time? Another summary, of an interview about developing model-based products, raised the issue of unpredictable model behavior. Together, they sharpened the question. Being able to start work simultaneously does not tell us when it will be finished.

Suppose an independent developer promises to deliver three tool user guides that day. AI drafts them in parallel; the developer checks and publishes them. All three drafts come back quickly. The first omits a necessary condition. The second describes steps that no longer match the updated interface. The third needs its scope checked again. Generation is finished. Delivery is not.

If the developer schedules work according to how quickly drafts appear, taking on another three can look reasonable. But there is no second version of the developer available that afternoon. Work requiring personal judgment piles up, while the people waiting for it are already making plans around the promised deadlines. What is missing may be a block of time that has not yet been promised to someone else, rather than a stronger model.

Exploration and commitment need separate accounts. When experimenting with guides for yourself, you can start many drafts, continue with one, and leave the rest aside. Once you have promised to deliver all three, you cannot shelve them whenever your interests change. Machines make additional attempts inexpensive. An additional external commitment also creates another obligation that must be handled on time.

A [study](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11436v4) conducted in 2023–2024, involving 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers, offers a useful reminder. The researchers assigned access to Copilot by chance, like drawing names from a hat, and compared those workers with people who were not assigned access. This helps keep pre-existing differences between the groups from driving the comparison. Copilot is an AI tool built into Microsoft office applications; it can summarize emails and draft text.

For the 6,441 workers with the relevant Outlook records, the researchers estimated what happened in months 4–6 after access was assigned. The access group spent about 1.4 fewer hours per week in Outlook email sessions, on average. Yet the researchers did not detect meaningful changes in the quantity or composition of workers' tasks. The study tracked activity within Microsoft office applications; it did not directly measure overall output or work quality.

Making one step faster is valuable. How many more commitments that permits requires different evidence. In our hypothetical case, that evidence concerns the whole process of taking on the three guides and delivering acceptable versions. Drafting speed cannot answer that question.

## What is the empty time for?

Rework that will not make an appointment.

A supermarket can let many people shop at once, but they must eventually pass through a limited number of checkouts. Wider shopping aisles do not let one register check out several carts simultaneously. Parallel AI drafting resembles many shoppers filling their carts at once. The checks and decisions that still require you resemble the checkout.

A queue need not be a serious problem. Trouble begins when one cart has a pricing error: the person resolving it cannot serve the next shopper at the same time. AI outputs add another kind of variation. Some need only a quick check; others send you back to the original material to reconsider the answer. Checking out groceries usually follows fixed steps. Judgment work can reveal what is missing only after you are halfway through it.

If every block of time already belongs to the next delivery, the extra work must push something back. Later tasks can start late even if nothing goes wrong with those tasks themselves. Slack catches that deviation between commitments. It also protects work that has not yet encountered any trouble.

Results arriving together need not be delivered together. For the three guides, the developer could keep drafting in parallel while staggering personal checks and delivery times. That would not make the checking itself faster, but it could reduce the chance of every problem landing at the last moment. If the recipient genuinely needs all three together, however, finishing must be planned around that requirement. The fastest completion of one guide cannot stand in for the whole batch.

A tempting response is to run more trials, learn the average duration, and then fill the schedule. The average helps. But knowing how accurately you have estimated an average differs from knowing how long the next attempt will take.

A 2023 [PNAS paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10438372/) examined how medical professionals, data scientists, and university faculty interpreted scientific charts in three randomized experiments. Charts emphasizing only the precision of estimated averages could make individual outcomes seem too predictable. Showing variation among individual outcomes alongside the estimates generally produced more accurate judgments. The experiments concerned particular scientific displays; applying that distinction to scheduling is my own extension.

For those three guides, I would want to know both the average revision time and why the most troublesome guide took so much work. If incomplete source material caused the problem, faster drafting might do little to shorten that delay. More observations can improve our understanding of the average without automatically making every attempt equally smooth.

## How much time should stay unassigned?

Measure your own work before choosing a ratio.

I do not favor a fixed slack percentage for everyone. Different tasks require your intervention in different places. An average that mixes formatting, fact-checking, and open-ended judgment may offer little help when making the next commitment.

In 2018, Kevin E. Thomas and Cornelius J. König conducted [three experiments on task duration predictions](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00760/full). Students performed tasks a week apart. Compared with participants who had previously done a different task, those who had already done the same task made less biased predictions of its duration the second time. Providing or prompting recall of the previous task's exact duration did not show an additional reduction in bias. These were short laboratory tasks, so their results cannot supply a slack allowance for real projects.

My takeaway is that recording duration is insufficient unless you also record what the task involved. If one guide merely organizes existing material while another requires resolving conflicts between sources, calling both tasks writing a guide erases a difference that matters for time.

The record need not be elaborate. I would note what I personally did after AI produced the draft, which delays came from missing material, which came from judgment, and what standard finally counted as delivered. Then a similar task can be compared with a relevant experience. A single total-hours figure makes it easy to mistake last time's good fortune for ability.

It is especially useful to distinguish time spent working from time spent waiting for material. If a guide's source material will not arrive until tomorrow, leaving more time free today will not make delivery today possible. The commitment needs a different starting point: confirm the deadline once the material is ready, or deliver a clearly bounded portion first. Slack can absorb extra work. It cannot bring an external condition into existence ahead of time.

In his 2013 [essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham advises that founders can solve problems manually for a small number of users, then gradually automate the bottlenecks. This is advice drawn from startup practice, not an experimental result that applies to everyone. For personal AI work, what interests me is the sequence: going through the whole process gives you firsthand evidence for deciding which step to replace and which still requires you to be available.

Uncertainty is no reason to stop all automation. Let AI handle the familiar parts while treating unfamiliar parts as experiments that need time. Learning a new approach requires room. Trying a new tool while assuming the delivery speed of an experienced user builds the desired outcome into the schedule before you have earned evidence for it.

## Isn't this too cautious?

That depends on how the slack is used.

Someone will reasonably object that leaving time unbooked means losing opportunities. For a person with limited resources, that cost is real. If a task is simple, its output is easy to inspect, failure is cheap to repair, or reliable automated checks are already in place, reducing human involvement and increasing parallel work may make better sense.

The work I am concerned with still requires your judgment when unexpected issues arise and has a deadline promised to someone else. If the checking itself becomes reliably automated, the old allowance should be recalculated. Organizing your life forever around an older model's worst performance would also waste new capability.

Before that reliability is established, however, a full schedule has a cost too: you lose room to rearrange. When a new problem appears, you must delay something, shorten the checks, or use time you had not intended to spend working. Choices remain, but each has a price. If these measures become routine, apparently high capacity contains time that has yet to be paid for.

Working longer in the evening to capture an unusual opportunity can also be a deliberate choice. What matters to me is how it is recorded. If completing this job required extra hours, those hours belong in the estimate for the next one. Recording only that delivery was on time, while omitting the additional work, turns emergency overtime into assumed everyday capacity. Eventually, even you may lose track of how much effort was actually saved.

I would use AI to expand the range of work within reach, without treating it as a reason to present myself as a larger organization. In a [personal account published in 2017](https://signalvnoise.com/svn3/act-your-size/), Jason Fried recalls describing his solo business as if it were a company with a team. He also acknowledges that he cannot tell whether the presentation actually won him business. One founder's experience does not guarantee that honesty will bring orders.

For one person working with several AIs, the reminder is concrete: customers need to know who handles surprises and when that person can respond. Describing yourself as if many people were available does not increase the number of people who can handle unexpected issues simultaneously. A business can be small. Its commitments must fit what it can actually absorb.

Slack should also be tested. If reserved time repeatedly goes unused while work is completed to the required standard, reduce some of it and try making more commitments. If rework consumes all of it every time and delivery is still late, change the scope, deadline, or way the work is handled. The allowance should evolve with experience, rather than becoming an insurance premium that never needs justification.

## What can change today?

Reschedule one commitment first.

There is no need to rebuild your entire working method. For the same three guides, the following small experiment is my proposal; I have not carried it out. The aim is to understand the pressure on finishing after parallel drafting, rather than to prove that I can necessarily take on more work.

1. Before starting, define when each guide counts as finished. Complete text is not enough: necessary conditions, instructions, and scope must have been checked. Also write down how much of your own time you expect to need.
2. When setting deadlines, keep a block of time unassigned. Initially, base its length on similar work you have recently done. Without a relevant example, reduce the scope of this commitment instead of assuming that every step will go smoothly.
3. During the work, record the reason for each extra intervention and how long it takes. Verification, revision, and waiting after AI has finished generating still belong to the actual process of completing the job.
4. Afterward, compare the estimate with what happened. If the reserved time went into the same recurring problem, consider improving the input, supplying missing material, or adding a check. If it mainly absorbed unrelated surprises, retain room to respond and collect more records of similar tasks.

If the record shows little rework, with the main problem being all three guides waiting for checks at once, try staggering drafting or checking next time. If every guide repeatedly lacks the same kind of material, improve the input requirements first. Both changes may reduce the need for slack, but they address different problems. Always adding time when work feels busy, or always adding tasks when it looks fast, skips the judgment that matters most.

One record cannot establish a person's permanent capacity. A smooth attempt may simply have had complete source material; a difficult one may have encountered an unusual problem. Use this round's record to improve the next comparable schedule, then expand commitments as experience accumulates. That is more useful than labeling yourself efficient or inefficient.

There is another point: if rework does not consume the slack, you need not immediately fill it with a new task. It can become rest, or time to notice something worth improving. If every open block must be filled as soon as it appears, it loses its ability to absorb problems that have not yet arrived.

I want AI to help me do more things I could not otherwise do. I am also willing to accept that this does not make every part of my schedule available for sale. As ability grows, the commitments I make remain my decision.

Look at the next piece of work you have already promised to deliver. If it needs rework partway through, where will that time come from?
