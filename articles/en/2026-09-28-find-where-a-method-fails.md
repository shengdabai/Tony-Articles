# Learn a Method by Finding Where It Fails

> 发布日期:2026-09-28 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-28-学方法先问它在哪儿会失灵.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-28-find-where-a-method-fails.md)

---

Imagine using AI to set up a simple spending sheet. Money received goes under income; money paid out goes under expenses; at the end of the month you check what is left. The sheet comes together quickly. Then you pay for something on someone else's behalf and later get reimbursed. Apply the old rule without thinking, and the repayment may show up as new income. The chart still looks tidy, but it now answers the wrong question. This is an imagined example, not something I claim to have experienced. It makes me ask: when we learn an operation, do we also learn where that operation stops making sense?

Among today's collected notes is a secondary summary about AI that treats changing strategy in an unpredictable environment as an important question. A summary is not evidence for a technical claim, but the question turns my attention back to human learning. Here is my view: one of the most valuable parts of learning a method is learning when it still applies and when to pause and change it. Repeating an action on familiar problems can make fluency feel like understanding.

## Why can a fluent action fail in a new situation?

How do we usually decide that we have learned something? We watch a demonstration, repeat it, get faster, and make fewer mistakes. That is useful. Complex work needs basic actions we can perform without starting from scratch every time; otherwise each small step consumes our attention. But speed mainly tells me that I have become smoother under these conditions. It does not answer a second question: will I notice when the conditions change?

Return to the spending sheet. Entering a payment as an expense is a repeatable action. A reimbursement brings a different difficulty. Whose money was that, and is the sheet meant to show cash moving through an account or what I actually spent? If I am reconciling account transactions, a repayment is indeed an inflow. If I am estimating what I earned, calling it income distorts the result. The same number means different things under different questions. A formula cannot choose the question for us.

This is what I mean by transfer of learning: in a new situation, using what you have learned where it fits and knowing when it does not. It sounds like a classroom term, but it turns up in ordinary life. You learn to write a polite reminder, then need to clear up a misunderstanding with someone you know. You learn a fixed format for reading notes, then encounter an essay whose author deliberately leaves a question open. The old format may help, or it may squeeze out the point. Good judgment requires knowing the problem the original steps were built to solve, as well as remembering the steps themselves.

[Barnett and Ceci's review of learning transfer](https://pdf.retrievalpractice.org/transfer/Barnett_Ceci_2002.pdf) highlights something easy to miss: a new problem is not merely an old problem with different words. What was learned, the time since learning, the place and purpose of use, and whether someone prompts you to use the old method all matter when we assess transfer. The paper offers a framework for comparing research; it does not prove that one training routine works everywhere. For a reader, the useful lesson is simple. When we say someone can do something, we should add the conditions under which we know they can do it.

## Why can AI make the boundary harder to see?

AI can make the first task look excellent. Give it a goal and it supplies steps, examples, and a checklist. You revise the result and deliver it quickly. That is real help. The trouble is that a fluent result can hide two questions at once: do I understand why this approach works, and does the next problem meet the same assumptions? If the tool keeps filling in the judgments between steps, I may not even notice which judgments I skipped.

A [math learning experiment published in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) gives this concern a concrete scale. In a single Turkish high school during the 2023–2024 academic year, researchers compared practice without AI, practice with a GPT-4 tool resembling a regular chat interface, and practice with a version designed to offer hints and supplied with teacher prepared solutions. The regular chat group did better during assisted practice. On a subsequent unaided test, however, its scores were about 17% lower than those of the group without AI. The hint based version largely removed that deficit, but it did not produce an unaided score above the control group's. This is a short term mathematics result from one school. It does not show that every adult who uses AI learns less.

I do not take this as an order to ban tools. I take it as a design question: which step is the tool doing for me? If it does the calculation, I can still practice deciding what needs to be calculated. If it chooses the formula for me, I lose a chance to recognize the relevant conditions. Both kinds of help save effort, yet they exercise different abilities. On the next unfamiliar problem, the hardest part may be realizing the precise point at which I started applying the wrong steps.

A [survey of knowledge workers using generative AI](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) collected 936 work examples reported by 319 people. The researchers found that greater confidence in AI on a particular task was associated with less reported critical thinking on that task. Participants described more of their thinking as checking information, integrating responses, and overseeing the work. These are self reports and associations, not proof that AI caused anyone to lose an ability. Still, the study sharpens a question I want to ask whenever an output looks effortless: did the tool spare me repetitive work, or did it quietly take over the recognition I meant to practice?

## Should we carry the rule, or the conditions for using it?

I think of a method as a key. A practiced person can insert and turn it quickly. A person with deeper understanding also looks at the lock first. In this analogy, the key is the set of learned steps and the lock is the task at hand. The similar mechanism is that success depends on a match, not just on how smoothly one performs the motion. The analogy has a limit. Real problems are not as stable as keyholes: goals can be negotiated, methods can be modified, and sometimes the task itself needs a new definition. The image reminds me to inspect the conditions; it cannot establish that any particular method works.

What conditions should we inspect? At least these: is the goal the same, are the inputs reliable, and is the cost of a mistake comparable? Using AI to organize a draft for my own review and using it to write instructions that another person will act on may involve similar writing steps. They should not share an identical standard of acceptance. A private draft can leave some uncertainty unresolved. Actionable instructions need checked grounds and visible exceptions. The steps may transfer while the standard of verification does not. Seeing that difference is part of knowing how to use a method.

This also explains why adding practice hours is not a complete answer. In a [2019 study revisiting influential violin practice research](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/16/The_role_of_deliberate_practice_in_expert_performance_-_revisiting_Ericsson%2C_Krampe_%26_Tesch-R%C3%B6mer_%281993%29.pdf), Macnamara and Maitra studied 39 violin students. Practice amount was meaningfully related to skill, but the researchers did not reproduce the earlier finding that the most accomplished group's average accumulated practice exceeded the next group's. The sample was small and the estimates of past practice relied on memory. The study does not make practice unimportant. It does make me wary of an overly convenient claim: repeat long enough and judgment will automatically appear.

What we practice matters too. If I only do one kind of problem, I may become better at spotting familiar surface cues. When the goal changes, fluency can push me to make the same mistake faster. By contrast, a practice set with one near miss, a problem that looks familiar but violates a key condition, forces me to name the assumption I had been skipping. With the spending sheet, I would not merely enter another ordinary purchase. I would decide how to record a payment made for someone else, a refund, and genuine income, explaining which question the sheet is meant to answer. The point is not to turn life into an exam. It is to attach a statement of scope to the method.

## Must every little task demand this much effort?

The strongest objection deserves a fair hearing: much of the time, we need to finish a job, not turn it into training. A calculator does multiplication for us; we need not work out every product first. AI can smooth an ordinary paragraph without requiring a special exercise in boundary recognition. Turning every task into a learning experiment would consume time and slow down work that is already clear. I agree. When consequences are light, answers are easy to check, and the task is routine, using a mature tool directly makes sense.

That is exactly why it helps to decide when practice is worth the effort. My test is not whether a task sounds impressive. I ask: will I need to judge this kind of thing on my own later? Could an error escape notice before it causes harm? Might I carry today's method into a setting with different conditions? If the answers are yes, possibly, and yes, I am willing to spend some time testing the boundary. If this is simply a one time, low risk formatting task, I will let the tool handle it and check the finished work. I can spend the attention I save on a judgment worth learning.

There is a harder objection than efficiency. Practicing judgment in changing situations can sound like shifting every uncertainty onto the individual. Some work depends on teachers, colleagues, public rules, and reliable tools. A beginner cannot know every gap in their knowledge in advance. In the math experiment, the hint based tool did more than withhold full answers: it also included correct solutions and common errors supplied by teachers. We cannot attribute its result simply to students thinking harder on their own. Good support can make important boundaries visible.

So I am not proposing that people struggle alone. I want support that can be examined. Alongside an answer from AI, I can ask for its assumptions, exceptions, and points that need human confirmation. When the source or responsibility matters, I can check the original material or consult someone who knows the field. This is no ritual that guarantees correctness. It is a way to bring judgments hidden inside a smooth result back into view. Even when I lack domain knowledge, recognizing that I need to check something is progress.

## Give a new method one boundary problem

If I wanted to try this today, I would start with one small thing already on my desk, not invent a grand training program. First I would write down the specific question the method answers and two conditions it quietly assumes. Perhaps my spending sheet is meant to show what I actually spent, assuming each payment was mine to bear and each incoming amount really belongs to me. If I cannot name those conditions, I may have memorized an operation without understanding why it works.

Next I would change just one condition to make a nearby new problem. Keep the sheet, but add a payment made on someone else's behalf. Before asking AI to edit the sheet, I would decide whether the old rule still applies, which number would become misleading, and why. Then I would ask AI for a different treatment and for the question each treatment is suited to answer. AI's value here is more than producing a revised table; it can help expose a condition I missed. If its explanation cannot be checked, I would keep the question open and look for a reliable definition or example.

Finally, after some time has passed, I would try a case that looks different on the surface without being prompted with the name of the old method. Can I recognize the shared structure myself? Can I also explain where copying the old approach would fail? If so, I would count the exercise as useful. If not, I would revise my account of the conditions instead of calling myself a bad learner. This delayed check is a small personal experiment, not a measure that proves long term growth after one success. It tells me what to practice next.

The same approach works for writing, language learning, or using an AI tool: name the task a method was built for, then find a case that differs slightly but might change the right answer. Do not alter every condition at once, or you will not know why your answer failed. Do not choose only cases guaranteed to work, or you will merely demonstrate your familiarity again. Judgment grows at the boundary, when you can explain why you keep using a method or why you stop.

In the opening example, the easiest addition to the sheet is another classification formula. The more valuable addition is a question: does this money really count as my income? I want to carry that pause into the next thing I learn. A method helps me move faster. Knowing where it fails tells me when to slow down. If I can explain why I follow a set of steps, and when I would refuse to follow them, the method has truly become mine.
