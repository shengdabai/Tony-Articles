# Who Gets the Gains from AI?

> 发布日期：2026-11-03 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-03-省时之后的分配题.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-03-who-gets-the-gains-from-ai.md)

---

In [a writing experiment](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf), participants assigned access to ChatGPT completed their tasks an average of ten minutes faster than the control group. The control group was the comparison group not assigned ChatGPT access. What interests me is what comes next: who gets those ten minutes? My view is that saving time with AI is only the beginning of a benefit. Whoever can decide how the saved time is used actually receives that part of the gain.

This finding comes from a 2023 working-paper version of a study by Noy and Zhang: a research draft shared for discussion before peer review. It involved 444 college-educated professionals completing short writing assignments, with some randomly assigned access to ChatGPT. Average completion time was about 17 minutes in the treatment group and 27 minutes in the control group, and output ratings improved too. The study examined short, self-contained professional writing tasks. It did not establish who received a raise, who worked less overtime, or what happened to their careers over the long term.

The time saving was real. But describing it as a benefit to the person using the tool can skip the step that matters most in daily life: who has the authority to decide what happens to that time?

## Myth 1: Working faster means the time is yours

The usual calculation is straightforward: subtract the new completion time from the old one, and the difference is the benefit. But that difference is simply labor time no longer required. It does not tell us whether more assignments will follow, whether the price will change, or whether the person doing the work can finish earlier.

For the same assignment, payment per deliverable at an unchanged price may mean that working faster raises hourly earnings. Payment for actual hours worked at an unchanged hourly rate may mean lower total earnings. With fixed pay and a fixed workload, the saving may directly create more rest. These are conditional possibilities; the writing experiment did not compare such arrangements. A timer cannot answer a contract question.

Another possibility is harder to see: you finish early, but the next stage is still waiting for materials or approval, so final delivery happens no sooner. Here, no one has necessarily taken the saving. It may not yet have become usable time. Realizing that gain requires looking at how work passes between stages. Urging everyone to finish their own part faster does not necessarily finish the whole job earlier.

Suppose a household gets a washing machine. The person who used to stay beside the washbasin can finally leave. If that person controls the freed time, they can read or sleep. If other chores immediately fill the gap, the household gets more done without necessarily giving that person more rest. The machine has reduced the effort needed to wash clothes. The people in the household still have to decide who enjoys the reduction.

AI resembles the washing machine in replacing some of the labor a task requires. Work also involves pay, competition, and customer demands, though, so household arrangements cannot simply be carried over into a contract. Two questions need separate answers: how much labor has the task saved, and how will that saving be shared?

This makes me want a little more from a personal productivity report. Beyond completion speed, I want to know what additional choices I actually have when the work is done. I might stop working for a while, serve another customer, or reserve my energy for a difficult problem. Without that choice, higher efficiency may still be valuable; much of the benefit may simply go elsewhere.

The shape of the time matters too. Scattered gaps in a working day do not automatically add up to an afternoon you can use freely. A task may return at any moment, leaving you on standby. For an individual, an uninterrupted period away from work is often closer to real rest than a total of minutes saved in a spreadsheet. Agreeing on finishing times and response expectations can turn saved time into time that is actually available for life.

Make the intended use explicit. In a working relationship where every improvement in speed immediately becomes a higher delivery requirement, workload and pay need to be discussed together. Otherwise, a person can repeatedly invest in learning tools and receive little more than an increasingly tight next round of assignments.

## Myth 2: More output means more income

An AI-generated summary of a clipped business commentary sets rising usage alongside pressure on unit prices. It raises a question; it does not verify that these market changes occurred. The question closer to individual work is this: why might producing more fail to increase income in the same proportion?

Because the buyer has reasons of their own for paying. Speed is a capability on the supply side; willingness to pay is a choice on the demand side. Between them sit alternatives, differences in quality, and how easily those differences can be judged. Making something cheaper to produce does not mean you get to keep the entire saving.

The writing experiment contains another revealing detail: 68% of treated participants reported submitting ChatGPT's initial output without editing it. The researchers also found no higher average ratings for participants' submissions than for raw model output. At least for that set of tasks, we cannot assume that an additional round of human work automatically adds value.

I draw a limited inference from this: when a type of deliverable is easy to generate and buyers can readily find alternatives, lower prices may pass some of the gain to buyers. They may also make a service accessible to people who previously could not afford it. That is a good outcome. A benefit going to customers does not automatically mean the tool has failed, and a lower service price alone does not show that the provider has lost out. If hourly earnings stay unchanged while access to the service expands, both sides may benefit. What needs checking is what each side actually contributed and received. Output growth alone cannot establish income growth.

Another easily overlooked step is whether the other person can recognize the difference. [Michael Luca's Yelp study](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/12-016_a7e4a5a2-03f9-490d-b093-8f951238dba2.pdf) used restaurant revenue data from Seattle for 2003–2009. It exploited thresholds in the rounding of displayed star ratings to examine restaurants with very similar underlying scores but different displayed ratings. Displayed ratings affected revenue at independent restaurants, while no significant comparable effect appeared for chains. This was a historical restaurant market: the study measured the effect of rating information on demand, and did not establish that star ratings equal objective quality.

It reminds me that doing something well and helping others recognize it require different work. Customers cannot inspect how clever every step of your generation process was. They can understand whom a deliverable suits, which parts have been checked, and which decisions remain theirs. Making those differences clear gives them a basis for choosing.

When discussing efficiency gains, I therefore want to include the effort the other person no longer has to spend. If they still have to do just as much work supplying missing material, revising requirements, and distinguishing truth from error, faster production may not have become a benefit for them. Taking on all that work for free is not a reasonable default either. The deliverable's scope and any additional service need to be made clear together.

## Myth 3: Remove the labor, and usable capacity remains

Some of the labor we remove also helps us become familiar with a task. An easier submission today does not settle whether we will be better able to handle a new situation next time. Here, the gain is being allocated between the present and the future.

[A mathematics-learning study published by Bastani and colleagues in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) compared two AI interfaces with classes that did not use AI at one high school in Turkey. Conducted in fall 2023, it recorded both performance while students practiced with a tool and performance when they answered independently without it. The webpage flags a correction, but the supplied packet does not contain its text. I therefore do not cite the exam results or infer which interface improved learning.

Tools may make practice easier in different ways, and what a person can do without them needs separate attention. For a skill you need to acquire yourself, skipping steps may mean missing practice. For work you intend to delegate to a tool, reducing human involvement can be a success. Every delivery does not need to become an exam, and completing a delivery does not establish that you have learned the skill.

Someone might ask: if the tool remains available, why spend time learning the underlying skill? That is a fair question. Using a tool does not require relearning every activity it replaces. What matters is whether this is a capability you will need for judgment, correction, and exceptions. If it is, reserve time to practice. If it is not, reliably getting the task done may be enough.

Replacement costs matter as well. Faster AI writing may require more fact-checking. Automating parts of a job may mean spending more time tracing the cause when an exception occurs. These are possible costs to record in the specific task. We should neither assume they occur in every task nor leave them out simply because generation is fast.

I propose counting the whole period from starting a task to having something genuinely usable: preparing materials, generating, checking, and reworking. For a learning task, I would separately preserve an opportunity to attempt it myself. This is an author's proposed recording method; its effectiveness has not been tested. The hint-based interface in the mathematics study was supported by correct solutions and common-error materials supplied by teachers. Its design cannot be reduced to adding a casual instruction to a prompt.

If saved time is spent on necessary checking, AI has not necessarily been useless. The same investment may have bought a more reliable result. But the gain being realized is then quality, with no additional rest for now. Naming the actual benefit accurately helps us decide whether to use the same approach next time.

## Myth 4: Every free minute must be filled again

I like the idea of investing some savings in more valuable work. I do not want it to become another demand: the stronger the tool, the less permission a person has to stop.

In [his 2013 essay on startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham described Airbnb's founders going door to door in New York to recruit users, helping existing users improve their listings, and photographing early hosts' apartments. He emphasized that working directly with early users can help founders understand how a product needs to change. These were startup cases he participated in or observed, rather than a general rule for everyone running a business.

This suggests one possible use of an efficiency gain: spend the energy no longer needed for manual drafting on understanding what someone actually needs. Airbnb's experience itself did not test AI or establish that saved time must be spent serving customers. What I value is the work it brings into view: contact with actual users can sometimes change a product more than another increase in delivery volume.

But founders need to survive. When orders arrive, they may have to accept them. How much choice is there? In the short term, turning a saving into more deliveries may be perfectly reasonable. The question is whether someone knows they are making that exchange and has placed limits on it. Taking on more work for now and accepting more work forever by default are different decisions.

Allocation cannot depend entirely on individual resolve, either. Wanting to keep saved time does not necessarily give a person the authority to keep it. A customer willing to pay may not know that the service scope has changed. Whoever controls work arrangements needs to discuss efficiency changes alongside task requirements. If greater speed is always just a reason to raise expectations, tool users will struggle to tell when their investment in learning will pay off. The work arrangement needs adjusting; another faster model may not solve the problem.

Rest deserves to be an explicit outcome too. No study plan, no side-business target: simply wanting an uninterrupted meal or an on-time finish is a sufficient use of AI. Greater human capability should include greater control over one's own time. An individual has no obligation to reinvest every efficiency gain in production.

Here is an untested proposal from the author: use a recurring task to record where the benefit may go.

1. Before starting, choose a task you already need to complete and know how to assess. Record its usual scope and total time. Then state what you want this time: less working time, capacity to accept more work, or higher quality. Choose one primary goal.
2. After completing the task with AI, include the time actually spent preparing, checking, and reworking. Record savings that have occurred, rather than treating the model's claimed savings as results.
3. When the task is over, record what actually happened to any freed time, and whether workload, charges, or checking effort changed. Compare this with the original goal to see whether the gain went to you, the customer, or another party involved in the work arrangement.
4. Before the next similar task, change one condition that affects allocation. Clarify delivery quantities, define the scope of extra revisions, or genuinely reserve a period for rest. Then observe whether that choice can be put into practice.

Tasks differ in difficulty, so results will vary. This recording method may offer clues, but no evidence yet shows that it can accurately identify who received a benefit; a single record cannot establish that a tool caused every change. I propose it to move attention from how much the model generated to what you actually received.

The last time AI saved you some time, what did you end up doing with it—and was that your decision?
