Seeing this title, I immediately thought of a book. Let me talk about it first. The book is called *Learning Entrepreneurship from Journey to the West*, and the angle is quite interesting; you can take a moment to read it. It tells the story of a "noble son" (Tang Sanzang) leading four "ex-convicts" (including Bai Long Ma and the other three disciples) through great difficulties and eventually successfully reaching the West to retrieve the scriptures. This book not only uses management theories to discuss entrepreneurship but also cleverly applies cutting-edge international economic theories, using the mythological story of *Journey to the West* as an example to humorously explain how Guanyin built the team for the journey, how Tang Sanzang established leadership authority, designed incentive mechanisms, evaluated employees, structured power, and implemented corporate governance. It deeply analyzes the process of a startup team moving from its initial stages to becoming stable.

This image looks pretty interesting, doesn't it? I really like it. It allows me to absorb the essence of traditional Chinese wisdom from *Journey to the West*, and also understand the core issues and solutions in modern entrepreneurial management. I think as my entrepreneurship journey deepens, I may have more insights.

Regarding this title, I mainly analyze the four main characters from *Journey to the West*. In Ferguson's *Leadership*, there is a question: What is leadership? It's not power, not ability, not being able to handle people, not being good at pleasing others. Most people are confused, so essentially, whoever has a clear map and is determined to follow it has strong leadership. Tang Sanzang seems to have no skills at all; it's always his disciples who save him. So why is he the leader of the team?

Sun Wukong is powerful, but he has no rules. His map is basically nonexistent. Zhu Bajie's cognitive map always points backward; he wants to pack up and go back to Gao Laozhuang the moment he's unhappy, so he's not suitable to be a leader. Sha Wujing has been stuck in the Flowing Sand River for his whole life and has little vision, so his cognitive map only includes what's in front of him, and he can work diligently. Only Tang Sanzang has a clear map. No matter whether you go or not, I will go to retrieve the scriptures, and the direction is India.

Tang Sanz,ang's advantage lies in his ability to build a resource exchange platform and a capability collaboration platform. He has a rich network of resources and power transactions. When Sun Wukong, who only has competitive advantage but no ecosystem advantage, faces a complex and difficult situation, he often relies on his ecosystem advantage to solve the problem. As a leader, one should seek ecosystem advantage. In today's interconnected era, a company is unlikely to win in the market solely based on a closed, internal competitive advantage. It should form a complex and precise system of cooperation and synergy, learning to position itself as a unit of capability and interest to collaborate with other companies for mutual benefit.

Recently, I've been reading *Atomic Habits*, which is also known as *The Power of Habit*; I prefer the former. "Atomic" has three meanings: atoms are small, and atomic habits are small changes, a 1% improvement; atoms are the basic building blocks of all matter, and atomic habits are the basic units of our lives. All atomic habits combined make up every aspect of our lives; although atoms are small, they contain great power, as seen in atomic bombs and nuclear power plants. Our investment in each atomic habit is not much, but when accumulated, they can bring significant changes to our lives. Although atomic habits are small and easy to do, their cumulative effect can have a huge impact on our lives.

We all know the importance of habits, but why do we keep repeating bad habits and find it hard to develop good ones? Why do we set goals on New Year's Day, only to give up before the Spring Festival because we can't keep going? The author believes there are two reasons. First, the level we try to change is wrong. Changing behavior has three levels: outcome level—what we want, such as losing weight or winning a championship; process level—what we do, such as going to the gym twice a week; identity level—what we believe, including self-perception, self-image, and how we judge ourselves and others. This is the deepest level. Although these three levels all play a role in our change, the author believes that most people approach the change in the wrong order. We usually focus first on the outcome, forming a habit centered on the outcome level. The author suggests we should first focus on our desired identity, forming a habit centered on the identity level.

I've started trying this. For example, when I look at my phone while reading this article, I reward myself with 10 push-ups. Although it's a small exercise, it helps me reduce phone usage. The habit is small, but it shouldn't be ignored. Take it step by step, keep going,keep going😊
