# Do Not Outsource Your Apprenticeship

> 发布日期:2026-08-21 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-21-别让AI替你完成学徒期.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-21-do-not-outsource-your-apprenticeship.md)

---

The most dangerous thing AI can do is not produce a wrong answer.

A wrong answer still leaves a trace. You can test it, reject it, argue with it, and learn something from the collision.

The more subtle danger is that AI can produce a good enough answer before you have earned the internal machinery that would let you judge it.

That sentence sounds conservative at first. It is not. I am not trying to protect every boring task from automation. Most boring work deserves to disappear. Copying, formatting, sorting, first-pass summarizing, draft generation, mechanical comparison, and repetitive checking should be delegated whenever the result can be verified.

But not all boring work is the same. Some boring work is just waste. Some boring work is apprenticeship in disguise.

The old route into skill was full of slow, awkward, low-status work: looking things up, rewriting a paragraph, comparing three examples, making a clumsy first draft, noticing why one sentence lands and another does not, fixing the same type of error until your hand begins to move before your theory catches up. Much of that work looked inefficient from the outside. From the inside, it was building taste, timing, pattern recognition, and the ability to suspect a plausible answer.

AI now walks into that room and says: here is an 80-point version in ten seconds.

That is wonderful if you already know how to evaluate the remaining 20 points. It is dangerous if the 80-point answer arrives before you know what was difficult about the task.

The new question is not whether we should use AI. We should. The question is what kind of human is being produced by the way we use it.

I kept thinking about this through a strange bridge: scientific software.

In a serious scientific workflow, a black-box model is rarely enough. A model can suggest a chemical route, a hypothesis, a design, or a ranking. But if the system cannot explain, verify, and constrain that suggestion, it is hard to trust. The issue is not merely that the model might be wrong. The issue is that when it is wrong, you may not know where the wrongness entered. If changing one condition silently changes several other hidden assumptions, improvement becomes guesswork.

So in serious domains, AI does not simply replace judgment. It has to be wrapped in interpretable mechanisms, domain rules, tests, and feedback loops. The answer has to pass through a white-box layer before it becomes action.

That is also a good description of human learning in the AI age.

If AI gives you an answer, your job is not to admire the answer. Your job is to build the white-box layer inside yourself. You need to know what assumptions were made, what evidence would change the conclusion, where the answer is fragile, which part is routine, and which part requires responsibility. Without that layer, you are not becoming stronger with AI. You are becoming a fast approver of someone else's reasoning.

This is why "AI makes people lazy" is too shallow a diagnosis. Laziness is only the surface. The deeper issue is a broken apprenticeship loop.

In the old loop, effort came before output. You struggled, produced something imperfect, received feedback, revised, and slowly acquired judgment. In the AI loop, output comes before effort. The first artifact appears immediately. If you treat that artifact as the end, the loop dies. If you treat it as a surface to interrogate, the loop becomes faster than before.

The same tool can weaken or strengthen you depending on where you place the difficulty.

Research on AI and cognition is still early, but several signals point in the same direction. A 2025 study from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of AI use at work. The finding was not "AI eliminates thinking." It was more precise: higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking effort, while higher confidence in one's own ability was associated with more. The study also found that critical thinking shifts toward verification, integration, and stewardship.

That last word matters. Stewardship is not passive use. It means you remain responsible for the work even when the machine helps produce it.

Another 2025 MIT Media Lab preprint studied 54 participants doing essay-writing tasks across three sessions, with 18 completing a fourth switching session. Its results should be read carefully because it is a preprint, the sample is limited, and essay writing is not all learning. Still, the pattern is worth noticing: the brain-only group showed the strongest and broadest neural connectivity, the search-engine group sat in the middle, and the LLM group showed the weakest connectivity. The LLM group also reported lower ownership of their essays and had more trouble quoting from what they had just produced.

Again, the point is not "never use LLMs." That would be a foolish conclusion. The point is that ownership matters. If the output moves faster than your ownership of the process, a debt forms. The document exists, but the writer did not grow at the same speed.

Learning science has known a related truth for a long time. The UCLA Learning and Forgetting Lab summarizes "desirable difficulties" as training conditions that can slow performance during practice but produce better long-term learning. The test-enhanced learning literature makes the same point from another angle: retrieval and testing can be learning events, not just assessment events.

This is uncomfortable because it means the feeling of fluency is not reliable. The moment AI makes a task feel smooth, we may confuse smoothness with mastery.

The practical answer is not to make work artificially painful. Pain is not virtue. Slowness is not depth. Plenty of old-school training was just bad design wrapped in tradition.

The answer is to move friction to the right place.

Before asking AI for help, make a prediction.

Write down what you think the answer will probably look like. It can be rough. It can be wrong. The point is to force your mind to enter the arena before the machine fills it. If you are writing, sketch the argument. If you are coding, describe the likely failure mode. If you are analyzing a market, name the variable you expect to matter most. A prediction gives you something to compare against. Without it, you only have a reaction.

While using AI, ask it to expose structure, not just produce output.

Do not only ask, "Give me the answer." Ask, "What assumptions are you making?" "What would make this wrong?" "Which part is routine and which part is judgment?" "Give me two alternatives and the tradeoff between them." The goal is not to make the model sound smart. The goal is to pull the task back into a form you can inspect.

After receiving the answer, perform one act of retrieval from memory.

Close the tab for a minute and explain the answer in your own words. Rebuild the core chain without looking. Name the strongest objection. If you cannot reconstruct it, you do not own it yet. You may still use it, but you should not confuse use with understanding.

Before shipping, sign your name to the consequence.

This does not have to be dramatic. It can be as simple as asking: if this is wrong, what happens, and who bears the cost? Low-risk work can move fast. High-risk work needs stronger verification. The important thing is that AI must not blur responsibility. A person who cannot own the consequence cannot claim the judgment.

These four habits turn AI from a shortcut into an apprenticeship machine: predict, inspect, retrieve, own.

They also change what "practice" means. Practice is no longer merely repeating the whole task by hand. Practice is designing the smallest loop that forces judgment to grow. Sometimes that means letting AI do the first draft and then doing a ruthless critique. Sometimes it means doing the first attempt yourself and asking AI to challenge it. Sometimes it means using AI as a simulator: create ten edge cases, compare failures, revise the rule, and run the loop again.

The old apprentice spent years beside a master because the environment produced dense feedback. The master saw the mistake, named it, demonstrated a correction, and made the apprentice try again. AI can multiply that feedback if we use it correctly. It can become a tireless opponent, a patient explainer, a library, a critic, a drill designer, and a verifier.

But it cannot want your growth for you.

That remains the human part.

This is where the phrase "use AI to become stronger, not lazier" becomes concrete. Stronger does not mean doing everything manually. Stronger means you can delegate more without losing the ability to judge. Stronger means the system around you gets faster while the person inside the system becomes clearer. Stronger means every use of AI leaves behind a trace of improved taste, sharper questions, cleaner standards, or better tests.

The future will not belong to people who proudly refuse AI. It will also not belong to people who let AI complete their apprenticeship on their behalf.

It will belong to people who know which difficulty to remove and which difficulty to preserve.

Remove the difficulty that only wastes attention.

Preserve the difficulty that builds judgment.

Then let the machine carry the weight. Just do not let it steal the training.
