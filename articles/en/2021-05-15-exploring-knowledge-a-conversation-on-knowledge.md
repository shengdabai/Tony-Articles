> The name Richard Feynman gave rise to a technique known as the Feynman Technique (which is also a psychological model). It is a direct process that allows people to gain a deep understanding of any concept. This is a targeted method of learning.

Here are the four steps of the Feynman Technique:

Step 1: Identify the concept. Take a piece of paper and write the name of the concept you want to learn at the top of the page.

Step 2: Explain in plain language. Now, start explaining the concept in your own words, as if you were teaching someone else. The most important thing to remember here is not to use any technical terms you've already read in your learning material. You must explain the concept in plain, easy-to-understand language. When many people are unclear about a concept, they often use jargon or terminology to explain it. This is because using jargon can mask our misunderstanding of others. Therefore, the method should be to use plain language, as if you were teaching this concept to a 70-year-old grandmother who knows nothing about it.

Step 3: Check the gap. In this step, you need to honestly review your explanation. Here, you will identify areas where you don't fully understand or where your explanation is unclear. Here, you must stop and return to your learning material or your previous notes on the concept.

Step 4: Reorganize and summarize. Once you clearly understand the missing concept, you need to explain it again in plain language. If your explanation still contains some jargon, subject terms, or complex language, challenge yourself to rewrite those parts in a simpler way. Ensure that your explanation is understandable to someone who doesn't have the knowledge you assume they already have.

> Many philosophers in the history of thought have tried to distinguish "knowledge," with the most famous being Hegel's distinction between "familiar knowledge" and "true knowledge." Familiar knowledge: something you're familiar with—"Who is that person? Do you know them?" "Yes, I know them well!" However, familiar knowledge is often not true knowledge. Using the analogy of interpersonal relationships, your relationship with another person can basically be summarized as either "familiar" or "not familiar," and the difference between these two is very significant. Or, if we use different colors to show the closeness of the relationship, the color difference is very large. The mastery of a piece of knowledge may seem similar at first glance, but the difference is actually very significant. Hegel simply called the kind of knowledge we usually think we know but actually don't "familiar knowledge," and the real knowledge he called "true knowledge."

> Bacon's theory—why is the spread of science so difficult?

The first illusion is the "race illusion," which refers to the innate defects in human cognition. Specifically, it means that we all have a preference for concrete expressions when facing unknown problems. For example, compared to text, we prefer images; compared to professional terminology, we prefer plain language; compared to abstract descriptions, we prefer personification. When doing science communication, we usually use some plain and vivid expressions, but using these methods also brings some problems.

For example, there is a very famous book called *The Selfish Gene*, written by Richard Dawkins, a renowned evolutionary biologist from the UK. He chose this title to make the concept more vivid, but this title easily leads people to understand the gene, which originally has no emotion, as an emotional substance. As a result, Dawkins had to re-explain in the beginning of the book what he meant by the word "selfish."

Moreover, besides the strategy of "selfishness," many organisms in nature have adopted strategies of "mutual benefit" or "altruism." If you tell people that these strategies are also forms of selfishness, just more advanced or more hidden, it might make people feel more complicated. Whereas using neutral, academic-sounding terms like "self-preservation tendency" might make people less interested in reading. This is the difficulty of rigorous science communication. The places where misunderstandings are easy to occur are precisely where pseudoscience can take advantage.

The second illusion is called the "cage illusion." It means that each person's talents, temperaments, and education are different, so their perspectives and depth of understanding are also different, which is what people often say: "different fields are like different mountains." This directly leads to a decline in people's interest in science communication and an increase in the difficulty of entry. Most people don't even have the interest to pay attention to fields they don't understand, let alone actively discern truth from falsehood. At the same time, it's also a challenge to make science communication content suitable for the tastes of most people.

The third illusion is called the "market illusion." It means that when people communicate in daily life, they talk as if they're in a market, using "natural language" that lacks precise definitions. This illusion is somewhat similar to the first one. When doing science communication, we usually express complex knowledge in plain language that everyone understands, but in this process, information accuracy can be lost, leaving room for pseudoscience.

The fourth illusion is called the "theater illusion." It means that people are easily misled by "dogmatism" in thinking, just like sitting in a theater watching a play, where the performance seems well-organized, but in reality, it's carefully "designed." If people's minds are filled with such "dogma," their thinking will be limited. People tend to believe knowledge that aligns with their previous common sense rather than the correct knowledge. This belongs to a type of stereotype. Moreover, the author emphasizes that stereotypes are not necessarily actively "designed," they might also be the result of people trapping themselves in them.

## An Example

Take the subject division in high school, for instance. It tends to lead people to view academic disciplines in a one-sided way, making it extremely difficult to popularize certain subjects. For example, some people might think that religious studies is just "chanting scriptures every day," and that literature majors are just people who read two more literary books. This not only makes one's knowledge structure incomplete, but also makes people look at the identity of science popularizers through tinted glasses, creating difficulties in the process of science popularization.

> First-hand knowledge is the primary foundation for wise living. To a large extent, the knowledge gained from books is second-hand information, and therefore never holds the immediate practical significance that first-hand knowledge does. Our goal is to see life's immediate events as examples of our general thinking. The world of erudition often provides only a small amount of second-hand knowledge, used to illustrate ideas derived from other second-hand knowledge. This second-hand nature of erudition is the secret to its mediocrity. It is mediocre because it has never been tested by facts. Francis Bacon's most important influence was not in expressing any unique theory of inductive reasoning, but in leading the rebellion against second-hand information. A unique advantage of scientific education is that it builds thought on the observation of first-hand knowledge; correspondingly, the advantage of technical education is that it follows our deep-seated natural instinct: to convert thought into manual skills, and manual activity into thought. Excerpted from *The Aim of Education* by Alfred North Whitehead, an English author.

First-hand knowledge is some fresh thinking that is currently being studied, thought about, and verified. It might be in the minds of these researchers or in their notebooks, not yet fully processed. It's fresh and hot. Other thoughts are systematized and become published papers in professional journals. Second-hand knowledge is high-quality, accurately transmitted information, such as Wikipedia, some books that explain the underlying logic, and some neutral industry reports. There are also systematically organized, high-quality textbooks and certification trainings in various industries. Third-hand knowledge is bestsellers. These texts have been rewritten into language that the public can understand and easily spread, but because they require low cognitive resources to understand, they include a lot of cases, stories, and also add some imprecise concepts. Fourth-hand knowledge is what you often see—based on these bestsellers and theories, most people write a lot of motivational advice (motivational advice for the soul) based on their personal experiences, adding too many personal stories or emotional factors. They present a point of view but pour in countless strong emotional elements. Third- and fourth-hand knowledge are not wrong, but they should be pursued.
