# Turn Off AI and See What You Can Still Do

> 发布日期:2026-09-26 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-26-关掉%20AI，看看自己会不会.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-26-the-test-after-ai-is-gone.md)

---

Suppose you want to understand a passage in English. You paste it into an AI chat, get a fluent translation in seconds, and ask for explanations of the unfamiliar words. Today's task is done. But tomorrow, when you meet a passage phrased a little differently, could you close the chat and tell which parts you understood and which parts you merely trusted the translation to get right?

A question in a recent summary of a recorded discussion about AI and learning has stayed with me: if a tool can translate and write code for us, why learn languages or programming? The summary records one speaker's view. It is not a study of learning outcomes, and it cannot settle the question for me. I want to ask something narrower: when we say AI helped us learn, what would count as evidence?

My answer is that learning should be checked after the help briefly leaves the room. I am not asking anyone to do all their work alone from now on. I want to know whether I can make a judgment I could not previously make on a new but related problem. A person and a tool can deliver a piece of work together. Growth in the person's ability needs its own receipt.

## A finished task and a learned skill need different receipts

Imagine two people spending an afternoon on the same kind of task. One asks AI to draft an English email, checks the recipient and the facts, and sends it. The other also gets AI help, but writes a first version, studies why it was changed, then writes a different email without prompts. Both finish their work. Only the second has created an opportunity to test learning. These are hypothetical people; their stories do not establish that the second person necessarily learned more.

If delivery is the only goal, the first person's approach may be better. An email exists to convey a message accurately. Nobody owes every email a lesson plan. But if the goal is to read more material independently or judge phrasing for yourself later, the finished email tells you too little. A fluent sentence from AI shows that the tool produced a fluent sentence this time. It does not show that you can produce or assess one next time.

That is why I am wary of treating time spent with AI as a measure of progress. You can spend hours asking follow-up questions and learn little. You can accept an answer quickly and still have exercised judgment. What matters is which actions you performed that a later task could test. Did you make a prediction before looking? Did you notice a discrepancy? Did that discrepancy change what you did on the next attempt?

This sounds like an exam, but it can be small and ordinary. If a tool wrote a spreadsheet formula for you, can you recognize an implausible result when the data changes tomorrow? If AI helped you read a specialist article, can you close the page and explain to a newcomer what problem the author tackled and how far the evidence reaches? Such checks do not require you to replicate the tool. They identify the piece of ability you may have gained.

I call this an off-switch question: after a task done with help, remove that help for a moment and try a related, different problem. It must be related so that the earlier learning has somewhere to go, and different so that repeating the answer will not be enough. Turning AI off is a test condition, not a manifesto about how everyone should work.

## How help can turn practice into watching

Imagine cooking dinner with someone who always tastes the food for you and tells you exactly how much salt to add. Dinner may be excellent. Yet if you never taste it first and make your own guess, you may still be unable to season the next meal alone. To learn that judgment, you need chances to taste, predict, and revise. The cooking example illustrates how the learner can lose a useful action. It is not evidence about the effects of AI on learning.

For more direct evidence, [a study by Bastani and colleagues in *PNAS*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) examined mathematics practice at one high school in Turkey. Classes were assigned different conditions: an AI tutor resembling a standard chat interface, another tutor supplied with teacher-written answers and common mistakes and instructed to give hints first, or a comparison condition without AI. Both AI groups did better during assisted practice. When students then took similar closed-book problems without the tool, the standard-chat group did worse than the comparison group. The hint-based tutor group was not statistically distinguishable from that group on the unassisted exam.

This does not mean AI inevitably makes people less capable. It does not mean that asking for hints is enough to raise achievement. The experiment involved a particular school, mathematics, and the tools available at the time; it measured short-term independent performance. The hint-based design largely avoided the negative result but did not show a positive gain on the unaided exam. That less flattering detail matters. A better score while help is present and greater ability after help is removed are separate outcomes.

After examining the conversations, the researchers proposed a mechanism: the ordinary chat interface made it easier to ask for complete answers, leaving fewer opportunities for students to try problems themselves. Interaction records and further analysis support that explanation, but they do not rule out every alternative. The effect cannot simply be assigned to adult work. What the study does give me is a useful question: am I asking AI to help me past an obstacle, or to cross the very step I meant to practice for me?

A second view comes from [a survey of knowledge workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf). Its researchers collected 936 reported work examples from 319 people who used generative AI. The more confident participants were that AI could do a particular task, the less likely they were to report exercising critical thinking on that task. Their descriptions of critical thinking also shifted toward checking information, integrating responses, and overseeing the work. These are self-reports and correlations. They do not establish that AI eroded anyone's ability, and they give us no basis for calling individual users lazy.

Still, the shift is worth examining. As producing an answer becomes cheaper, being able to check it becomes more important. Someone who knows almost nothing about a field may be willing to review an output carefully and still miss its central error. Asking the tool for a more polished explanation may not fill that gap. To develop judgment, you also have to encounter the underlying material, attempt small problems yourself, and check answers against something beyond the tool that produced them.

## The strongest objection: why do it yourself at all?

There is a good objection. If the tool is readily available, why take an off-switch test? We do not ask everyone to repair an elevator or build a camera before using one. Spending the saved time on something more important may be wiser than practicing a skill that a reliable system can perform. I agree. This objection sets the limits of my argument.

If all you need is a routine email sent once, you can verify its content, and any error is easy to correct, there is no need to turn it into language training. For a specialized matter that you should not decide alone, a qualified professional may be the right person to take responsibility. A few self-tests would give you false confidence if you treated them as a professional credential. The off-switch question is not one.

Some skills, though, return repeatedly and shape whether you can spot the tool's mistakes. You regularly read material in another language. You often maintain a program you depend on. Or you intend to decide whether an analysis is sound enough to act upon. In those cases, some independent ability is valuable. It lets you take a few steps on your own, locate where you need help, and recognize when you ought to pause and verify.

So my question is not whether I could live without AI forever. It is this: will I be responsible for judging the consequences of this kind of task again? If not, and a better qualified person can verify the result, I may need very little depth. If I will be responsible, I should preserve at least one attempt that does not lean on the original answer. The consequences and recurrence of the task should determine how much I learn, not whether the skill sounds prestigious.

This also keeps independence from becoming a badge of virtue. Someone may work alone in a familiar domain and need collaboration in a new one. Someone else may use a tool and still judge its output responsibly. The real distinction is whether this instance of help delivered a result, and whether it also built an ability available for the next task. Both benefits are real. The task should decide which one you seek.

## Make help into practice that remains with you

I would begin with a small change: write down your own answer before looking at the tool's. A partial answer is enough, provided you say what led you to it. After seeing the response, do more than copy the corrected version. Find the largest gap between the two and ask what made it: a missing fact, a mistaken relationship, or a standard you had never stated clearly? Then aim the next attempt at that gap.

[Ericsson and Harwell's paper on practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6824411/) cautions against equating many repetitions with effective training. They emphasize a clear goal for improvement, timely feedback, and revisions to the next attempt. They also distinguish deliberate practice in the strict sense, guided individually by a qualified teacher, from purposeful practice organized around a goal you set yourself. The AI check I am proposing is, at most, a design for purposeful practice. Using a tool does not magically supply the individualized instruction of an expert teacher.

Here is one way to try it with English reading. Read a short paragraph and write down its main claim and the words you are unsure about, without worrying about elegant prose. Ask AI to explain the sentences you could not follow, then return to the original and check it, especially the negations, conditions, and exceptions. Finally, find a new paragraph on the same topic. Before opening AI, mark its claim and qualifiers yourself. If you cannot yet do that, you have identified the next small step to practice. You have not proved yourself incapable of learning English.

Programming offers a similar opportunity. AI can write a small script to process a spreadsheet, and you can use it to finish today's job. If you also want to maintain the script later, take another set of data whose structure differs slightly. Predict the output before running the code and inspect any mismatch. You can ask the tool to identify a possible error while trying to propose your own fix first. The point is not to memorize every piece of syntax. It is to become better at predicting what the program will do to a given input and which results deserve suspicion.

Why use a new problem? [Experiments by van Eersel and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5183614/) examined whether knowledge learned from texts could be applied to questions in different domains. Participants who tried to recall information during practice and received feedback later did better on new questions than those who only reread. But when rereaders also received focused exposure to the key information, that advantage shrank. The benefit cannot all be assigned to the act of recall; feedback directing attention to what matters also played a part. The materials and participants were specific, so the experiment cannot promise the same size of benefit for every AI-based exercise.

That finding gives me a concrete way to improve an off-switch question. It should test more than whether I remember an answer; the feedback should identify what actually determined the outcome. In English, a single turn of phrase may reverse the author's position. In code, an empty value may cause a calculation to fail. Finding that key difference and using it on a related new task tells me more than becoming familiar with a complete answer. And if the new task is too distant, failure may show that I asked for too much transfer, rather than that I learned nothing.

## Leave yourself an off-switch question today

I would try a short sequence. Choose a task you already need to do and are likely to meet again. State the ability you want to keep: noticing a qualification in English, say, or detecting a suspect spreadsheet result. Complete today's work with AI as usual while saving your initial judgment and the tool's changes. Then take a related new example, close the original conversation, try it on your own, and note where you get stuck. The order matters because it lets you see what the help actually left behind.

The standard need not be a perfect score. I care more about three signals: can I identify the new problem's decisive condition; can I make a reasoned prediction before seeing an answer; and, when I am wrong, can I say what to change on the next attempt? If I can do none of these, I should make the exercise smaller rather than ask AI for an even fuller, prettier finished product. If I can do them, I have made some progress on this kind of related problem. I am still a long way from being qualified to handle high-stakes work alone.

The off-switch question also forces me to name a learning goal I can act on. Learning AI, English, or programming is dauntingly broad. Independently recognizing an exception in a passage, or noticing when a script treats a blank cell as zero, gives me a boundary for today's practice. A small boundary does not mean a small ambition. Long-term ability often grows from actions you can test repeatedly.

Back to the English passage at the start. The translation is still useful, and AI can stay in my workflow. After reading it, though, I want to find a similar passage, try it myself first, mark what I am unsure of, and then reopen the tool to check. That may not make me a fast reader immediately. It will help me answer a more honest question than how quickly I finished: when the next passage arrives, who has learned to read it?
