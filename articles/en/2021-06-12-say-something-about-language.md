> What Is Language?

This game might be familiar to many of you: *Animal Crossing: New Horizons*, abbreviated as *Animal Crossing* or *Animal Crossing*. During the pandemic, many people stayed home and played this game. A lot of players found the most interesting part of the game to be the language used by the characters, called *Animalese* (nekomimi speech). Those who have played the game often feel that this language has a familiar ring to it. This isn’t just random voice design—it actually has meaning. Not only do Chinese players feel this way, but players from all over the world also feel that the *Animalese* in *Animal Crossing* sounds like their own local language.

*Animal Crossing* has different versions of *Animalese* depending on the language version of the game. In the Japanese version, the characters sound like they’re speaking Japanese; in the English version, they sound like they’re speaking English. How does the game achieve this? A game website called *GCORES* (Ji He Wang) has a radio show called *“So HeavySo Light”* (This Heavy, This Light), and the host analyzed the design philosophy behind *Animalese*. His analysis goes like this:

> First, what we hear as *Animalese* is not just Chinese played at double speed. Many netizens have stretched the audio and found that even when played very slowly, *Animalese* is still meaningless sound, with no existing words in it. So how does *Animalese* sound like a language? The radio host made an assumption: *Animalese* actually still has some kind of correspondence with the languages we speak in daily life. Take Chinese, for example. The designers of *Animal Crossing* might have broken down each Chinese character, leaving only the very short, abrupt sound of each character. For instance, the character “好” (good) might be reduced to just “h,” a short “呵” (huh). Then, through a series of processing, it becomes what we call *Animalese*. However, because *Animalese* still retains some features of Chinese, it sounds familiar to you. It’s like when you hear someone speak and notice their use of child tones and the way words stick together, and they often say “您” (you), you might think they’re speaking Beijing dialect.

From the first generation of the game, players have had to read a lot of text. The amount of text is often more than that of a long novel. To keep players reading, the game creates immersion, such as by reading out the text through voice. But if they used real human voice recordings, a 10-minute recording would fill up the game cartridge. So, under those limitations, *Animal Crossing* developed the ability to create *Animalese*. Moreover, to distinguish the characters, they assigned different speech patterns and tones to different animals, and even gave each character unique catchphrases. In this way, all the small animals in the game have their own unique styles.

> Although you don’t understand what the animals are saying, they are actually speaking your language. This also makes you a member of the community.

The designers of *Animal Crossing* are particularly gentle, carefully crafting such small details to make their game a utopia where every player can find a sense of belonging. So how does this relate to the language I want to talk about today? We know that language and writing help people express themselves, communicate, collaborate, and also preserve the new knowledge created in the process. But language also has unavoidable problems: it is vague, inaccurate, and can be deceptive.
