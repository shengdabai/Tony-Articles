Tony's English Reading Experience

First Stage: During my student days, our English reading mainly included but was not limited to textbooks. I must admit that apart from English textbooks and various workbooks, I hardly read any extracurricular materials. Endless memorization of vocabulary, phrases, and texts made me once think my English was pretty good, since the tests were all things I had memorized. For words I didn't know, I would put Chinese notes in parentheses. What about unfamiliar words? Guess, or copy them a few times after the teacher explained. Compared to today's English teaching, many schools, especially private international schools, place great emphasis on extracurricular English reading. I have students whose reading abilities far surpass mine. Some read English biographies in elementary school, which makes me feel inadequate. In university, I didn't intentionally study English, but due to my major, I read some professional English texts, which were actually quite simple—just memorize the professional vocabulary. Throughout my student years, strictly speaking, I did only intensive reading, limited to the intensive reading practiced in class. There was no extracurricular or extensive reading.

Second Stage: After becoming an English teacher, I still relied on my initial English foundation at first, but soon found it insufficient. With a lot of classes to teach and few opportunities to observe other teachers, I quickly hit a bottleneck if I stayed there. Reading became the fastest way for me to learn. By reading English teaching books, I could break down each teaching section, find summaries from experts, and explore various teaching methods and activity designs. The reading efficiency was high, and I didn't need to read everything. For example, we think English vocabulary is easy to teach, right? The basics are three aspects: M-Meaning (meaning), P-Pronunciation (pronunciation), F-Form (form), plus parts of speech, tense changes, usage, common collocations, and related contexts. Each aspect has various activities, so just find the content that matches your needs. Do students really need to know these professional terms? Usually not, but during lesson preparation andcurriculum development (teaching research), we need to communicate on this basis. Background knowledge related to games, psychology, and pedagogy in teaching also requires reading. Still, the same point: read what you need.

This year, after the Spring Festival, I started my online postgraduate life, and English reading became a daily necessity. My major is Educational Leadership. Those familiar with leadership know it doesn't belong to any specific field. The content involves psychology, management, sociology, and more, plus education-related topics. The research starts from the UK and explores education systems in various countries. The daily reading volume is quite large. Additionally, by following the recommendations of Professor Xiaolai and Liuda, I read *Beyond Feelings* and *The Most Important Thing: Uncommon Sense for the Thoughtful Investor*. I especially thank the Flight Captain for letting me join the class's English intensive course. Now it's upgraded to a class-intensive course. My first shared book, *Leadership Pipeline*, has been updated and recently posted on the class's official account. I'm currently reading three more books recommended by the Flight Captain. Through reading, I distill the essence, combine my own thoughts, and share it with everyone. Teaching is the best way to learn, and I've benefited a lot from it. I think this year is my breakout period for English reading, and the previous years were a period of silent preparation.

After sharing my reading experience, let's look at the minimum essential knowledge needed for English reading. The most crucial point is that even if you forget everything else, just remember this: read, open a book and read it seriously! Integrate reading into our lives, and everything becomes much easier. Chinese works from modern times are far from comparable to Western works. After all, world-class scholarship is mostly in English, so English reading is our top priority. A lot of reading naturally leads to a sufficient reading speed. If you really want to read faster, read with questions in mind. Follow the questions like a map, and it's not strange to read dozens of books in a day. This is what Professor Wang Dingding calls broadband reading. Everyone reads to solve their own problems. Reading with purpose is highly efficient, and reading with questions and thinking is even better.
