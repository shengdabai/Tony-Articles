# Don't Let the Outcome Rewrite Your Decision

> 发布日期:2026-10-07 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-07-别让结果替你改写当初的判断.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-07-dont-let-outcomes-rewrite-decisions.md)

---

Imagine deciding on a weekend to turn your reading notes into a short public guide. You could put out a version that people can use, or keep researching until every corner feels complete. You choose to publish. Someone tells you it helped. When you look back, it is tempting to write, “Publishing early was the right call.” But what has that sentence established? More time spent checking sources might have made the guide better. And if nobody had seen it, publishing early might still have been a reasonable decision.

A course summary about reviewing projects recommends starting with surprises, then asking why they happened and where a lesson stops applying. It also warns against turning one success into a timeless rule. This was a summary of someone else's course, not an account of something I did. It raises a further question: can we keep our judgment at the time separate from the outcome that came later? My view is that reusable experience includes what we expected, which option we passed over, and what evidence would have changed our minds.

## How a good outcome flatters an old decision

Once the outcome is visible, it pulls the whole story toward itself. If readers use the guide, you may think you saw their need clearly from the start. If no one uses it, you may decide you were careless. Yet feedback after publication cannot, by itself, prove the reasoning that preceded it. It tells you what happened under one set of conditions. Finding out which part of your choice mattered takes more work.

Praise may reflect a clear guide. It may also reflect an existing audience, or a topic that happened to arrive at the right moment. Silence may mean the work needs improvement, or that people could not find it. I am not suggesting excuses for a poor result. I am protecting a distinction: judge the decision against the information and options available then; judge the result against what actually happened later. Combine both into a neat success story, and you may copy the wrong lesson into a different situation.

It is like photographing a finished meal while forgetting how hot the pan was. A good picture shows what reached the table. It does not tell you whether the heat, the ingredients, or an extra minute of cooking made the difference. The analogy stops there. A project is not the same dish cooked again under identical conditions. A process record helps you see which conditions might change the conclusion; it is not a guaranteed recipe.

In their [2009 paper on expert intuition](https://www.pef.uni-lj.si/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Kahneman_Klein_2009_Conditions-for-Intuitive-Expertise_A-Failure-to-disagree_Am_Psi.pdf), psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein identify two requirements for learning sound intuitive judgments: the environment must offer sufficiently reliable cues, and the person must have opportunities to learn those cues from experience. They also caution that feeling certain is an unreliable guide to being right. That gives a review a plain job. Ask whether there is a pattern to learn here, and whether the feedback is clear enough to reveal it. Without those conditions, repeated experience can increase confidence without improving judgment.

Suppose you publish similar guides regularly and can see who finishes them, where readers get stuck, and whether they can act afterward. You may gradually learn which explanations help. If the topic, audience, and route to readers change every time, and all you receive are scattered likes, extracting a writing formula is risky. Applause alone does not determine the value of the earlier decision. Your ability to learn from it depends on knowing what else might have produced the applause.

Another possible omission is a folder that holds only the work you finished and published. Abandoned attempts never make it into the record. Later, the folder seems full of examples showing that a certain approach works, while you have already screened out its failures. My inference is that confidence built this way may come from which examples were kept. I suggest making room in a review for an attempt that missed its expected result, with its conditions and outcome recorded. That is a way to avoid an incomplete record, not proof that keeping more records improves judgment.

## Keep the prediction you made before acting

If you write a review only after the fact, I think it is easy to explain your earlier choice in light of the result. Here is a record keeping method I propose; the studies cited here have not tested it. Before acting, write a few lines: whose problem am I trying to solve, why this approach, what first signal do I expect to see, and which response would make me doubt my judgment? The note can be rough. Its job is to preserve a version you can check against what happened later.

Write down the option you rejected, too. You are not claiming it would have been better. You are making sure you remember that you had a choice. In the guide example, another option might be to ask a small group to try reading it first. If people later say a crucial step is hard to follow, you can ask whether a trial reading would have exposed that problem sooner. You cannot return to that weekend and run a controlled comparison. You can still change what you try next time.

Steve Blank recounts a product decision in [an essay about customer development](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/). He gathered prospective customers' preferences for two ways a chip could arrange data, and the company implemented both. The engineering and software teams faced different costs from that choice. His later point is that collecting requested features does not replace testing which customer problem a product solves and whether people would use it. This is his retrospective account, not proof that every product should choose only one option. The lesson is to name the hypothesis you are testing before a feature list makes the decision for you.

Keeping the road not taken also shows what you were optimizing. Publishing first could bring reader feedback sooner. Testing with a few people first could reduce the cost of public revisions. Both have a price. If the record says only “people liked it,” you lose sight of what speed bought you and what a slower route might have protected. Experience should preserve a tradeoff for your future self to examine, not compress a victory into a slogan.

I think of this record as a map with blank areas. Before acting, you draw the routes you think are possible. Afterward, you mark the terrain you actually crossed. The untraveled route remains labeled “unknown.” The map tells you where to explore next; it does not let you paint a cliff on a road you never walked. This analogy has a limit as well. Real work changes its surroundings. Once people see your guide, their needs may shift, so the map needs redrawing.

## Visible progress may hide the ability you meant to build

A review also has to ask what it is counting. Using AI to produce a guide in a day is faster output. Being able, later on, to check a source yourself and explain why the guide has its particular structure is a different outcome. Put delivery speed and personal learning on one scorecard, and a polished artifact can conceal a missing skill. I am not arguing that every task must be done from scratch. I am asking us to decide whether this task is primarily a delivery job or a chance to practice.

A [high school mathematics experiment published in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) measured performance during practice separately from performance without a tool. At one school in Turkey, students had different forms of AI assistance during practice and then worked on their own in an exam. Students without AI formed the control group: the group used for comparison. The original paper reports differences between groups in practice and on the independent exam, but its page says the paper has been corrected and the supplied packet does not contain the correction. I therefore do not treat those differences as verified findings here or use them to predict how adults learn to write. The design still raises a useful question: if you want to see whether you learned a skill, when should you try the task on your own?

An [author abstract of a study in customer support work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11771) reports that, using data from 5,172 customer support workers, access to a generative AI assistant raised productivity, measured as issues resolved per hour, by an average of 15%. Less experienced workers gained more; the most experienced workers had small gains in speed and small declines in work quality. These are the results stated in the v2 abstract, revised on November 6, 2024; I do not use the abstract to infer long term learning. The support study examines work performance. The math paper's design separates assisted practice from an independent exam. Different tasks, people, and measures cannot honestly be collapsed into “AI makes us stronger” or “AI makes us lazy.”

I suggest checking two goals separately in a personal review. If the goal is to deliver a dependable guide on time, check whether a reader can use it and whether the key facts are correct. If the goal also includes growing judgment, reserve a small step without AI: a few days later, take one claim and explain its source, its limits, and a possible counterexample yourself. This is my proposed exercise, not a method the math experiment verified for adult writing. It gives an ability that a tool may hide a chance to be checked.

## The strongest objection: must every small choice become a file?

One fair objection is that life is busy enough. Recording predictions, alternatives, and counterexamples for a short guide could take longer than writing it. Many choices carry little risk; if they go wrong, you can adjust. I agree. If a task happens once, the cost of a mistake is small, and the feedback arrives immediately, a detailed decision record probably has little value. Turning review into paperwork can redirect energy from making something useful toward proving that you were diligent.

In a [2013 essay on early startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes founders personally recruiting users and helping them through their first experience. His lesson is to get something into people's hands, watch how they use it, and adjust. The essay offers firsthand examples, not a measured success rate for all small businesses. It reminds me that a record is no substitute for contact with the world. If you do not yet have one person willing to try the guide, ten pages of decision notes will not find that reader for you.

So I would not document every move. The choices worth recording are recurring ones whose results are easy to misread and whose apparent lessons you are likely to reuse. Perhaps you repeatedly hesitate between publishing early and filling every gap first. Perhaps one success will tempt you to invest more time or money next time. Perhaps a failure will make you say, “I knew it.” In those cases, a short statement made beforehand is more useful than a beautifully written explanation produced afterward.

There is a harder objection: the road you did not take has no observed result. How can you know where it would have led? You cannot. Recording an alternative cannot show what happened on the path you did not take. Nor can you assume that one event caused another just because they appeared together. For example, a guide may get more clicks after you publish it early, but that alone cannot show that timing caused the increase; the way people found it may also have changed. A record keeps that uncertainty visible and may help you design a smaller test next time. Readers who volunteer to test a guide may already be unusually interested and may not represent other readers. If a platform changes how it recommends work, last time's clicks no longer describe the same conditions. Admitting these gaps is more useful than awarding yourself a certificate for having been right.

## Try a small review that reality can overturn

If you are about to do a small task that will come around again, you could try this small review I propose; it is not a proven general method. Leave the full knowledge base template closed. On a sheet of paper, name the action you plan to take and the result you hope to improve. Write one alternative you seriously considered but did not choose. Then write the first observable signal you expect and one signal that would make you admit, “I may have this wrong.” Set a time to look back. Only then add what happened and which competing explanations remain open. The order matters: the prediction must come before the outcome.

Suppose you plan a short guide to help people start reading. You might predict that a reader will know which book or page to begin with. Another approach would be to ask someone to try a page before you write the guide. If readers later say they like the guide but cannot name a next step, that is a useful counterexample: being pleasant to read and being usable are different. Next time you might watch where someone stops during the attempt, then decide what to write. This is a hypothetical feedback scene, not an experiment I have already run. Silence alone would not prove the guide failed.

You could ask AI to do one restrained job: read the decision note, identify an unstated condition, offer a competing explanation, and flag what is still a guess. You still decide whether to publish or change direction. AI can make the alternative easier to see, but it cannot produce the result of the path you did not take. When real feedback arrives, it can help organize the notes. Do not let it quietly rewrite your original prediction into a story of foresight.

Return to that weekend guide. If someone found it helpful, enjoy that. If someone found a problem, listen carefully. What would make me willing to do the work again is not stamping my first choice “correct.” It is being able to say more clearly what I expected, how the world responded, and what I still do not know. Leave that blank area in the record. It gives the next attempt somewhere to begin learning.
