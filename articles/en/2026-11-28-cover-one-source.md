# Cover One Source. Would You Still Go Ahead?

> 发布日期:2026-11-28 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-28-%E7%9B%96%E4%BD%8F%E4%B8%80%E4%BB%BD%E6%9D%90%E6%96%99%EF%BC%8C%E4%BD%A0%E8%BF%98%E4%BC%9A%E7%85%A7%E8%BF%99%E4%B8%AA%E5%86%B3%E5%AE%9A%E5%81%9A%E5%90%97%EF%BC%9F.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-28-cover-one-source.md)

You finish two articles and fill a page with notes. The next day, when you actually have to decide what to do, you stick with your original plan. Here's what I'd like to know: if you did change your mind, could you point to the source that changed it? Did the whole decision change, or just one condition? A full page of notes won't tell you that on its own.

I'd rather keep two versions on paper. Before I read, I write down what I plan to do. Afterward, I write down the new plan. Then I cover up one source. Would I still go ahead with the plan on what's left? I don't need to score the sources. I want to see exactly which words in the new plan I can no longer stand behind. If nothing has to change, I note which other source still supports it.

## Write down what you need to decide

Take a shift schedule. Say you're due to open the shop on Friday and normally stay to run the counter. You want to book a Friday morning appointment. Before you pick a time on your phone, you jot down your current plan: if someone covers the early shift, I'll go straight to the appointment and skip the shop. There are really two jobs in that sentence, opening the shop and running the counter. At first you've treated them as one.

You open the group chat. The previews for two notices say the early shift is covered. Friday's notice is about the counter. A coworker will take over. Saturday's notice covers the Saturday counter, but it has a handover note at the end: the person opening on Friday hasn't changed. That's you. The two previews make it look as if your morning is free. Once you open them, you find out you're only free after opening up.

Where did you go wrong? Friday's notice tells you who runs the counter on Friday. The first line of Saturday's notice tells you who runs it on Saturday. Those lines confirm the counter is covered. They don’t establish that anyone will open the shop. It's the last line of the Saturday notice that changes your plan. It applies to Friday, even though you have to look in Saturday's notice to find it.

Change the plan on paper: I'll open the shop on Friday, wait for my coworker to take over the counter, then go to the appointment. Cover the Friday notice and you still know you have to open up, but you can't say when you'll be free to leave. Cover the Saturday notice instead and you still know someone will cover the counter, but that's not enough to go straight to the appointment. At that point you don't need a third summary. You need to ask: am I still opening on Friday?

If both notices said you're still the one opening on Friday, covering the Saturday notice wouldn't change that part of the plan. You'd still have a notice that answers the question. The second one is useful as a cross-check. There's no need to squeeze a new conclusion out of it. But if neither notice says who's opening, leave the question open. Not finding the arrangement isn't the same as finding out that no one is opening.

You could copy out both notices in full and still just need to know what time you can leave. Writing down your plan is more useful than copying the notices again because it shows you what you haven't worked out.

When I read a study, I first look for the score I need. The university researchers compared writing before answering with answering right away by looking at both groups’ scores on inference questions. For example, could students use clues to work out something the text did not state? They also scored the short pieces the writing group turned in. The sixth-grade history study reported scores for both answers and writing. The result I cite from the Dutch tenth-grade study comes from scoring the students’ writing. Keep those scores separate before asking what the students got better at doing. [University study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y) · [Sixth-grade study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625) · [Tenth-grade study](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1032/917)

## What question does each study answer?

Take a reading exercise. Say I wanted a class to read a set of texts and then do an exercise. I'd start by asking two things. Should everyone write a short piece before answering questions? If they are going to write, should I show them how first? I can put both questions on the same sheet of paper. If I treat them as one, I might go looking for the wrong evidence.

Castells, Minguela, and Nadal randomly assigned 155 first-year psychology students at a Spanish public university to different conditions. The students all read three texts about intelligence. Some went straight to the questions. Others wrote a synthesis before answering the same questions. A synthesis draws key points on one topic from several texts into a single short piece and explains how those points fit together. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y)

The researchers scored students on an inferential comprehension test. Questions about a single text might ask them to use clues to work out something unstated, bring together information scattered through that text, or do both. Questions that went beyond one text might ask them to connect information across texts, combine something they'd read with what they already knew, or do both. While answering, they couldn't consult the readings, notes, or syntheses. The writing group received no specific instruction in synthesis writing, and this test gave too little evidence to establish a score advantage for that group. I'd leave the decision to add a writing task open until I knew what I wanted the exercise to accomplish. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y)

In a study published in 2015, Martínez, Mateos, Martín, and Rijlaarsdam compared 62 sixth graders in four classes at two schools. Both groups read the same history materials and completed synthesis assignments. The conventional instruction group did textbook activities and then wrote individually. The other group watched a demonstration, wrote together, and went on to practice individually with instructor guidance and written prompts. [Original history study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)

On the history test after instruction, the latter group scored higher on questions that required students to draw inferences or bring together scattered information. Their final syntheses received higher quality ratings too. The groups had different instructors, and the researchers didn't measure the separate contributions of demonstration, collaborative writing, and prompts. If I borrowed one activity from those lessons, I'd point learners to two pieces of information to connect and give them time to check the originals. That's a way I'd like to teach. The study assessed the whole arrangement. [Original history study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)

Here's where I'd be most tempted to write a few words too many: synthesis writing only works if you teach it. That reads as if researchers had split the same students into two groups, taught one and not the other, then given them the same task. The first study compared undergraduates who wrote with undergraduates who didn't. The second compared two ways of teaching sixth graders who all wrote. The students and the reading material changed too. I'd keep those two questions separate on my lesson plan, even if I have to answer both in the same class. [University study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y) · [Sixth-grade study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)

Now make the same edits you made with the shift notices. Take away the university study and you have to drop the comparison about whether to add writing. You still have a comparison of how to teach when both groups write. Take away the sixth-grade study and you have to drop the comparison between teaching arrangements. You still have a comparison of whether to add a writing task. Put both studies back and I can mark what's still unknown, such as what would happen if just one kind of guidance changed. Keeping that question open is already enough to stop me from turning one classroom activity into something everyone ought to do. [University study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y) · [Sixth-grade study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)

The sixth-grade history test itself offers a good example. One question asked whether the steam engine affected both industry and transport. One text dealt with industry and another with transport. If you use those two texts to write an answer, taking either away means withdrawing the corresponding part of your judgment. Together they can answer whether both were affected. If the question changes to which was affected more, you'll need material that lets you compare the size of the effects. [Example from the history test](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)

## If you want to remember one text, keep it simple

Sometimes I finish reading something and only want to be able to explain it a few days later. A passage might explain why something happens, and I might worry that I'll remember the conclusion while forgetting how two steps in the reasoning fit together. Finding another source and working out a judgment across texts could send me off on a different task. I can keep the reference, try explaining the passage from the beginning, and look back wherever I get stuck. That's a complete reading exercise in its own right.

In their second experiment, Blunt and Karpicke had 80 undergraduates read short science passages and work with the material in paragraphs or concept maps. A concept map puts key ideas on the page and uses lines to show how they relate. Some students could look at the text during practice, while others had to recall it from memory. Each format included students with and without the text, so the researchers could ask whether it mattered more to have the text available or to use a particular format. [Original experiment](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Blunt_Karpicke_JEDP.pdf)

On a short-answer test a week later, pooling the two formats, the students who had practiced from memory did better on questions about what the original said and on questions that required an inference. Scores for the two recall formats were similar. The practice in the experiment was written in both formats. I might do something simpler. I'd set the text aside, try explaining aloud how the ideas relate, and go back for whatever step I couldn't remember. The spoken version is what I'd choose to try. [Original experiment](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Blunt_Karpicke_JEDP.pdf)

Back at the shop, if I only need to remember who covers the Friday counter, I can look up the name in the Friday notice and stop. If I also need to know whether I can make an appointment on time, it's worth checking the opening and handoff arrangements. The same goes for reading notes. I'm willing to spend time removing a source and revising a sentence when that sentence will guide a decision or someone else might rely on it. If I just want to remember an explanation, I can spend that time finding the step I've actually forgotten.

Sometimes it's worth consulting more material even though there's no sentence to revise by removing a source. Say the handoff arrangements have changed and you're simply trying to find out which notice is newer. Check when each was issued and which dates it applies to, and ask the person who makes the schedule if necessary. Once you have a clear answer, use it to decide on a time for the appointment. When several sources each supply part of what the decision needs, going through them one by one helps you see which part of the plan has to move if a piece of evidence falls away.

## Leave yourself a sentence you can check against the originals

To keep this small, I'd borrow an activity from a study of Dutch tenth-grade classes by Alkema, van Weijen, and Rijlaarsdam. Students first worked on understanding their sources and taking notes. They then put arguments from different texts side by side, explained how they were related, gave a reasoned assessment, and finally wrote paragraphs. I'd start with one small part of that process. Write a sentence explaining how two pieces of information connect, then check whether I can find what that connection depends on in the originals. [Classroom study](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1032/917)

Classes that took the full course scored higher on the final writing task for presenting source information, connecting it, and assessing it. The comparison involved classes at two schools, which also differed in their regular curricula. The results here are ratings of finished writing. The study included no separate comprehension test and did not measure the contribution of connecting arguments on its own. I'm borrowing an activity I can try right away. Whether I keep doing it depends on whether it helps me check the judgment in front of me. [Classroom study](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1032/917)

1. Before you read, write down what you need to decide and what you're leaning toward. If you have no initial view, write down what remains open. As you read each source, note the people, date, or task it applies to, and mark the passage you'll want to check again. If you're practicing with the writing studies above, leave a line beside each paper for what it compared and what got scored. [University study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10341-y) · [Sixth-grade study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625)
2. Restate the sources separately. Then write one sentence you can use to make the decision for now. If each source adds a condition, name both conditions. If the sources seem to agree, check whether they're answering the same question. If they really do disagree on the same point, spell out what that disagreement means for your decision. If you have only two accurate restatements, keep them as they are and write down one piece of information you still need.
3. Cover up the originals in turn, leaving the others at hand each time. Read through your draft sentence and record what you can still say, what you have to narrow, and where you have to suspend judgment. Put the sources back and check those edits against the original passages. If your sentence about the tenth-grade study credits the entire writing result to the activity of connecting arguments, for example, bring it back within what the comparison of the full course and the ratings of written work can support. [Classroom study](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1032/917)
4. If you want AI to help you spot something you've overlooked, number the public excerpts you can share and give them to it with your draft sentence. Ask it to keep your sentence as written, identify the excerpt that supports each clause, flag wording that makes the sources sound more comparable than they are, and report separately what loses support when each source is removed. Ask it to mark unsupported parts for follow-up. Then open the originals yourself. Pay particular attention to what both groups actually did and whether the scores came from answers to questions or from written work. Revise the sentence yourself. [History study](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/660/625) · [Classroom study](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1032/917)

I wouldn't do this every time I read two articles. I'd pick one sentence I intend to use, perhaps to plan a class, set a time, or ask someone to act. Once I know what to do or can say exactly what I still need to find out, I can put the paper down. If I haven't checked who's opening the shop on Friday, I won't book the appointment yet.

Open a page of notes you wrote recently and find one sentence you plan to act on. If you could reread only one of its sources, would you still do what the sentence says?
