Unfounded assumptions — this point really resonates with me. I often make such unfounded assumptions. I think the reason for this is the stereotype, which is a kind of preconceived notion before thinking. Often, this kind of cognition may be prejudice. The term "stereotype" first appeared in 1798, originally meaning a printing plate that could reproduce any pattern. Later, it evolved to mean "preconceived notions," not the contribution of psychologists, but rather the work of American journalist and political commentator Walter Lippmann. In his 1922 book *The Public Mind*, Lippmann first used the term to refer to the fixed impressions the public has of certain groups — usually based on gender, race, age, or occupation. Stereotypes are related to how we learn knowledge and understand the world. They are a kind of generalization of the vast world around us, based on our past experiences. Because our stereotypes tell us that chairs can be sat on and apples can be eaten, we are able to respond appropriately when we encounter them. We don’t need to bite into an object that looks like a chair to confirm whether it’s edible. In other words, stereotypes help us save cognitive resources. What we really need to worry about is not the stereotype itself, but the "stereotype threat" — "threat of stereotype."

The famous experiment that proposed the concept of "stereotype threat" was conducted in 1990: in the experiment, Black students took the GRE, with some being told the test was purely a measure of intelligence, while others were told the test scores had nothing to do with intelligence. The results showed that the group of Black students who were told the test was a measure of intelligence performed significantly worse than the other group. In other words, negative stereotypes — such as the belief that Black people have low intelligence — can actually affect people’s performance.

We should be cautious of negative stereotypes, but positive stereotypes can be "used" to our advantage. For example, a study found that when Asian female students were told both that "women are not good at math" and that "Asians are good at math," their math performance was no longer affected. This is because the negative stereotype was balanced out by the positive one, thus neutralizing the negative impact. Stereotypes exist because they have statistical validity — for instance, the stereotype that "Asians are good at math" is indeed statistically accurate. However, we should be careful with such statements. Most of the "stereotypes" in our daily lives have no statistical basis; they are merely subjective opinions or influenced by the media. Of course, even conclusions that do have statistical support cannot be used to predict individual performance.

Either-or thinking, I understand as a form of binary opposition. It often limits choices to two options, appearing like a zero-sum game. For example, as the teacher said, when asking children whether they like their mother or father, are they really opposing? Can we only choose one? Often, the question itself is biased. For me, either-or thinking is rare. I usually give myself one choice and stick with it. If I haven’t decided yet, I give myself more possibilities, weigh them, and choose the best option.

Relativism. Educational psychologist William Perry studied the changes in students’ worldviews during cognitive development, later simplified into three stages. The first stage: dualism — "the enemy is not of my kind, their hearts are different." The second stage: relativism — when you learn more different perspectives and opinions, you begin to doubt everything, or even deny everything — "anything has two sides, so you can’t say I’m definitely wrong." The third stage: commitment — here, relativism refers to the second stage, where you begin to doubt or deny everything, believing that things have two sides. During my university years, I had these feelings. When I heard the professional knowledge that professors taught, I would question whether it was absolute and tend to think in relative terms.

Lastly, blind following. Thinking of Li Xiaolai’s words about following the crowd, making choices without understanding why the majority thinks something is right or good. The simplest example is my major in civil engineering in university. I had no understanding of my major. At that time, I felt relieved after the college entrance exam, and as for my major, my family said civil engineering had good job prospects, and the real estate and construction industry was booming. But years later, looking back now, the civil engineering industry is more oriented toward the outside — for example, the Belt and Road Initiative — while domestic development is moving toward higher-level talent. So sometimes, blind following is hard to be correct. Li Xiaolai told us to follow the crowd, to follow long-term successful investors, and to learn the simplest strategy — just buy, buy, buy. I think this is one of the few, if not the only, pieces of advice that can be blindly followed. That’s what I thought today — I’m recording it down!
