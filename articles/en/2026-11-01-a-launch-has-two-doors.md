# A Launch Has Two Doors

> Published: 2026-11-01 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-01-上线有两道门.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-01-a-launch-has-two-doors.md)

---

What do we mean when we say a product has launched? A working URL is a real step. But I find it useful to split “launch” into two doors. The first puts a public explanation where it has a chance to be found. The second lets a person arrive and accomplish something worth doing. You can open them at different times. You should not give them the same passing grade.

A summary of website building advice argues for getting a first version online so programs that read web pages automatically can understand it, then polishing the details. I agree with its warning about delay. I also want to ask what happens after publication. If a page is public, what exactly allows us to say that the product is live?

This distinction changes the next thing you do. Someone can spend a day perfecting colors and call that product work. Someone else can publish a page in a hurry and call that demand validated. Both may have worked hard. Neither necessarily knows whether a visitor can finish the one task that matters.

## The case for opening the first door

The argument for publishing early deserves its strongest form. While a site remains private, outsiders cannot open the page. You can check whether the title explains the purpose, the text is clear, and the essential links work before publication; a public URL lets outsiders make the same checks. A “crawler” is a program that reads web pages automatically. The summary proposes making the explanation readable to such programs first, but it provides no evidence about how search services handle the page. Waiting for every visual detail to feel finished delays the checks outsiders could already make. For a site hoping to attract visitors through search, I propose publishing an accurate explanation first and polishing details afterward. That is starting advice, not a research finding.

The website summary describes a team that spent part of a limited development period revising its interface while the core journey remained unfinished. That is a retold account, not something I witnessed, and it cannot establish a rule for every product. It makes me ask what job the first version is meant to do. If the job is simply to publish a clear description, a reachable page with an accurate topic and working main links is meaningful progress. It may still have no actual users. Calling that proof of demand would be claiming one step too many.

There is another limit to this door. The summary gives no evidence about whether a search service will add a public page to its searchable list, a process called “indexing.” Nor can the summary tell us whether the service will show that page to a searcher. Making the page's words clear is an entrance check I propose; it cannot answer why a visitor would arrive or where they could go next. A clear sign on a door is useful, but you still need to know where the door leads. The image distinguishes an entrance from a completed journey; it does not explain how search services work.

I would give this stage a plain name: a public description outsiders can open. Its scorecard is plain too. Can the page be opened? Is the description accurate? Does the main route end abruptly? Having no visits yet is not necessarily failure. Having visits is not automatically evidence that people need the product. A visit can be an accidental click; a bookmark can mean “perhaps later.” Keep these signals separate and you can tell which hurdle you have actually cleared.

If you are not yet taking payments, collecting personal information, or making consequential promises, a public description and a way to register interest can be a relatively contained first move. Once the page says that a visitor can complete a task, though, people will act on that statement. If the second door is closed, greater visibility sends more people toward a dead end. “Publish early” can still be sound advice here. We simply need to say what has been published: an explanation, or a usable service?

## What can a person actually do inside?

Builders can easily imagine a user journey from their own screen. I understand this button, so surely other people will. Maybe not. In [Set Boundaries, a chapter of Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03), Ryan Singer recounts a customer request for a calendar. Working from home, the customer could not see the office's wall calendar for meeting rooms. She drove to the office to answer a caller's scheduling request, only to find no suitable opening. The team already had a view listing events. The missing piece was visibility into free time. This is the product author's account of a case. It shows how a broad request can be narrowed to an action; it does not guarantee that every interview will uncover the right answer.

For a first version, the lesson goes beyond asking which feature someone wants. At which step is the person stuck right now? If a page promises to help schedule a meeting, a visitor needs at least a way to see available times, or a clear account of who will confirm them next. Otherwise, a polished calendar picture merely makes the entrance look convincing. The journey still breaks after the first step.

In his 2013 essay [Do Things that Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes Airbnb's founders contacting hosts one by one in New York and helping them improve their listings. People often use this story to praise personal effort. I take a narrower lesson: early on, a human can connect parts of a critical journey manually and notice obstacles the original design missed. This is Graham's account of a particular company, not a controlled test showing that manual service works for every business.

So the second door does not require every step to be automated. Can someone understand the offer, send the necessary information to the right place, and receive a credible next step? A person may handle part of the process initially, provided the page honestly says what will happen and what the visitor must wait for. Whether AI runs behind the scenes, or whether the interface resembles that of a large company, is secondary. The visitor's action must reach somebody or something that can receive it. A button should do more than send a person into silence.

A builder acting as the visitor cannot be the only test of this door. We know where our own buttons lead, so we skip the guessing that a newcomer must do. Suppose a page says “Start now,” while the builder knows that clicking first opens a form and a result will arrive later. A first-time visitor may expect an immediate result. That is an invented example to clarify the point, not a visitor I have met. It reminds me to test one entire action: the person understands the promise, tries it, and receives a response. If they can proceed only after an explanation from me, I need to put that explanation on the page or change the step.

Nor does Airbnb's later success prove that every project should copy its early tactics. This success story does not tell us what happened to other projects that made comparable efforts, so it cannot establish how well manual service works in general. The case supports one modest practice: while you can still find the first few users, watch someone complete the task. Find where the journey breaks, then decide what to automate. A success story becomes misleading when treated as a fixed recipe.

## Why do we keep circling the first door?

If meeting users matters, why is another pass at the homepage so tempting? One ordinary explanation is that changing a margin gives you a visible improvement today. Inviting an unfamiliar person to try the product leaves you unsure whether they will come or what they will say. That is my inference about work choices, not an experimental finding about developers' motives. But it suggests a useful question for myself: Did today's work reduce a crucial uncertainty, or did it merely give me the certain feeling of finishing something?

In their 1979 paper [Prospect Theory](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf), Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky asked people to choose among hypothetical monetary options with stated probabilities. They found that changing a certain outcome into a probable one could substantially change preferences. Their work concerns particular risk choices from that period, not web design. I use it only as a prompt for self-examination: when one action produces a visible result and another might produce uncomfortable feedback, am I giving too much weight to the first? Only what I do next can answer that. The paper cannot diagnose me.

Time estimates create a second trap. In one study reported in [Exploring the Planning Fallacy](https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Exploring-the-Planning-Fallacy_Why-People-Underestimate-Their-Task-Completion-Times.pdf) in 1994, Buehler, Griffin, and Ross asked 37 psychology students nearing completion of their honors theses when they would finish. Among the 33 included in the completion-time analysis, the average best prediction was 33.9 days; the average actual time was 55.5 days. These were student projects, not website launches, so those figures are not a multiplier for a development schedule. They do expose a familiar plan: assume the page, copy, and functions will proceed smoothly, then fit user testing into whatever time remains.

Another experiment in the same paper adds a useful qualification. Some participants were required to connect experience from similar previous assignments to their current task. That group's average optimistic bias, or how much too early its forecasts were on average, shrank; the absolute prediction error did not significantly improve. “Absolute prediction error” means the distance between a prediction and what happened, whether the prediction was early or late. Connecting past experience to the current task may make a group's forecasts less optimistic on average without making each forecast more accurate. I find that more useful than a generic instruction to “leave extra time.” Pick a genuinely similar past task, identify the step the first estimate missed, and decide what to remove from the current scope.

I especially want to apply this to launch plans. A plan can list design, development, and testing while omitting the time needed to follow a willing user through the whole journey. When feedback arrives, it is then called an extra request. Yet observing that journey was always part of checking the second door. Put all discovery time at the end and the schedule will look tidy. The thing you most need to learn is also the first thing the schedule will squeeze out.

## A separate check for each door

Here is one thing you can try today. Write a complete promise in one sentence: who arrives at this page, what can that person do, and what happens after the action? If the sentence can only contain broad phrases such as “boost productivity” or “start growing,” the second door is still too vague to test. You can absolutely publish an explanation first. Just do not present it as a finished service.

Then check the doors in order. Open the page on a device without your saved login. Read the title and copy as a newcomer would, follow the main link, and see whether the entrance makes sense. Next, ask one person who resembles your intended reader to start at the page and complete the action in your sentence. Record where they stop, what they misunderstand, and whether somebody receives their next step. Resist explaining the interface while they try. Finally, separate what you saw: if the entrance is missing or unclear, repair the first door; if the entrance is clear but the task cannot be finished, repair the second. This is my proposed check, not a universally validated procedure from a controlled study.

Give the work a time boundary as well. In the same Shape Up chapter, Singer distinguishes estimating the time for a proposed design from deciding how much time a problem deserves and shaping the design to fit. I prefer the latter for a first version. Set a budget for checking the entrance and another for making the task work. If the promise will not fit, reduce the promise rather than leaving unfinished functionality hidden in the copy. The thesis students' delays do not prove that fixed time is always right. This recommendation combines a product method with my judgment about early product risk.

I would also leave a blank line on the check sheet for problems I did not anticipate. A word on the page may confuse the visitor; the visitor may complete the task only to find nobody responsible for replying. I would not immediately turn every observation into a feature request. Which problem blocks the promise I wrote down? Which would merely make the experience more comfortable? Address the blocker; the other improvement can wait. That is not an excuse for low quality. It gives first-version quality a concrete subject: this journey must actually work.

Some obligations cannot be removed by narrowing a promise. Before a public test involving payments, sensitive information, or content on which people may make consequential decisions, check safety, accuracy, and responsibility. If nobody can receive a request or follow through, a submit button has not opened the second door. Seeking earlier feedback does not entitle a builder to transfer an unseen cost to visitors.

## Which door has the first version passed?

Someone may object that there are no visitors at the start. If a real user must complete a journey before publication, won't the date slip forever? That concern is fair. I am not asking for both doors to open on the same day. You can publish an honest explanation of what will be available while clearly naming what is not available yet. If outsiders can open and understand it, the entrance check has passed; the task check has not. When someone is willing to try, test the shortest real journey. Giving these milestones separate names makes it easier to move forward.

On the other hand, if somebody is already willing to try and you keep postponing because of indexing, layout, or homepage animation, ask yourself a less comfortable question: Am I waiting for the page to improve, or for a visitor who will never point out a problem? Put the URL in a real person's hands. Let them try one small task. If they get stuck, good. Your next change finally has a direction.
