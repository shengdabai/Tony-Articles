> Confirmation Bias

I often hear the phrase — “Do you really think what you think?” Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out evidence that confirms rather than challenges one's existing beliefs. The public tends to favor information that confirms rather than challenges their current beliefs. Confirmation bias is like an information filter in our minds, letting in information that aligns with our values and blocking out information that goes against our will. Confirmation bias doesn't just filter information; when faced with situations that contradict our beliefs, it also leads us to actively seek out evidence that supports our existing views. People can stubbornly cling to beliefs that seem absurd or foolish without realizing it, because they don't have to “change their mind.” For example, I once became obsessed with a person or a product to the point of being unable to let go. In reality, what I was obsessed with wasn't the thing itself, but rather the emotions and choices I invested in it. Fans, like those in celebrity fan clubs, often argue with each other, each believing their idol is the best. It's similar to how people argue over phone brands like Apple and Huawei, convinced they're expressing personal bias.

> Double Standards

This point is a bit painful, but I have to admit that I sometimes fall into double standards, and my double standards are somewhat different. For instance, at work, I don't set high expectations for others, often just accepting their completion, while I myself must meet my own standards and can't easily let others take the lead. In university, my double standards were very obvious — I took on a lot of work, while my teammates had little to do. I understand the danger of double standards as leading to complaints. I often complain about why they don't do well, while I have to do so much. In short, it's because I haven't learned to delegate and have an overly high self-perception. In fact, most things can be done by others, it's just a matter of time.

> Overgeneralization

Drawing an extreme belief from a single incident and applying it inappropriately to other events or situations, which is a common mistake among second-language learners. The essence of overgeneralization is linguistic transfer, along with mother-tongue transfer strategies and simplification strategies, which are learning strategies for foreign languages. They are methods for forming and testing hypotheses and building an intermediate language system. In my teaching, overgeneralization is often evident. For example, a student learning verb past tense changes might wrongly assume that all verbs just add “ed,” and refuse to memorize irregular verbs. In short-term English learning, memorization might be the only way. The real way to build a solid foundation is through reading. The more you read, the more words you'll encounter, and the more you'll naturally understand. Many learners want quick and easy methods, always looking for shortcuts. In fact, there is one — use English constantly. If you don't know something, look it up. If you don't understand, learn it. Read and practice repeatedly!

> Post hoc fallacy

Assuming that because event A happened before event B, A caused B. The sequence of events does not necessarily imply a causal relationship. Although events may appear to be connected in time, this connection does not mean there is a causal relationship. For example, the moon landing and my feeling unwell today have no connection. Temporal continuity does not equal causation. We need to identify all influencing factors and analyze the effect of each variable independently. Why do people like to attribute causes? I think it's because they need a reason. If I didn't do well, it's not my fault, but because of something else. One thing that's not good about middle and high school Chinese is the heavy use of conjunctions like “because...so...” The key is that teachers don't help students understand how these connections are inferred or what the logical relationship is. On the contrary, when others make mistakes, it's easy to analyze rationally and give logical arguments, which allows for critical understanding of oneself and questioning one's own thoughts. This is something that needs constant practice!

> Unjustified Assumption

Unjustified assumption is something I've deeply felt. I often make such assumptions, and I think the reason is due to stereotypes — preconceived notions that we hold before thinking. Often, these cognitive biases are prejudices. The term “stereotype” first appeared in 1798, originally meaning a printing plate that could reproduce any pattern. Later, it evolved to mean “fixed notions,” not the contribution of psychologists, but rather the work of American journalist and political commentator Walter Lippmann. In his 1922 book *The Public Mind*, Lippmann first used the term to refer to the fixed impressions the public has of certain groups — usually based on gender, race, age, or occupation. Stereotypes are related to how we learn knowledge and understand the world. They are a form of generalization about the world, based on our past experiences. Because our stereotypes tell us that chairs can be sat on and apples can be eaten, we can react appropriately to them without having to bite into a chair to confirm if it's edible. In other words, stereotypes help us save cognitive resources. What we should really be concerned about is not stereotypes, but stereotype threat — “threat of stereotype.”

The famous experiment that introduced “stereotype threat” was conducted in 1990: Black students took the GRE, with some told the test was purely a measure of intelligence, while others were told the test score had nothing to do with intelligence. The results showed that the group of Black students who were told the test was a measure of intelligence performed significantly worse than the other group. That is, negative stereotypes (such as Black people having low intelligence) can genuinely affect performance.

We should be cautious of negative stereotypes, but positive ones can be “used.” For example, a study found that when Asian female students were told both “women are not good at math” and “Asians are good at math,” their math performance was no longer affected. This is because the negative stereotype was balanced by the positive one, thus neutralizing the negative impact. Stereotypes exist because they have statistical validity. For instance, the stereotype that Asians are good at math is statistically accurate, as Asian students tend to perform better in math. Be careful with such statements. Most of the “stereotypes” in our lives have no statistical basis and are merely subjective opinions or influenced by the media. Even for conclusions that do have statistical support, they cannot be used to predict individual performance.

> Either/Or

I understand “either/or” as a form of binary opposition, often limiting choices to two options, appearing like a zero-sum game. For example, a teacher might ask a child, “Do you like your mom or your dad?” Are they really opposing each other? Must we choose one? Often, the question is skewed by us. For me, “either/or” is rare. I usually give myself one choice and stick with it. If I haven't decided yet, I give myself more possibilities and choose the best option after weighing the options.

> Relativism

Educational psychologist William Perry studied the changes in students' worldviews during cognitive development, later simplified into three stages. The first stage: dualism — “Those who are not like me are different in heart.” The second stage: relativism — when you learn more different perspectives and opinions, you begin to doubt everything or deny everything — “Everything has two sides, so you can't say I'm definitely wrong.” The third stage: commitment — here, relativism refers to the second stage, where you start doubting or denying everything, believing that things have two sides. During my university years, I had these feelings. When a professor taught a subject, I would question whether it was absolute and prefer to think in relative terms.

Finally, blind following. Thinking of Li Xiaolai's words about following the crowd without understanding. The simplest example is my major in civil engineering in university. I had no understanding of the major, thinking that after the college entrance exam, I was free. As for the major, my family said civil engineering was good for employment, and the real estate industry was booming. However, years later, looking back, the civil engineering industry is more oriented toward the outside, such as the Belt and Road Initiative, while domestic development is moving toward higher-level talent. Sometimes, blind following is hard to be correct. Li Xiaolai told us to follow the crowd, to follow long-term successful investors, and to learn the simplest strategy — buy, buy, buy. I think this is the only or even the only advice that can be blindly followed. That's what I thought today, and I'm recording it here!
