**Self-contradiction, faulty analogy, and irrational demands**

After careful reflection, I realize that the fallacy I’m most prone to is faulty analogy. The reason is simple: my understanding of some things is shallow, and I sometimes fall into a closed-minded way of thinking, believing I’m right. Then, I try to understand or convey my perception of something or something else through analogy, hoping to express it in a fresh way, and also to make it easier for others to understand. For example, during my time studying civil engineering in university, many people would ask me if my major was "earth and wood," meaning I was a bricklayer. (This isn’t the usual sense of work—it really means working on construction sites!) I wasn’t very clear about it before university, but when people said I was "earth and wood," I couldn’t help but explain. If they didn’t understand, I’d use analogy to explain. But due to limited knowledge, I now realize that my previous explanations were wrong and conveyed the wrong perception. While analogy can help us better convey our understanding, we should first think clearly or practice more, constantly refining our understanding to avoid misusing analogy.

For the above three points, I’ll give examples to illustrate my understanding:

1. **Self-contradiction**: A leader assigning tasks might do this. For instance, they might first tell you to complete one task, and then immediately ask you to do another. If the two tasks don’t interfere with each other, you can just carry them out. But if the leader’s statements are contradictory, the two tasks are opposing, and completing one makes the other impossible. I understand this as the leader sometimes not thinking through the task clearly, just feeling like it needs to be done and rushing to do it without considering things thoroughly. Of course, not all leaders are like this—mainly those who are just starting out in management or lack management experience and haven’t studied management. Managing oneself is similar. Setting a reasonable plan for yourself, if it’s self-contradictory, you can find out through practice. Taking action might be a better way.

2. **Faulty analogy**: I think faulty analogy might appear in middle school teaching. For example, in the study of science subjects like math, physics, and chemistry, some teachers might use incorrect guidance to help students memorize so-called mnemonics or magical formulas, just to make students think the teacher is strong. Some teachers even go so far as to make analogies. In university, many professors will say, “What you learned in middle school is wrong—it should be this or that.” I’ve always been thinking about this. In middle school, our understanding is limited, and most knowledge is taught in a fill-in-the-blank way, so we just need to memorize it. The so-called perfectionists who insist on being precise might show their talent, but this doesn’t help much in the long run. Since our understanding is limited, using simple and crude analogies to teach is wrong. Even if students don’t understand the real reasons behind it, teachers should still pass on true knowledge, or help students learn to think independently. With the development of technology, our knowledge will become outdated and be corrected, but without independent thinking, we can only follow authority or teachers, which is not feasible. After all, teachers are also learning and updating their understanding.

3. **Irrational demands**: After hearing about the story of Bao Li, I felt a chill. Irrational demands are so terrifying. Thinking more carefully, isn’t this phenomenon common in our lives? People have many irrational demands, and these often weaken our work ability, including how we use time. These irrational demands take up a lot of time convincing ourselves to overcome them, and they stubbornly exist, causing our good methods to fail.

In reality, we all have some of these emotions to some extent. These emotions are not unfamiliar to us. Human emotions are complex, and there are other irrational emotions as well. The term "irrational" might cause misunderstanding, making it seem like these emotions are bad. But the existence of emotions is reasonable and has value for us. Understanding their value reasonably can help our personal growth. The rational brain and emotional brain are used to different degrees depending on the situation. It’s interesting to understand this, and we don’t need to be too strict with ourselves. First, accept yourself, and then gradually change!
