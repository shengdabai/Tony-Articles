# The More You Use It, the Less You Resemble a New User

> 发布日期:2026-10-27 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-27-自用久了，你就不再是新用户.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-27-the-more-you-use-it.md)

---

“I use my own product” sounds reassuring. Surely the person who works with a tool every day will spot its rough edges first. I want to add a condition to that claim: the smoother your own experience becomes, the more you need to check whether you have simply learned to work around the rough edges. The tool improves, and you become more practiced. Both happen at once, but the improvement is often credited entirely to the tool. Then comes the puzzling result: you finish work faster than ever, while a first-time user cannot get past the entrance. Why does your ease of use say so little about theirs?

An AI-generated summary of a product discussion places two ideas side by side: use your product daily, find friction, improve it with AI, and pass the improvement to users; then consider the gaps in understanding that may appear with a different group of users. These are a proposed practice and a warning in a summary, not findings from a study. Together, they raise a harder question: are we improving the whole route, or just learning to run faster along a route we already know?

I am in favor of using what you build. If you will not touch your own tool, it is hard to ask someone else to rely on it. But using it yourself is a way to discover problems, not proof that the product is usable. This matters especially with AI tools. If you give the tool familiar files each time and add requirements you learned to state, you have already prepared the task for it. You also learn where it makes mistakes, which request needs an extra sentence, and which button to avoid for now. The better you work together, the harder it becomes to tell who actually got better.

## Ease can mean that both the tool and its user have changed

Think of a drawer at home. A long-time resident knows exactly where the tape is, even without a label. A guest can open the same cabinet and still fail to find it. The resident is right that the arrangement feels convenient; the guest is right that its organization is unclear. They are doing the same job with different memories. An AI tool adds another twist: a practiced user may organize the files and add requirements before handing it a task. A newcomer may not know to prepare either. The drawer explains how familiarity can hide friction. It does not prove that the software can handle unfamiliar tasks any better.

Daily use makes recurring annoyances easy to see. If you fix the same formatting problem every morning, you will soon ask AI to handle it. If you retrieve the same kind of material each day, you will improve the entry point. Those changes save real time. Yet a new user may not know how to describe the task, where the result went, or where to resume after a failure. The thresholds you have already crossed are the first thresholds they face.

That is why I am careful when someone says a tool is “already smart enough.” The person making that claim usually knows the goal, can recognize a plausible output, and knows when to stop. For an expert, removing a confirmation step may improve speed. For a newcomer, that step may be their only chance to understand what just happened. Giving a new user the expert's shortest path can make a demo look fluent while making mistakes harder to catch.

A practiced user has another skill that rarely appears in the product's score: they redirect it before a failure becomes obvious. They split confusing material, add a constraint the system did not ask about, or retry as soon as a result looks odd. An observer sees the task completed, but not those small corrections. If the product is meant for someone else, the corrections need to become visible again. Otherwise a team may mistake “our expert can rescue it” for “the system is reliable on its own.”

[Thomas and König studied predictions of task duration](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00760/full) by asking students to do tasks a week apart. Some formatted an essay twice; others assembled a model before formatting an essay. Across three experiments, predictions for the second task were less biased when participants had done the same task before. Simply telling them how long the earlier task took did not consistently improve their predictions. These were students doing bounded tasks, so the findings cannot predict retention for an AI product, meaning whether people keep using it later. They do offer a useful warning: experience helps only when it fits the job now in front of us.

Store a hundred of your own operations and a system may look experienced. But when it faces an unfamiliar objective, it might merely retrieve whichever old operation looks closest. A similar screen does not imply the same intention. The same button can sit inside a different risk. The more records you keep, the easier it is to bury that distinction under “this is how we always do it.” Useful experience needs more than a record of actions. It needs the goal, constraints, signs of failure, and a reason why a past solution applies this time.

There is a more uncomfortable possibility. Heavy users may gradually reshape their work to suit the tool they have. For example, someone who wanted help solving a difficult problem may discover that the system is good only at organizing source material. They reduce the request to “organize this material,” get a fast result, and call it efficient. The speed can be real, while the original problem may remain unsolved. This is my inference, not a result of the task-duration experiments above. To judge whether the product improved, we need to ask whether it completed the original task, not only whether a revised task ran faster.

I would ask two separate questions about that progress. Can the tool now handle material it could not handle before? And has the person become better at stating requirements and catching mistakes? The first can become a product capability. The second may be a skill the user earned. Both have value, but when we promise a product capability, we can count only the part that does not quietly depend on expert skill. This distinction also gives the person credit for what they learned.

## A stranger's pause reveals the other half

There is still a great deal to learn from working with your own product. [In his 2013 account of Viaweb's early days](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes the team using its store-building software on behalf of merchants. While building a merchant's site, he would find a missing feature, implement it, and then resume the work. The gap appeared during the actual workflow, while the developers themselves operated the tool. This is a founder's account of a startup, not an experiment showing that the method works for every product.

Notice the decisive detail: they were not merely using their software to build their own store. They were doing a merchant's job for a merchant. The operator was familiar with the product, but the task came from someone else. That pushes self-use beyond one's own habits. Another person's material, order of work, and concerns can expose gaps that the developer's routine no longer reveals. For me, this is more instructive than simply increasing the number of times I use a tool.

It is easy to swing too far the other way: ask new users for feature requests and build everything they mention. That can fail too. Someone who asks for an export button may actually be worried about losing the finished result. Someone asking for more templates may really be stuck on the first choice. Adding each requested feature looks responsive while leaving the person's actual job unclear.

[Alex Osterwalder's Customer Value Map](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-customer-value-map-v-0-8-now-called-value-proposition-canvas) separates what customers are trying to do, what causes them trouble, and what they hope to gain from the value a product proposes to deliver. He makes another useful distinction: the customer side is observed in the market; the product side is designed by the team. The map is a way to organize observation and design choices, not proof that anyone will buy. For an AI product, it helps prevent a convenient shortcut in reasoning: “I designed a feature that removes a step” does not establish that this was the step users wanted removed.

Choose your unfamiliar user carefully. If the product is meant for people who already know their way around software, testing with someone who barely uses a computer may reveal real difficulties without answering the product question. If you claim that anyone can open it and begin, testing only with colleagues will not do either. Name the people you intend to help, then ask someone in that group who has not learned your personal routine to try it. The wrong participant can make both failure and success misleading.

So a stranger's hesitation needs a more precise record. “They could not use it” gives us little to change. What were they trying to deliver? Where did they first hesitate? What did they expect after clicking? What actually happened? Those details point to a part of the route we can repair. The point is neither to grade the user nor to let the AI mark its own work. It is to find the section that did not connect.

Compensation for AI can be especially hard to see. A user receives a polished answer without knowing which source the system skipped. A developer sees a successful run without noticing that a person manually uploaded the file again at the last step. It may be fair to call the whole sequence a success, but that label does not tell you what to change next. Whether to repair the input, clarify instructions, or make the system pause when uncertain depends on where someone had to catch it.

The most experienced user is not a universal benchmark either. [Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond studied 5,172 customer-support agents at one firm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11771). The authors' abstract for the revised 2024 paper reports that access to a generative AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. Less experienced workers saw larger gains in both speed and quality; the most experienced saw small speed gains and small declines in quality. This concerns customer support at one firm, not every occupation. It reminds us that the same tool can affect different groups in meaningfully different ways, even when the average looks good.

Test only with your most practiced users and you may miss gains for newcomers as well as losses for experts. Watch only whether a newcomer feels immediate relief and you may discard value that takes practice to appear. The useful question is not “Did it get a good average rating?” It is “For which person, doing which task, did which step change?” That will never fit as neatly on a marketing slide, but it can prevent a lot of misplaced product work.

## Turn self-use into a bounded test

A person building a small tool cannot recruit a panel for a formal study every day. I do not think they should wait for a perfect research design before improving it. I propose a practical boundary: take the same problem and watch two attempts, one by a familiar user working as usual and one by someone encountering the product for the first time with their own material and goal. Compare where the attempts diverge. This is a test I propose, not a method shown to work by the studies cited here. At first, you need a clear view of the broken step more than a beautiful chart.

Do not compare only their speeds. The experienced user may have spent a long time preparing prompts and folders beforehand, while the newcomer has to pay that setup cost during the attempt. Comparing only the time shown on screen will favor the expert. Start from the moment each person receives the raw task, and include preparation and recovery after errors. The aim is not a polished universal score. It is to see where the time actually went.

Before either attempt, write down what “done” means. For a merchant building a web page, a generated page is only part of the outcome. Can the merchant check that its information is right, find where to edit it, and know whether it is public? The merchant workflow comes from Graham's public account of Viaweb. These proposed checks are mine, not measurements Viaweb reported making. Define success backward from the end of the user's task, or the tool will be tempted to declare victory as soon as it produces output.

Next, keep two accounts: what the tool did for the person, and what the person did to compensate for the tool. Count the extra prompt you wrote, the file you reformatted, the output you selected by hand, and the error you explained on its behalf. This is not an argument against AI assistance. Seeing human compensation clearly tells you which part the tool should shoulder next time and which part still requires a person's judgment.

Then let the unfamiliar task stay unfamiliar. Do not tidy the material into the format your tool prefers before the test. Do not teach every hidden phrase and then call the product intuitive. If instructions are part of the product, give everyone the same instructions. If the task works only when you stand beside the user and translate, that translation is work the product has not yet delivered. One attempt cannot stand in for a whole market. It can still tell you honestly who made this attempt succeed.

The distinction is particularly visible in learning tools. [A 2026 study of Learn Your Way](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2026.1783117/full) asked 60 high-school students from the Chicago area to study one textbook chapter. One group used an interactive AI platform; the other read a digital textbook. All 60 completed the immediate test; 58 completed the recall test three to seven days later, 29 in each group. The platform group did better on both tests. Within the platform group, using more presentation formats did not show a clear additional score benefit. The study covered one chapter and one platform, the groups spent different amounts of time studying, and the contribution of individual features was not isolated. For me, its useful lesson is to keep “used more features” separate from “learned more.”

An AI product deserves the same distinction. Clicks, conversation turns, and automated actions show activity, but they do not necessarily show that the original job got done better. Can the user complete a second task using different material? Can they find the cause of a small mistake? Can another person with the same kind of need get the job done? These checks take more effort than measuring time spent in a tool, but they are closer to the outcome the product promises.

The cost of a mistake also matters. If a tool helps draft something easy to redo, a wrong turn is usually visible and reversible. If it submits an outcome that takes effect immediately, the same mistake needs a different standard. In higher-stakes work, show the person the evidence and the action awaiting confirmation before expanding automation. In lower-stakes work, a few real attempts, a quick change, and another newcomer may be more useful than an elaborate process designed in advance.

You might object that this process is slow. AI products change quickly. Recruiting newcomers and retaining human observation could eat up the speed advantage. That concern is fair, especially for low-risk tasks with clear goals and experienced users. There, daily self-use is cheap, timely feedback. Every button does not need a laboratory, and not every personal tool needs to become a product for strangers.

The boundary changes when you turn “I can use it easily” into “other people can use it,” or when you ask the system to handle an important outcome on its own. A wider claim needs tests involving the people and tasks it covers. You can test fewer things, but test the ones that might change your conclusion: can a first-time user begin, can they recover from failure, and can they judge the result themselves? Keep the speed. Just do not ask speed to answer a question you never tested.

Here is something you can try today. Pick a task you know well and often do with AI. Write down the original goal and what would count as complete. Replace your usual input with fresh material you have not prepared, and ask someone who has never watched you use the tool to try the same kind of task. If no suitable person is available, at least record every extra instruction and manual correction you normally supply. Then ask one question: did this attempt work because the tool made the route clear, or because someone knew what the tool left unsaid?

I still like the phrase “use your own product.” I just do not want it to become an all-purpose pass. At its best, it means that builders are willing to put their own work through the tool. Bring an unfamiliar person's goal into that test, and the route you know by heart may finally become a route someone else can follow.
