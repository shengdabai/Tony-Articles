# AI Didn't Remove the Starting Line. It Moved the Line Before the Prompt.

> 发布日期:2026-09-14 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-14-AI 没有取消起跑线，只是把它移到了提问之前.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-14-the-starting-line-moved-before-the-prompt.md)

---

A while ago, I put aside a small software project.

The idea still mattered to me. The problem was that the code became tangled faster than I could untangle it. Every fix seemed to expose two more things I did not understand. At some point, continuing was no longer persistence. It was just expensive confusion.

Recently, I opened it again. With AI beside me, work that had once felt beyond my reach began moving. Around the same time, I stepped into an unfamiliar area of visual recognition and trained my first working model. I had not spent years preparing for either task.

It was tempting to draw a dramatic conclusion: AI has put everyone back on the same starting line.

I almost believed it.

Then another small tool, which I thought would be quick, took far longer than expected. AI could suggest an architecture, write code, explain an error, and offer five alternatives in minutes. It could offer opinions about which inconvenience was the real problem, which compromise I would regret later, or when a merely working result had become good enough for another person to trust. It could not take responsibility for those decisions.

That changed my view.

AI has lowered the walls around many fields. It has not flattened the ground inside them. The old head start—memorizing commands, knowing every menu, recalling the right syntax—is becoming less valuable. But the head start has not disappeared. It has moved earlier, to the moment before we type the prompt.

## The wall is lower, but direction costs more

There are at least two kinds of difficulty when we enter a new field.

The first is access difficulty. You need to know the vocabulary, install the tools, understand the file formats, find the right library, and learn enough syntax to make anything happen. This used to consume months before a beginner could receive meaningful feedback.

AI compresses much of that journey. It can translate unfamiliar terms, generate a first version, and stay with us through errors that once would have ended the attempt. This is real progress. More people can now enter rooms whose doors used to be guarded by credentials and years of procedural knowledge.

The second kind is orientation difficulty. What is worth solving? What does success look like? Which constraint is real and which one is merely inherited? What evidence would show that the current approach is wrong?

AI does not remove these questions. It can help us phrase them and challenge our first response, but a suggested objective is not an owned objective. Someone still has to decide which outcome matters and bear the cost of being wrong. By producing more possible answers, AI can make that responsibility more urgent.

Imagine entering a huge workshop where every machine responds to ordinary language. The machines can cut, print, assemble, calculate, and revise almost instantly. That is extraordinary. But if you do not know what you are trying to make, faster machines give you a larger pile of impressive parts.

This is what happens when someone asks AI to “build a reading app.” Within minutes, there may be screens, buttons, accounts, themes, summaries, and recommendations. Yet the actual problem may be much smaller: a reader wants to reopen a book and find one marked passage without sending private notes to a server. Until that outcome and those constraints are clear, additional features are not progress. They are polished uncertainty.

AI expands the space of possible answers. A larger search space is valuable only when there is a way to judge direction. Otherwise, a faster explorer can simply get lost faster.

## Experience did not vanish; it changed form

One idea in my notes was that the weight of talent and intelligence is falling as AI makes it easier to cross into unfamiliar fields. I still think there is something important here. But “everyone is now at the same starting line” goes too far.

Some experience is losing value quickly. The exact sequence of buttons in a tool, the syntax of an API, or the approved way to structure a project may expire. If years of experience mean only being fluent in yesterday’s procedure, AI can make that advantage surprisingly fragile.

Other experience becomes more valuable when generation gets cheap. A person who has seen a project fail knows where hidden assumptions usually live. A careful reader notices when an explanation changes the definition halfway through. Someone who has shipped real work knows that a clean demo and a dependable tool are not the same thing.

This is experience stored as judgment rather than memory.

A beginner with AI can often produce a respectable first version. An experienced person with AI can turn old mistakes into constraints, checks, and better questions. The gap in generating an answer may shrink, while the gap in evaluating ten plausible answers may grow.

That does not mean established experts automatically win. Experience can also harden into a reason not to try. The advantage belongs to people who can extract a portable principle from what they know, carry it into a new field, and still let reality correct them.

When I first worked on a visual-recognition problem, getting model-training code was no longer the mysterious part. The harder questions were ordinary and concrete: Does the training material resemble the conditions in which the tool will be used? Which mistakes are tolerable? What happens under poor light? Does the result help outside a demonstration?

AI helped me cross the technical threshold. It did not spare me from building a standard for “works.”

The new head start is made of problem sensitivity, conceptual range, evaluation skill, and the discipline to close a feedback loop. These qualities are learnable, but they cannot be downloaded as a finished package.

## Write a starting-line card before the prompt

If the advantage now begins before the prompt, we need a practice for that moment.

I use a simple idea: before asking AI to help me enter a new field, I write a “starting-line card.” It takes one page at most and answers five questions:

- Whose situation should be observably better when this works?
- Which constraints must not move, even if moving them makes the task easier?
- What is the smallest artifact that reality can judge soon?
- What result would make me abandon or reframe this direction?
- Which reusable concept do I want to carry out of this project?

The first question prevents a technical exercise from pretending to be a useful result. The second keeps AI from solving an easier but different problem. The third creates contact with reality before the project becomes emotionally expensive. The fourth gives failure permission to teach us. The fifth turns a one-off attempt into accumulated capability.

For a personal reading tool, the smallest artifact might not be a complete application. It might be a rough flow in which a few different books can be imported, marked, closed, and reopened without losing the marks. If that fails, a beautiful home screen is irrelevant. If it works, the project has earned the right to become larger.

This card also changes the conversation with AI. Instead of asking for a complete answer, we can ask it to expose assumptions, propose competing approaches, design checks, or identify the cheapest way to falsify an idea. AI stops being a machine that fills empty space and becomes a partner inside a learning loop.

That is a better use of intelligence than requesting more output.

## Expand your search space, not your saved-items list

I keep returning to the phrase “expand your cognitive search space.” It sounds abstract, but it should lead to visible behavior.

Saving twenty articles from unfamiliar fields does not expand a search space. Neither does asking AI for a long list of concepts. The space grows when a new concept changes the questions we can ask or the actions we can take.

A practical cycle is: explore, build, return.

Explore a field far enough to find one useful concept. Build the smallest thing that forces the concept to meet reality. Then return to your original work and ask what changed in your judgment.

The return matters. Without it, cross-disciplinary learning easily becomes intellectual tourism. We collect fresh vocabulary but keep making the same decisions.

The small software project I reopened did more than produce a tool. It changed how I think about breaking work into testable parts. The visual-recognition experiment did more than give me a model. It made data quality, edge cases, and real-world conditions impossible to treat as footnotes. Each project enlarged the next question I was capable of asking.

That is the kind of expansion worth pursuing. Not knowing more in the abstract, but becoming able to notice a better problem.

## The new starting line is open, not equal

AI is making many first attempts radically more accessible. That matters. People who once had to wait for permission, training, a team, or years of spare time can now test some ideas while the need is still alive.

My few experiments do not prove that anyone can enter any field on equal terms. Access to tools, time, data, money, and a safe place to fail remains uneven. Some fields also carry consequences that make a quick AI-assisted attempt irresponsible. The claim I am willing to make is narrower: for a growing class of low-stakes knowledge and building tasks, the cost of obtaining the first piece of real evidence has fallen sharply.

But access is not the same as equal outcomes. The starting line is more open, not equal. Some people arrive with richer concepts, sharper standards, stronger bodies of feedback, and a greater willingness to revise what they believe.

The encouraging part is that the new advantages are built through practice. We can learn to notice problems. We can turn experience into checks instead of status. We can enter unfamiliar rooms, make something small, listen to what reality says, and carry the lesson forward.

AI can make us faster at crossing a field. The point is not to cross more fields for the sake of movement. The point is to become the kind of person who can tell, once inside, what deserves to be built.

That judgment begins before the prompt.
