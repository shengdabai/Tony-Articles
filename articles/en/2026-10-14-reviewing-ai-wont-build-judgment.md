# Reviewing AI’s Work Won’t Build Your Judgment by Itself

> 发布日期：2026-10-14 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-14-只挑%20AI%20的错，练不出自己的判断.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-14-reviewing-ai-wont-build-judgment.md)

---

You can spot a flaw in an AI answer at a glance. Then you face a blank page and cannot take the first step yourself. Have you learned the skill? I don't think so. Reviewing a proposal and making one call for different actions. As AI takes over first drafts, a small question follows: where do we practice making a judgment from scratch? We can still make room for it, but we have to do so deliberately. In particular, we need a safe chance to create a plausible but flawed proposal ourselves, explain its flaw, and repair it.

## Spotting a flaw is some distance from making a sound proposal

01. An AI-generated summary of a report on the future of work raises a concrete concern. If AI takes over entry-level tasks such as drafting documents, organizing information, and checking files, beginners may lose relatively safe places to try, err, and watch experienced people work. The summary warns against reducing a person's role to approving AI answers. I want to take that warning one step further: even a careful reviewer may miss practice in deciding where to begin, what to set aside, and how to revise a choice that turns out to be wrong.

02. You can taste a dish someone else made and tell that it has too much salt. When you cook it yourself, you must decide how much salt to add, when to stop, and what to change if the flavor goes wrong. Both activities use your sense of taste; the second also demands prediction and adjustment. Reviewing an AI proposal differs from making one in a similar way. Cooking gives you another taste before you serve the dish. Many decisions at work offer no such reset, which is why this sort of practice belongs in a reversible task that cannot hurt anyone.

03. An [Anthropic experiment on coding skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) gives us a narrow, observable view of the gap. Researchers recruited 52 developers who already used Python but had not learned the library in the exercise. After the task, the group with AI access averaged 50% on an immediate quiz; the group coding by hand averaged 67%. The AI group finished a little faster, but the difference was too uncertain to rule out chance. The quiz placed particular weight on reading code and diagnosing errors, skills a person needs when taking responsibility for AI output. This was a short exercise followed by an immediate test of understanding. It does not show that long-term AI use inevitably erodes skill.

04. The researchers also observed differences within the AI group. Participants who handed code generation or debugging over to the assistant tended to do poorly on the comprehension quiz. Participants who asked conceptual questions, sought explanations, and resolved errors themselves tended to do better. Asking AI where the bug is differs from asking why I made that mistake there. The first question can get the task moving; the second may change how I approach the next task. The researchers classified these behaviors after observing them; they did not randomly assign people to usage styles. When two things occur together, that does not prove one caused the other. We cannot treat these observations as a tested training method.

05. A reasonable objection is that work asks for a correct result, and catching mistakes may be enough. For an experienced person doing a familiar, repetitive task, that may be a sound choice; the coding experiment does not show that such a task must be drafted by hand. My concern is a different case: a beginner has yet to acquire a basis for judgment but is still expected to stand behind the result. They may notice that an AI draft sounds polished without knowing which assumption needs checking. They may catch a typo while overlooking an option that never appeared in the draft. The finished draft has already narrowed the visible choices, and an omitted option comes with no red underline. This is my inference about review work, not a result the experiments measured directly. Calling that review evidence of mastery changes the question without answering it.

## Some mistakes are more useful when we make them ourselves

06. A [2026 study of how students learn experimental design](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-026-09819-x) offers a sharper clue. After instruction in the basic principles, 276 university students practiced in one of three ways: create a valid design, identify and repair deliberate mistakes in someone else's design, or deliberately make an invalid design of their own, explain its mistakes, and correct them. Both error-based groups did better than the valid-design group on a closely related task. On a more complex new task, the group that made and repaired its own errors outperformed the other two groups. “More complex” meant a different presentation and more conditions to handle, not merely a change of words in the old question. The tested skill was experimental design; the result is not proof that this method works for every job.

07. Why might making your own error help? I propose one explanation: it asks you to account for how the error came about. When someone hands you a flawed proposal, you can circle the conspicuous problem. To create a flaw that still looks plausible, you first need to consider what a sound proposal depends on, then intentionally remove one condition. When you repair it, you revisit your choice and explain why the conclusion no longer holds without that condition. This may help you trace the error to a choice you made, rather than merely label a ready-made error. The study compared test scores after different kinds of practice; it did not directly test this sequence of thought.

08. Consider someone learning how to compare two study methods. One common error is to give the new method to students who were stronger to begin with, then credit every difference in the final scores to the method. It is easy to nod when that flaw appears in a worked example. Designing the comparison yourself makes you decide how the groups form, what stays the same, and how you will measure the result. This is an example of reasoning that can be checked on paper. It is no invitation to experiment casually on real learners.

09. “Make a mistake on purpose” can sound like an excuse for shoddy work. The sequence matters. Students in that experiment had first been taught the relevant principles, could consult their definitions while practicing, and had to explain and correct the errors they made. Someone who lacks the basics may simply rehearse a misunderstanding; a textbook, trustworthy example, or teacher is a better starting point. A live decision about safety, medical care, or a real payment is no place for this exercise either. Mark the flawed practice version clearly and keep it out of anything you deliver. Practice must have a boundary separate from delivery before we can talk about the value of an intentional mistake.

10. [Ericsson and Harwell's account of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full) makes that boundary clearer. In its original sense, deliberate practice involves an experienced teacher designing tasks for a learner's particular weakness, giving specific feedback, and guiding repeated adjustment. Merely doing a lot of work does not meet those conditions. The individual exercise I suggest below borrows the ideas of a clear target, feedback, and another attempt. Without a qualified guide, I would not claim that it is deliberate practice in the strict research sense.

11. I suggest bringing AI in after your first attempt: write your intentionally flawed example, then ask which assumptions might fail, request counterexamples, or ask whether the solution works with different material. The coding experiment observed an association between ways of asking AI questions and quiz scores; it did not test this exercise sequence. AI's counterexamples may themselves be mistaken, especially in an unfamiliar field, so check them against reliable material or with a knowledgeable person. These questions may help you examine what you wrote, but they cannot replace the choice you must first try to make.

## Put the exercise back into work that has an outcome

12. A separate [randomized study with undergraduates](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849) puts a necessary brake on any sweeping conclusion. Here, “randomized” means the researchers used chance to decide who could use AI, rather than grouping students by their existing ability. Researchers gave 211 students a timed assignment to learn an unfamiliar topic and write an analytical essay. One group could use an ordinary AI tool; the other could not. Both later took assessments without the tool. The AI-access group did better on the immediate test, and the estimated score difference was still positive a week later, though the later estimate was less precise. Permission to use AI did not mean that every student used it, so the comparison captures the average effect of making access available. The authors also observed different later outcomes among people who used AI to explain concepts and those who used it to draft for them. Those usage patterns were observed, not randomly assigned.

13. Taken together, the two AI studies do not make me want to tell everyone to turn AI off while learning. One tested developers learning an unfamiliar coding tool. The other tested students learning a topic and writing an essay. The tasks, participants, and time constraints differed. AI can let a person skip a crucial attempt, and it can help that person spend more time understanding. If the goal is to deliver another document in a familiar format tonight, speed can come first. If the goal is to handle a new situation independently next month, the action that builds judgment needs time today. The useful question is more precise: which action in this task forms a judgment I will eventually need to make on my own? I should perform that action myself at least once.

14. Building a product offers another way to see the point. In a [2013 essay on early startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes Stripe's founders setting up willing early users in person and the value of founders meeting users one at a time. This is a founder's account, not an experiment establishing a universal launch method. I borrow only the action: encounter one real difficulty in use yourself, and you may hear a condition your original plan left out. Someone might agree to try a product but get stuck at the very first step; revising the sales copy would teach less than finding out what stopped that action. AI can organize the notes or help you rehearse questions. It cannot turn user feedback that has not happened into a fact.

15. The following exercise is my proposal drawn from the research, not a tested research result; the studies did not test these full steps. You can try it today. Choose a small task that stays private and cannot affect anyone else. Consult one trustworthy basic rule, write your own solution, and then deliberately break one important assumption in it. Explain how that change would make the result fail. For instance, while practicing a comparison of two public proposals, quietly add an extra condition to one and explain why a direct comparison would now mislead. Ask AI to find the flaw and suggest a repair; check its answer against the rule. Finally, switch to new material and redo the task without AI. If AI finds a different issue, first verify that it would actually affect the conclusion instead of accepting its confident wording. I suggest using your explanation on the new material to check whether you understand the rule. If you cannot explain it, find the missing knowledge or someone who can give sound feedback. This check has not been tested either.

16. I also suggest a question for checking your practice: besides asking whether the final version is correct, can you say in advance where it is most likely to fail? If a beginner can name only one point, they can use it to choose the next practice task. This is a suggestion for organizing practice, not a research-tested passing mark. An expert seeking dependable delivery on a familiar task can hand more work to AI. On a new task, while training a beginner, or when you will answer for the outcome, put the chance to propose and revise a solution back into the workflow. The balance between practice and delivery changes with the task; responsibility for learning does not automatically move to the tool.

One small question can help catch a confusion between learning and editing: without AI's version in front of me, can I write an answer that might be wrong and explain where it might fail? Catching mistakes has real value. What takes more effort, and is worth protecting, is making the first attempt and owning a mistake that I can still correct.
