# Cognitive Biases

> Published: 2021-08-19 · [中文](../zh/2021-08-19-%E6%80%9D%E7%BB%B4%E5%AE%9A%E5%8A%BF.md) | [English](../en/2021-08-19-cognitive-biases.md)

---

*The MIT Method of Deep Thinking: Thinking About Phenomena Through Models and Mechanisms*

In the book, Hironori Hiraoka divides nine cognitive biases into three categories. Let's take a look at how he describes them.

The first category consists of basic cognitive biases, including *reverse causality* and *satisfaction with the ordinary solution*. *Reverse causality* is the most common cognitive bias, referring to when people ignore the underlying essence of a phenomenon and instead take the phenomenon itself as the cause. For example, when you feel tired, your first thought is to rest. Of course, being tired does require rest, but this might only address the symptoms rather than the root cause. The real reason for your fatigue could be that you've lost your sense of future goals, or you might be sick. In such cases, what you need is to re-plan your goals and treat your body, not just rest.

*Satisfaction with the ordinary solution* refers to when you settle for the usual way of solving a problem in your work or life. For instance, when analyzing why weight loss always fails, people often come up with the ordinary answer: control your diet and exercise more. This solution seems universally applicable, but people who are trying to lose weight often lack the perseverance to stick with it long-term. Hironori Hiraoka says that if the real reason for weight loss failure is the deliberate ignoring of the fact of obesity, then simply weighing yourself daily and making your weight visible is more effective than reducing food intake and exercising more.

This is the first category of cognitive biases.

The second category of cognitive biases can lead people into a "dilemma." That is, the more you try to improve your thinking ability under these biases, the more likely you are to become superficial. This category includes four cognitive biases: *reliance on frameworks*, *range adaptation*, *stopping thinking at keywords*, and *clinging to initial assumptions*.

Let's look at each one in turn.

*Reliance on frameworks* refers to when, after learning some frameworks or models, we use them to organize information. The sense of accomplishment from organizing information makes us feel as though we've thought and understood. As a result, we stop further thinking. Hironori Hiraoka says that frameworks are just tools to aid thinking, not machines that can derive answers.

*Range adaptation* is very common in daily life. It refers to finding causes within the category to which something belongs. For example, if we say someone is too nitpicky, we might assume they are a science major. When a small company always struggles to hire excellent employees, we might assume it's because the company isn't big enough.

Similarly, chasing popular keywords can create a false sense of understanding, leading us to stop thinking. Hironori Hira, believes that what's more meaningful than the keywords themselves is the time, effort, and thinking involved in the process of finding them.

Those who cling to initial assumptions often get stuck in their earliest assumptions. Hironori Hiraoka believes that assumptions need to evolve with new information and discoveries. If you cling to your initial assumptions, you fall into a cognitive bias and block the path of evolution, unable to move beyond your original ideas. The result could be that your thinking remains stuck at the "tip of the iceberg," missing the whole picture.

The third category of cognitive biases, as Hironori Hiraoka puts it, involves standing still at the entrance of thinking. People trapped in these biases often fail to realize that they haven't actually been thinking.

This category includes three cognitive biases: *forgetting the purpose of thinking*, *overemphasizing the process*, and *losing independent thinking*.

What does it mean to forget the purpose of thinking? Hironori Hiraoka explains that new consultants in consulting firms often fall into the trap of analyzing for the sake of analysis, forgetting why their analysis is being done in the first place. As a result, they end up producing a lot of contradictory information.

*Overemphasizing the process* refers to when people mistake "executing a procedure" for thinking. The result is that "the content of the answer is empty, and the mind is used only for executing the process, not for real thinking." For example, when your boss asks, "What is the real need of the customer?" you might reply, "Through a questionnaire, we can see the customer's needs, and I'll prepare for the next meeting to discuss it." This is overemphasizing the process. What your boss really wants is something like, "What the customer truly needs is speed of response, not the method of response, and this should be reflected in the customer questionnaire."

Now, looking at *losing independent thinking*, it refers to the unconscious reliance on others' ideas and the laziness to think for oneself. Knowing that without deep thinking, you lack confidence in your own ideas and end up fully accepting others' viewpoints.

Through the analysis of these three categories of nine cognitive biases, Hironori Hiraoka found that most people have three to four cognitive biases.

He suggests that if you feel your thinking has fallen into *reverse causality*, try to seek other answers; if you realize you're stuck on keywords, try explaining the problem without them; if you notice you're satisfied with using a framework to organize the problem, abandon the framework and approach it from a different angle, or even create your own new framework; and if your initial assumption doesn't lead to further thinking, think of counterexamples and try to challenge your initial assumption.

Hironori Hiraoka concludes: "Changing your actions concretely is an effective way to overcome cognitive biases. Humans are actually weak-willed creatures, so it's easier to change your will by first changing your actions than by trying to change your actions through willpower."
