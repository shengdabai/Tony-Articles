# The People Least Afraid of AI Want Their Roles to Disappear

> 发布日期:2026-09-21 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-21-最不怕被 AI 替代的人，反而盼着自己的角色消失.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-21-the-role-was-never-the-goal.md)

---

Today I was organizing a note about a volunteer fan-subtitling group when one response stopped me.

Someone asked what would happen if AI eventually made every video instantly understandable. Would the group disappear?

One volunteer's reaction was almost immediate: that would be wonderful. Everyone could finally enjoy the work without waiting for us.

There was no attempt to prove that humans translate more beautifully. No anxious defense of the group's importance. No argument that the old workflow had to survive.

At first, this sounds strange. If a tool can make your role unnecessary, why would you welcome it?

Then the answer becomes obvious: the role was never the goal.

The group did not exist so that its members could remain indispensable. It existed because something they loved was difficult for other people to understand. Translation was a temporary bridge. If a better bridge appeared, protecting the old one would betray the reason they built it.

That distinction matters far beyond subtitles.

My judgment is simple: the people best prepared for AI are not those who can defend every task they perform. They are the ones who know which result matters enough that they would happily let their current role disappear.

## When a role becomes a cage

We often describe ourselves with verbs.

I write. I translate. I teach. I design. I code.

The verb begins as a method. After enough repetition, it becomes an identity. Once that happens, a subtle reversal takes place: instead of using the role to solve a problem, we start protecting the problem so the role can continue.

Imagine a team that has prepared the same weekly report for years. Then an AI system can produce it in minutes. The sensible question is whether anyone makes a better decision because the report exists.

But that is rarely the first question. People add approval steps, polish the formatting, or explain why only an experienced human can produce the “real” report. The report survives. Its purpose remains unexamined.

AI is unusually good at exposing this reversal because it makes methods cheap before organizations have time to reconsider their goals.

The risk is not only losing work. It is confusing the continuation of work with the continuation of value.

Of course, a paid worker cannot treat the disappearance of a job as an interesting thought experiment. Income, bargaining power, credit, and transition time are real. Telling someone to “embrace replacement” while they carry all the cost would be shallow.

But even when a role must be defended in the short term, we still need to know what the role was for. Without that clarity, we defend the container and accidentally abandon the thing inside it.

## One activity can pay three different wages

The subtitling note contained another clue.

After AI took over transcription, timing, and rough translation, not everyone left. People with language ability moved toward review. Some members had little production work left but stayed active in the group. Elsewhere, people continued translating manually because translation itself was how they practiced a language.

From the outside, all of them appeared to be doing the same work. They were not receiving the same reward.

I now think many activities pay three different kinds of wages.

The first is the **result**. A video becomes understandable. A product ships. A customer receives an answer.

The second is **practice**. By doing the work, you acquire judgment, fluency, taste, or a mental model that cannot be downloaded with the final output.

The third is **relationship**. The task gives people a reason to gather, trade small observations, earn trust, and feel part of something.

These are easy to bundle together when the work is expensive. AI begins to unbundle them.

Research on volunteer fansubbers found a similar mix. Passion and gaining experience helped bring people in; finding friends and having a place to volunteer helped keep them involved. The study also found that participants associated collaboration with stronger motivation. It was one case study, not a universal law, but it gives language to something that is easy to miss: production may be the visible activity while learning and belonging are part of the actual reward. ([2020 case study](https://doi.org/10.1075/tcb.00039.mor))

There is an important caveat. We can see the people who stayed more easily than those who quietly left. A community surviving automation does not prove that everyone welcomed the change. It only shows that some motives can outlive the original workflow.

This also connects with self-determination theory. A foundational paper describes autonomy, competence, and relatedness as three basic psychological needs that support sustained motivation. AI can reduce the labor required to produce an output, but it does not automatically replace the agency, mastery, or connection people were getting through the process. ([research paper](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf))

That is why “automate the task” is incomplete advice.

If the task is mainly delivery, automation may be pure progress. If it is also practice, total automation can quietly weaken the person. If it is a social ritual, deleting the task can dissolve a community even when the output improves.

The visible work disappears first. Its invisible wages disappear later.

## Use the disappearance test

Here is a small test I want to keep using.

Take one recurring activity and imagine that tomorrow a machine can perform its visible output instantly, at near-zero cost, and at a quality you trust.

Then ask three questions.

- **What result would I be glad to receive without doing the work?** Automate this part.

- **What ability would weaken if I stopped doing the work myself?** Preserve a deliberate practice loop for this part.

- **Which relationships would fade if the activity vanished?** Design a new reason for those people to meet before removing the old one.

Suppose I use AI to help write an article. Transcription, source organization, and format checking may be delivery work. I am happy to make them cheaper.

Choosing the central claim, noticing where an argument cheats, and deciding what I am willing to stand behind are practice. If I hand those over every day, I may publish more while slowly losing the ability that made the publishing useful.

The exchange with readers is relationship. A perfectly automated publishing pipeline can increase output and still make the work feel empty if nobody is genuinely listening or responding.

The same test works for learning. If my goal is to understand a paper, AI can help me locate terms and compare interpretations. If my goal is to become fluent in English, letting AI speak every sentence for me removes the exact repetition I need.

It also works for a small team. Automating meeting notes is usually harmless. Automating the conversation in which disagreement becomes visible is not the same thing. One removes clerical work. The other may remove the place where collective judgment develops.

The practical move is not to preserve friction indiscriminately. Some friction is just waste. The move is to distinguish **production friction** from **training resistance** and **social ritual**.

A slow export is production friction. Remove it.

Struggling to explain an idea in your own words may be training resistance. Keep some of it.

A weekly review may look inefficient, yet it may be the ritual through which a team notices weak signals and learns to trust one another. Redesign it if necessary, but do not delete it merely because a summary can be generated automatically.

## Let the method die without losing the mission

There is a limit to this argument.

Not every role is attached to a noble mission. Not every displaced worker has the time or power to move “up the value chain.” AI systems can concentrate control, erase attribution, and shift risk toward the people with the least leverage. A clear purpose does not pay next month's bills by itself.

So I do not see the disappearance test as a moral demand. It is a design tool.

It tells us what to automate, what to retrain, what to rebuild, and what deserves a fight. It also prevents two opposite mistakes: preserving useless work because it feels like identity, and deleting valuable practice or community because it looks like inefficiency.

The weakest AI strategy is to keep proving that today's task still requires a human. That argument comes with an expiration date.

A stronger strategy is to attach your identity to a problem worth solving, an ability worth developing, and a group of people worth growing with. Roles can then change without making the person disappear.

The volunteer subtitlers understood this before many companies did. If everyone could understand the videos tomorrow, the old bridge could go. They would still have the thing that mattered: the work they loved, the abilities they chose to practice, and the people who had found one another while building the bridge.

So perhaps the useful question is not, “How do I make myself impossible to replace?”

It is this: **What outcome would make my current role unnecessary—and when it arrives, what human thing will I deliberately keep?**
