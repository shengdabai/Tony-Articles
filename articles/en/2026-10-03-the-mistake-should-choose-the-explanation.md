# The Mistake Should Choose the Explanation

> 发布日期:2026-10-03 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-03-解释该长成什么样，先看人会错在哪里.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-03-the-mistake-should-choose-the-explanation.md)

---

Give someone a jumbled string of water temperature readings, and they must first work out which time goes with which number. Arrange the same readings in two columns ordered by time, and the rise followed by a leveling off becomes easier to see. Writing researchers [George Gopen and Judith Swan use this example](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/comp/150FP/archive/george-gopen/sci.html). The data have not changed. What a reader can make of them may have.

A saved AI summary proposes turning text into charts, interactive web pages, and even explanatory video. That is a proposal for making explanations, not a research finding that livelier forms improve understanding. It raises a question: does a livelier form necessarily make something easier to understand? My current answer is that we should first identify where the reader is most likely to go wrong. The form of an explanation should make that particular mistake easier to notice and correct. A beautiful presentation may help, but beauty alone does not mean the point has been made clear.

## What work does each form save the reader?

The water temperature example looks like a formatting trick, but it removes a real task. The reader no longer has to hold each time and reading together in memory before noticing the pattern. In their 1990 article, Gopen and Swan go further. By revising passages of scientific prose, they show how connecting a sentence to familiar information and placing its new point where readers can find it may reveal a logical link the original author never stated. Their evidence here consists of worked writing examples. It is not an experiment showing that every reader will understand every text better.

Charts can help with a different task. When I want to compare several values, position and length make differences easier to spot. Yet a chart also makes choices on my behalf: which variables appear, where an axis begins, and how missing observations are handled. Reading quickly can mean that I have only seen what the maker selected. A useful chart should help me notice a relationship while still letting me find the units, the range, and what is missing. Otherwise a crisp image can lend an undeserved sense of certainty to a vague conclusion.

Take the same water temperature readings. A table can show that the final two recorded values are equal. A smoothed line might suggest that the temperature kept rising gently throughout. Conversely, if the readings were taken at unequal intervals, equally spaced bars could mislead. Before choosing a chart, I need to say whether readers must see each observation or mainly the direction of change. There is no universal answer, but there is a cost to ask about: what original detail disappears when the trend is made more visible? If the reader must decide when to stop heating, those last two readings are hardly decoration.

An interactive page suits another kind of difficulty: a reader knows that several factors matter but cannot see what happens when one changes. Imagine a page comparing two savings plans, with controls for monthly contributions and an assumed rate of return. It could draw curves for different assumptions. This is a hypothetical example, not financial advice or a prediction. The page saves repeated mental calculation, but it cannot tell the reader whether an assumed return is credible. If it shows only favorable cases, the ease of moving the controls may deepen the misunderstanding.

Video can show a sequence of movements. If a task depends on the direction of a hand, its timing, and where an object sits in space, a still picture leaves the reader to imagine the transitions. Video can remove that burden, but it also sets the pace. Following the picture is not the same as following the cause. Pausing, rewinding, and trying the action are opportunities to turn viewing into understanding. Charts, pages, and videos all reduce some mental translation. The resemblance ends there: each reduces a different burden and leaves a different blind spot.

## Which misunderstanding is actually in the way?

If someone confuses the order of steps, a timeline or short video might help. If they reverse the size of two figures, a table or chart is more direct. If they do not know what a changed condition would do, an interactive demonstration may help. If the central term is still unclear, one accurate sentence is often more useful than an animation. I am not ranking media. I am matching a tool to an observable error. Choose the wrong form, and greater polish may conceal the mistake more effectively.

There is a harder distinction here: understanding this explanation and carrying that understanding into a new situation are different achievements. Education researchers [David Perkins and Gavriel Salomon discussed “transfer” in 1988](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/journals/ed_lead/el_198809_perkins.pdf). Familiar routines may carry over when a new situation resembles an old one. When the surface looks very different, a person often has to identify a principle deliberately and connect the two situations. In everyday terms, can you recognize the same problem when the next example looks different? Their article offers a framework for teaching and discusses research. It is not a standalone trial proving that any particular interactive page or video creates transfer.

That distinction raises my standard for a good explanation. Suppose a web page demonstrates the cost of a plan. The reader can move its controls and read the resulting curve. That is a beginning. Once the page is closed and the conditions change, can they name the variables that matter? If a new fee appears that the page did not include, will they notice that the original calculation was incomplete? That response suggests the relationship has begun to travel beyond the graphic. A polished demonstration may help someone take the first step. It cannot take the next one for them.

I am especially concerned about the fee that never appeared on the page. An interactive demonstration must decide in advance which variables people can change and which stay fixed. After repeatedly moving a control, it is easy to mistake the available controls for the complete list of important factors. One simple remedy is to state beside the demonstration what it calculates and what it leaves out. Another is to leave a blank question: what important factor might be missing? Neither device makes the model automatically correct. It does turn a hidden assumption into a sentence people can examine. When an explanation will guide a decision, what has been left out is part of its content.

This standard has limits. Not every lookup should train someone to become an expert. Before leaving home, a person checking a venue's opening hours needs an accurate, easy to find answer; asking them to derive the schedule would be absurd. But when they must use the explanation to make a decision, teach someone else, or handle changing conditions alone, it matters whether they can carry the relationship away. I would ask about that use first. Does the person need to see an answer, or take away a way to work out the next one?

## Why fluent output can feel like learning

The saved AI summary proposes turning outputs into charts, pages, or video, but offers no comparison of what those forms cost to make or how well readers understand them. For writing, [a 2023 experimental working paper](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf) by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang asked 444 college educated professionals to complete short occupational writing tasks. Those given access to ChatGPT spent about 37 percent less time on the second task, while professional evaluators rated their work about 0.45 standard deviations higher. To make that comparison, the researchers grouped participants by how they were paid for the tasks and used the spread of each relevant group's first task scores, before ChatGPT access, as the yardstick. The figure does not mean 45 percent of the highest possible score. The researchers also observed a shift in time away from rough drafting and toward generating ideas and editing. The experiment shows a change in how these particular writing tasks were completed. It did not compare the cost of making charts, pages, or videos, measure long term writing gains, or represent participants' whole jobs.

One detail in the same paper gives me pause: many participants submitted the AI's initial output without editing it. That is not inherently wrong. If the aim is to deliver usable text, a tool can help do the work. But if the aim is to learn to explain something to another person, receiving a fluent draft may skip the step that would test your understanding. Looking at a smooth finished piece cannot tell us whether someone delegated the writing, collaborated with a tool, or worked through the idea themselves.

[A 2025 study by Bastani and colleagues of mathematics classes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) offers a way to distinguish finishing work with help from doing it independently. At one high school in Turkey, with nearly a thousand students, researchers compared practice with a standard AI assistant, practice with an AI tutor that provided teacher designed hints, and practice without AI. Students received different kinds of help during practice according to their assigned group, then all worked alone on a test without access to those tools. That gives researchers two separate observations: what students can complete during practice and what they can complete after the help is removed. The article page notes a later correction, but the correction text is absent from the packet. I therefore cannot treat the original article's reported score differences or its explanation for them as findings verified after that correction.

The study's design covers one school, one subject, a particular set of tool designs, and short term tests. Until the correction is checked, I cannot use its results to claim that AI explanations harm learning, much less compare video with text. The two measurements do suggest a writing question to me: when learning is the goal, should we stop at “how many problems were solved with the explanation available?” We can also leave a chance to explain or try something without it. Otherwise we may only be seeing the performance of a person and a support system together. That last point is my inference, not a result this study has established for every form of explanation.

## The strongest objection: sometimes the machine should clear the path

There is a fair objection here. People are busy. Why turn every explanation into a test? For a newcomer, an animation that gives an initial picture may be more welcoming than a page of definitions. If someone needs captions to follow spoken words or slower playback to follow a movement, the explanation should offer those choices. If the task only requires a dependable result, asking a person to reproduce the underlying method without a tool may waste their time. Treating every convenience as laziness turns learning into an ordeal.

I accept that limit. The question is which effort we save. Handing repetitive formatting, calculation, and drawing to a machine usually leaves room for my judgment. Handing over why two things are related and when that relationship fails is different if I must later decide on my own. The water temperature table is a useful example: it puts the times in order without deciding for the reader why the rise slows. A good form frees attention for judgment. It should not fill that space with a feeling of ease.

Readers also need some freedom to enter at different points. Someone who has done a task before may only need a table to check a detail. A complete beginner may need a slow demonstration before the words in the table mean anything. Making both watch the same video from beginning to end looks consistent, but wastes their time in different places. Where possible, I would put concise text, inspectable data, and a pauseable demonstration side by side, so each person can follow their own question. That is a design proposal, not a universal effect established by the research cited here.

Some processes genuinely must be seen. When repairing an object, the direction of a movement or the point where force is applied may be hard to communicate in prose. A short video can be the right choice even if it does not train abstract reasoning. The same criterion still applies: which mistake must the reader avoid? Installing a part backward, omitting a step, and misreading a concept are different problems. Choose video because it puts a crucial movement in view, not because it looks advanced. Whether to add freeze frames, written cautions, or checkpoints depends on the mistakes that remain.

## Name the error before choosing the explanation

The next time I ask AI to explain something I will actually use, I want to hold back the instruction “make it more engaging” for a moment. I can take a page I intend to share and first write down a specific mistake its reader may make: treating a total price as a monthly price, assuming that because two things change together one causes the other, or mistaking conditions in an example for conditions that always hold. “They may not understand” is too vague. The mistake should be visible enough to check. Then the form can follow: a table for comparisons, an interactive view for changing conditions, a moving image for the order of actions, and exact sentences for definitions and limits. If several forms are needed, each should have a job.

Once the explanation is made, change one condition in the original example. On my own, or with a willing reader, I can ask: does the key relationship still hold? What needs to be recalculated or checked again? This is not an exam score, and one small trial cannot prove one medium superior to another. It offers a signal closer to actual use than “that looked clear.” If someone can follow the animation but cannot identify the input that matters most, the explanation needs work. If they can explain the limit but still install the part backward, the picture and the checkpoints need work.

I would not judge the revision only by asking whether people liked the new version. Keep the old one. Ask someone to try a small task with one version, and note exactly where they stopped, what they asked, and what they changed. Let different people try the other version so that seeing the answer first does not shape the comparison. With only a few people, this can reveal problems; it cannot establish that one format is generally better. Finding even one mistake that the earlier version concealed gives the next revision a direction. The point is to help the reader avoid a consequential wrong turn.

The time ordered water temperature table works because it makes one particular mistake, mixing up the order of readings, less likely. When I consider using AI to change an explanation's form, my choice would start somewhere simpler: say where a person might read the situation wrongly, then let the form help. Leave enough room, too, for the person to notice where the form itself may be wrong.
