# Correction Counts on the Next Problem

> 发布日期:2026-10-31 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-31-纠错的价值发生在下一道题.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-31-correction-counts-on-the-next-problem.md)

---

An AI summary of a recorded lesson divided learning into getting information, digesting it for yourself, and putting it to work. That division leaves a smaller question that is easy to miss: after someone points out your mistake, can you make the next attempt yourself? Otherwise, correction merely places a nicer answer in front of you. AI can produce an explanation quickly and make an error sound thoroughly understood. Whether learning happened shows up later, when you meet a new problem without that explanation and recognize what matters. The smoother the tool feels, the easier it is to mistake a smooth completion for a skill you now possess.

## Keep the two scorecards separate

If I get a problem right with AI's help, I know at least one thing: I and the tool completed that problem together. I still need different evidence to know whether I can recognize the conditions and choose a method next time on my own. Both outcomes matter, but for different purposes. At work, getting a deliverable finished may be enough. If I want the ability to stay with me, the second outcome matters too.

[A study by Bastani and colleagues of high school mathematics](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf) makes the distinction unusually visible. Conducted at one Turkish high school in the 2023–2024 academic year, it involved nearly a thousand students; the score estimates below come from the 839 students included in the main analysis. During practice, students used either an ordinary GPT-4 chat assistant, a GPT-4 tutor equipped with teacher-designed hints, or textbooks and notes without AI. They then took an unaided test on closely related concepts. Compared with the no-AI group, practice scores rose by 48% with the ordinary assistant and 127% with the tutor. On the unaided test, the ordinary-assistant group scored 17% lower; the tutor group was statistically indistinguishable from the no-AI group. That means the study could not confirm a difference between those two groups; it does not mean their scores were exactly the same. The study measured short-term math performance at this school. It did not establish what happens to long-term learning in every subject, let alone prove that AI makes people less intelligent.

The important point is not the most alarming percentage. It is that the two scorecards can move in opposite directions. A better practice score and a worse independent score can coexist: a person can have a polished piece of work while the ability needed to produce it has yet to grow. The paper also reports that students using the ordinary assistant did not correspondingly believe they had learned less. Immediate ease is a persuasive feeling. It is easy to remember a quick completion as a firm understanding.

The guarded tutor did not magically raise unaided test scores, either. Its more modest achievement was to largely avoid the loss seen with the ordinary assistant. Its designers supplied teacher-prepared solutions, common mistakes, and matching hints, and instructed the tool to guide students step by step instead of handing over a full solution. That preparation took work. It is easy to say “make AI more supportive.” To aim a hint at a learner's actual mistake, someone must know how the problem works and where learners tend to go wrong.

To keep track of learning, I would use two entries: “completed with the tool” and “completed a related task independently.” The first shows what the tool helps deliver. The second shows what a learner can carry into the next situation. Neither entry should sign off on the other.

## Put the missing piece beside the mistake

What makes feedback useful? It identifies the step to change on the next attempt. Reading a model answer from beginning to end can feel wonderfully clear. Placing it next to my own attempt reveals a more useful difference: did I miss a fact, select the wrong condition, or fail to recognize the structure of the problem at all? Those are different gaps. A missing fact calls for that fact. Misreading the structure may survive ten more readings of the finished answer.

[A 2016 study by van Eersel and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5183614/) offers a useful complication. Psychology undergraduates read short passages. The researchers compared rereading, attempting to recall answers and receiving feedback, and rereading followed by focused statements of the key information. The final questions required participants to apply principles in new knowledge domains. Across the two experiments, retrieval attempts with feedback generally beat rereading alone. But the gap narrowed considerably when rereaders were also shown the key statements. These were university students working with short texts; this is not a permanent ranking of every way to learn.

One example in the paper connects a bat's echolocation to a submarine's sonar. If the time for an echo to return tells us something about distance, what does a change in that time say about whether an object is moving toward the submarine or away from it? The characters in the problem have changed. The relationship that needs to travel has not. Feedback that merely says “wrong” leaves the learner unsure which quantity mattered. Feedback that identifies the link between echo time and distance gives the learner something to carry forward. The researchers themselves note that part of the retrieval group's advantage came from focused exposure to key information. We cannot credit the entire result to the effort of remembering alone.

For someone learning with AI, this suggests a practical shift. Feedback need not be a long, elegant explanation. Ask the tool to identify the most consequential gap in your answer and give you just enough information to cross it. Too little leaves you stuck; too much turns the next problem into another exercise in following along. The only good way to adjust that amount is to try the next problem independently. Feeling enlightened while reading the feedback cannot settle it.

## Arrange a second attempt after the first

The AI summary of the recorded lesson argued that tools can widen access to information and help people act, while digestion and correction still require the learner. I agree with the direction, but I want to make “doing it yourself” observable. There should be a before and an after that you can compare. Where did you originally go wrong? Once you received feedback, how did you handle fresh material? Without those traces, digestion can become little more than a feeling you report to yourself.

[The 1993 paper by Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf) concerns the development of expert performance, not an experiment on AI tutoring. It distinguishes activities designed to improve a skill from everyday work and casual repetition. In its account, practice targets weaknesses, uses informative feedback, and returns to the same or related tasks. The authors also explain why doing a job is not automatically good practice: delivery demands reliability, so people often stick to familiar methods rather than expose a weakness in public. “I do this every day” and “I get better at it every day” are not equivalent claims.

Based on these studies, I propose putting AI between two independent attempts. This is a suggested practice routine, not a complete method that the cited studies tested. First, produce an answer or a rough version yourself, even if it is only an outline of your reasoning. Then ask AI to point to the specific mismatch in that version and explain the fact it relies on; check that fact against a trustworthy source. Finally, take a problem with a similar structure but a different surface and solve it without help. The tool supplies feedback and fresh opportunities to practice. The learner keeps the work of answering, judging, and revising.

The bat and submarine example shows why the final step matters. Memorizing “bats use echolocation” and rereading the explanation may get you through the original question. A submarine problem requires you to notice that you are still comparing the time between sending a signal and receiving its echo. If you retain only the two nouns, the change of setting may stop you. If you can explain that a shorter return time can indicate a shorter round-trip distance, you have a relationship you may be able to apply elsewhere. AI can generate more settings, but its problems and answers may contain errors. Check the central relationship against the original text or another reliable source.

Cooking offers a familiar parallel. Suppose you are learning to season soup, and a skilled cook tastes it and tells you that you used too much salt. Carrying away that bowl and understanding the comment do not establish that the next pot will taste right. You need to change when or how much salt you add, then taste again. Concept learning also connects feedback to another attempt. The parallel has a limit: taste allows personal preference, while a math problem normally has specified conditions for a correct answer. What carries over is the act of adjusting the next attempt in response to a specific difference, not the idea that everything can be graded by the same standard.

This arrangement makes mistakes usable. A failed first attempt does not require an immediate verdict that you are “bad at this.” Being stuck in the same place a second time does not mean you should simply try harder. Ask whether the feedback omitted a crucial fact, whether the new problem jumped too far, or whether you never made an initial attempt of your own. Those causes call for different changes. Separating them is part of practice.

## AI can be a practice partner without being the judge

Someone may reasonably ask why you should struggle to learn a task that AI can already complete. For a one-off task with an easily checked result and no future need for independent judgment, using the tool directly makes sense. Turning every task into personal training would exhaust anyone. I would reserve deliberate learning for abilities I meet repeatedly, whose outcomes I must answer for, and for which I need to notice when the tool is wrong. The useful question is not “How much AI did I use?” It is “If the next result is faulty, can I notice and handle it?”

AI can also play a more interesting role. In [a 2022 teaching proposal](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/how-to-use-ai-to-teach-some-of-the), Ethan Mollick suggests having AI generate examples of a concept or produce a piece of student work. Learners then identify gaps, supply evidence, and explain how to improve it. The AI output becomes material to work on; the learner practices explanation and evaluation. This is a proposed teaching workflow, not a report of measured gains. It is useful for designing an exercise, not a guarantee that the exercise has already been proven to raise scores.

A practice partner can also present a flawed problem as if it were authoritative. The guarded tutor in the high school math study made fewer feedback errors partly because teachers had supplied correct solutions and common mistakes beforehand. Most of us do not have a teacher preparing every question we ask AI. We need to check whether the underlying material is reliable, whether a generated question really tests the same principle, and whether our own evaluation missed a point. In medicine, law, or any other high-stakes field, neither a person nor an AI should declare competence on the strength of one conversation.

Yet waiting for a perfectly reliable practice partner can become another way never to begin. I prefer two levels of work. Use low-risk exercises to expose gaps in understanding. Before carrying a judgment into the world, bring in appropriate sources, qualified people, or real-world checks. Small exercises can absorb mistakes; real consequences should not be used to pay for them. AI can multiply opportunities to try, but fluent answers do not automatically make it an examiner.

## Try one “next problem after feedback” today

Choose a small thing you keep using and for which you can find a trustworthy answer. It could be a concept in public material or an ordinary skill you are learning. Avoid private material and decisions where an error could harm someone. Write your answer to one concrete question, along with why you think it is right. It need not be polished. That first draft is the point of comparison.

Only then ask AI: What is the most important fact my answer may be missing? Give me a clue; do not rewrite my answer. Check the fact it identifies in the original material, and write beside your draft what you missed. Ask for a new situation that tests the same principle. Without looking back at the explanation, answer it yourself, then check both the new question's setup and its answer. If AI has made a faulty question, replace it; do not treat its mistake as your failure.

How will you tell whether that round helped? Do not count how many messages you exchanged with AI. See whether you can explain the cause of your first error and independently spot the relevant relationship in the new problem. Try another after some time has passed to find out whether the initial ease lasted. If you still depend on hints, record exactly where you got stuck, make the next problem less of a leap, obtain feedback aimed at that gap, and try again. You are not proving to a machine that you are clever. You are giving your own learning an honest scorecard.
