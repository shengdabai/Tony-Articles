**Huang Xiaoyan — A Child's Self-Driven Motivation Comes from Accumulating Success Experiences**

The article lists many parents who used "material rewards" to solve problems, but in the end, the child's motivation to do things was for the "reward" rather than the task itself.

My child is almost 7 years old, and I want to share my insights on cultivating self-driven motivation along this journey of leveling up and overcoming challenges.

**① The Fundamental Reason for a Child Having Self-Driven Motivation Is Accumulated Success Experiences**

Every child is born with curiosity. When they first try to do something, they are full of interest, but often can't maintain the persistence. **It's likely that they lack the ability or practice to solve the problem, leading to a lack of success experience.**

Let me share my child's experience when she first started learning to write in the first semester of her big class. At that time, her hand strength wasn't enough, so the characters she wrote were always crooked or too light. In the first half of the semester, every time she did her homework, it was painful for her because she felt she wasn't writing well, and she was very discouraged while writing.

As a mother, I told her that since the class had already been enrolled, she couldn't get a refund. **You are responsible for the homework assigned by the teacher in the class. If you need help, I can assist you, but I will still take you to class.** This ensured she would continue attending classes. On the other hand, after she finished writing, I would tell her, "Wow, you've only taken three classes and can write like this. When I was young, I practiced for a long time and couldn't write as well as you. It's okay, you just need to practice more and you'll get better." After realizing she needed more practice, I would play games related to writing, like a competition to write door numbers, to create opportunities for her to practice.

By the end of the semester, the second semester began. In the first semester, my child was among the last in the class in writing. But one day, the teacher told me that my child was improving steadily. By the end of the semester, she had become the most stable and improved student in the class. Because the teacher praised her, she decided on her own to continue attending classes during the summer vacation. Now that she's just started elementary school, whenever there's a writing task, she's confident and even tells me, "Mom, I want to be better at writing than XXX." Of course, I don't encourage children to compare themselves with others, so I always tell her, **"Did you notice how you were before? Now you're better than you were before!"**

Through this experience, I believe three concepts are very important for children.

**1. Once you start, you can't stop in the middle of a stage.**

This awareness directly cuts off the child's desire to give up, or from the beginning, there's no way to retreat.

**2. Although there's a best standard, remember to always compare with your past self.**

Because only by comparing with yourself does progress have meaning, and the child will want to move forward step by step.

Parents must hold the line here. If you've enrolled in a class, you can't get a refund. If you don't do the homework, you need to explain it to the teacher yourself, but I can help you because you're just starting and you're still young.

**3. Help the child correctly.**

For example, every stage a child will face problems and fail. Help her find the cause—whether it's lack of ability or insufficient practice. Once the method is found, it's okay.

When a child can't see her own success experience, help her find it. Whether by using yourself as an example or helping the child refocus on her past self, it's a good method.

**② Treat "Material Rewards" as a Tool for Positive Guidance**

Many parents use "material rewards" to solve their child's problems, such like not wanting to practice the piano and constantly complaining.

In our family, we also use "material rewards," but not when the problem occurs. Instead, it's after the task is completed or the problem is solved.

For example, when my child was younger, she would complain and want to buy something. I would let her cry until she calmed down, and then I might (not every time) tell her, "I noticed you cried for a shorter time today than last time. You stopped crying quickly, so I think you deserve a reward..."

Another example: she ran for 5 minutes last time, and this time she ran for 10 minutes. I would tell her, "You're really good, you stuck it out for 10 minutes."

Besides material rewards, give her more spiritual success she can see and achieve. This kind of reward will amplify her small progress.

**③ Make the Rewards More Diverse**

At this time, the "material rewards" given to her can be more diverse. Sometimes, they can be replaced with things like a hug, a kiss, or being carried for 10 minutes. These kinds of rewards make the child's perception of rewards more varied, and the motivation (goal) for doing something becomes more specific and easier to sustain.

In summary, I think the fundamental reason for a child having self-driven motivation is the accumulation of continuous success experiences, which come from comparing with oneself. In this process, the parents' role is to help the child summarize when her ability is insufficient, encourage her to do more when she lacks practice, and help her see her success experience and set a clear next small goal when she wants to give up.

Thanks to Bandu editors for providing these methods! They are all real-life experiences that have been implemented, and some points left a deep impression on me, such as **"Using material rewards should not be done after the problem occurs, but rather when the child has made progress."** Moreover, after rewarding the child, I always tell her the reason, which actually **strengthens the child's understanding of her own effort and progress.** Also, shaping the child's mindset and providing help are crucial roles for parents, and they are worth learning from.

**Zeng Chunmei — Once I Used Rewards to Shape My Child's Bad Habits, Now I Understand the Reason.**

During the recent summer vacation, my child participated in the 8th-level Chinese classical dance exam and also in the school's performance. Because she could wear makeup and wear beautiful costumes, she was very happy, and we were too.

After the dance exam, my child once asked me to buy a set of blind boxes as a reward. Because she had taken Li Xiaolai's family education course during the vacation, I didn't fulfill her request, and I could see she was very disappointed and unhappy.

My child started learning dance in the small class of kindergarten. At that time, watching such a little person doing frog poses and straight leg stretches was very cute, and she herself also enjoyed it.

Later, as the difficulty of the basic dance moves increased, she started to refuse to learn, saying that the leg press was too painful, and sometimes she even cried and had to be taken to class by us.

At that time, I still believed that if the child gave up on dance, she might also give up on other things. I tried every method to make her "stick it out."

**Now, looking back, the most common method we used was rewards.** For example, if she attended class seriously for a week, we would buy her a small toy or something like that.

Sometimes, when she really didn't want to go to class, I even used what I thought was a very rational method to persuade her:

If you keep going for a while, and if you still don't feel interested after this year's tuition is paid, then you can stop. This tuition is quite expensive.

The result was that although she wasn't always willing, she eventually still went to class.

In this process, I discovered some problems:

My child now often asks us for rewards. The rewards she asked for started from small stationery items, then small blind boxes, and now even whole blind box sets or big dolls. The things she wants are getting bigger and more expensive.

Later, we realized that this method was problematic, so we started to refuse to give her rewards. Her reaction was always very strong. Sometimes, I would give in and buy her something again.

I think this is a kind of unthinking kindness. After listening to Li Xiaolai's live Q&A, I now know its side effects are too big.

For this issue, I've been very troubled. Today, after reading the article *"[Parents Arbitrarily Giving Children Rewards Is a Cheap and Thoughtless Kindness](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzkwNTM5Mzc5Ng==&mid=2247483898&idx=1&sn=0ddeaf0711b0279565357df6eb0be9f8&chksm=c0f92211f78eab07d6615c1c74ea0aa0997c0977c096bf1b5d70c813def8ed194c5d677c2243&scene=21#wechat_redirect)"*, **I finally realized that my approach was actually using the "random reward method" to shape bad behaviors in my child.**

"The lack of principles in rewards is the key to spoiling a child," this sentence hit me like a wake-up call, making me see the real problem.

On one hand, unprincipled rewards can make the child turn what should be done into a transaction, gradually weakening the child's intrinsic motivation.

On the other hand, **since I had decided to enroll my child in dance classes, regardless of whether she could stick with it or not, I shouldn't use the economic loss as an argument.**

This was a pit I once fell into, and now I understand the reason. I'm sharing this with you.

In this article, there's another point that deeply moved me:

As parents, we should believe that our children naturally have intrinsic motivation. Try to let them find positive feedback from the efforts they put into things.

Seeing this point reminded me of my child's habit of making paper doll blind boxes to give to her little friends. She would design various outfits for the small dolls, including shoes and head accessories, all in small details. She would patiently dress the small dolls in the outfits she made, putting them into her homemade blind boxes.

In my opinion, these activities are very time-consuming and require a lot of patience, but she never found them troublesome. Sometimes she could do them for over two hours, and when she looked at the small dolls dressed in several outfits, she would imagine the joy her friends would feel when they received the blind boxes.

Every time like this, she could gain confidence and happiness from the things she did, without needing anything from me to reward her.

In the future, I want to find more such moments, **encourage the child's effort, not reward the child's results.**

**Zi Mo — After Learning the Course, I Immediately Adjusted My Teaching Methods**

Today, during class, I discussed with my students to cancel the system of adding points for raising hands to answer questions, and I explained it using Li Xiaolai's reward case. To my surprise, the students were very receptive. For a long time, many teachers in schools have used the habit of giving students extra points for answering questions to encourage active participation in class. However, the effect of this method wasn't very good. The same few students always raised their hands, and the majority of students' initiative wasn't awakened by the "bait" of points.

So I asked the students to think:

1. Do they have a subconscious motive to answer questions for the points? Once this becomes a habit, would their enthusiasm for speaking decrease if there were no points or other stimuli? After thinking about it, they told me, "Yes." So they started to negotiate with the teacher in class, saying, "Teacher, if I answer your question, can you give me two more points?" I then asked them to continue thinking: **Would this habit deprive them of their initiative?** Through these layered questions, the students realized that some of the rewards they had become accustomed to were actually stifling their initiative and causing a lack of long-term motivation.

2. **Bring real society back to the students.** The students are studying early childhood education and will soon enter society and start working. So I try to recreate their future work scenarios in class, helping them understand how thinking in a student context can create resistance in real work situations. This helps them get a sense of social rules and prepares them for entering society.

3. I invited them to join me in a semester-long educational experiment. After benefiting from Li Xiaolai's family education course, I tried applying some of the reforms to my teaching. I asked them to observe their own changes in cognition and behavior over the course of a semester.
