# AI Breaks The Speed Ratio

> 发布日期:2026-08-20 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-20-AI打坏的是速度比.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-20-ai-breaks-the-speed-ratio.md)

---

The first thing AI changes is not the org chart.

It changes the speed ratio.

Before AI, many parts of knowledge work were slow in roughly compatible ways. Writing code took weeks. Preparing a prototype took days. Drafting a campaign, reviewing a document, checking a policy, preparing a report, asking for approval, and getting feedback all lived somewhere between hours and weeks.

This was not elegant, but it was balanced enough to hide many problems. A slow process is less embarrassing when every other process is also slow.

Then one part accelerates.

A small team can build a prototype in an afternoon. A developer can test three approaches before the old meeting would have ended. A writer can produce ten drafts before the previous outline is approved. DeepLearning.AI described this clearly in an April 2026 piece on AI-native software teams: when coding becomes 10x or 100x faster, product, design, marketing, legal compliance, and communication suddenly become the bottlenecks around it.[^1]

That is the uncomfortable part. AI does not merely make the fast work faster. It makes the slow work visible.

And once the slow work becomes visible, the organization has to answer a harder question: should this step still be slow, or was it only slow because no one had bothered to redesign it?

## The Bottleneck Moves

Many teams still talk about AI as if the main problem were tool access.

Buy the subscription. Open the model. Train the staff. Encourage everyone to try prompts. Count usage. Celebrate activity. Report that adoption is rising.

Some of that is necessary, but it is not enough.

McKinsey's 2025 global AI survey reported that 88 percent of respondents said their organizations regularly used AI in at least one business function. Yet only 39 percent reported any enterprise-level EBIT impact, and most organizations were still experimenting or piloting rather than scaling across the enterprise.[^2]

Those numbers should make us calmer.

They suggest that AI use and AI value are not the same thing. A company can have AI everywhere and still have almost no real transformation. The tool can be present in the browser, the chat window, the IDE, the meeting room, and the slide deck, while the actual workflow remains untouched.

In that situation, AI becomes a faster employee trapped inside the same old hallway.

The queue is still the queue. The approval rule is still unclear. The data is still scattered. The responsibility is still ambiguous. The user context is still missing. The person closest to the problem still has to wait for someone far away from the problem to interpret it.

The speed ratio breaks, and everyone mistakes the noise for progress.

## Strong Middle Does Not Mean Heavy Middle

The common reaction is to praise small teams.

That instinct is partly right. When AI lowers the cost of building, a small team close to users can move with shocking speed. Fewer handoffs means less lost context. Broader people can connect product, engineering, writing, support, and sales without waiting for five departments to pass a baton.

But there is a dangerous half-truth here.

If we only copy the "small team" part, we create a mess.

Every small team starts building its own data access, its own prompts, its own evaluation rules, its own security habits, its own cost tracking, its own approval logic, its own version of legal memory, its own brand judgment, its own messy folder of examples. The org chart looks flat, but the hidden middle office has been copied badly twenty times.

That is not agility. That is duplicated confusion.

The better pattern is small front, strong middle.

But strong middle does not mean a larger bureaucracy. It means reusable judgment turned into infrastructure.

The middle should provide shared model access, clean data interfaces, permission boundaries, evaluation methods, cost visibility, approved templates, security rules, escalation paths, and decision logs. It should turn repeatable judgment into guardrails. It should make low-risk work pass quickly and make high-risk work stop early.

The expert does not disappear. The expert moves upstream.

Instead of reviewing every case from scratch at the end, the expert helps define risk levels, standard clauses, approval conditions, red lines, examples, and exception rules. The work that can be standardized becomes a callable interface. The work that cannot be standardized remains with a responsible human being.

That is the important distinction.

AI-native does not mean removing responsibility. It means moving responsibility into the system earlier.

## People Need More Initiative, Not Less

There is another trap in the AI discussion: we often describe AI as if its best use were to reduce effort.

That is too small.

The better use of AI is to give people back the power to improve the work in front of them.

Most people spend years tolerating broken processes. A report has to be assembled by hand. A situation has to be reconstructed from scattered notes. A weekly task requires seven clicks in four systems. A piece of knowledge lives in someone's memory and disappears when that person is busy.

Before AI, many people could only complain about these things. They did not have the programming skill, time, or permission to change them.

Now the person closest to the friction can often build a rough tool, draft a workflow, clean a dataset, write a small script, compare options, or at least describe the problem well enough that a better system can be made.

The first version may be ugly. It may be slower than doing the task manually. It may need review. It may fail.

But the psychological shift matters. The worker is no longer only a user of the process. The worker can become a builder of the process.

This is where AI can make people stronger rather than lazier. Not by removing all difficulty, but by moving more people from passive execution into active improvement.

The strongest organizations will not be the ones where everyone waits for a central AI team to rescue them. They will be the ones where the middle gives people safe rails, and the front gives people real problems.

## The Personal Version

The same pattern applies to an individual.

Most people do not have an organization around them, but they still have a speed ratio.

You may be able to write faster than you can think. You may collect information faster than you can digest it. You may generate plans faster than you can test them. You may save time faster than you can decide where that time should go.

If you only accelerate the visible task, you will create a private version of organizational chaos.

More notes, weaker memory.

More drafts, less judgment.

More automation, less understanding.

More output, less responsibility.

So the personal question is not "How can I use AI more?"

The better question is: which part of my own workflow is now out of ratio?

If writing is fast but thinking is weak, build a claim ledger before drafting. If collecting is fast but remembering is weak, build a retrieval habit instead of a bigger archive. If generating options is fast but choosing is hard, define criteria before asking for more ideas. If executing is fast but quality is uneven, create tests, checklists, and review loops.

In other words, build your own middle layer.

Not a heavy one. Not a bureaucratic one. A practical one.

A place where your repeatable judgment becomes reusable.

That may be a checklist. It may be a template. It may be a review script. It may be a file naming rule. It may be a budget limit. It may be a simple question you force yourself to answer before acting: what would make this wrong?

The point is not to slow yourself down.

The point is to make speed responsible.

## Speed Is Not Maturity

The easiest way to misunderstand AI is to treat speed as the final result.

Speed is only the first disturbance.

It reveals what was previously hidden: unclear goals, weak data, fragile trust, missing standards, vague ownership, overloaded experts, and people who were never really allowed to improve the system they had to use.

That is why the next stage of AI will not be won by people who simply produce more. It will be won by people who can redesign the conditions under which production happens.

The test is simple.

When one part of the work becomes ten times faster, what happens to the rest?

Does the whole system learn to move, or does the fast part just crash into the slow part harder?

Do people gain initiative, or do they receive a faster whip?

Do experts become bottlenecks, or do their judgment become guardrails?

Does the saved time return to better work, better relationships, better learning, and better decisions, or is it immediately consumed by more noise?

AI breaks the speed ratio first.

That break is not the failure.

It is the signal.

The real work begins after we see it: rebuild the slow parts that should not be slow, protect the slow parts that carry real responsibility, and turn repeatable judgment into systems that help people act with more power and less blindness.

That is what it means to become stronger with AI.

Not faster in one place.

Stronger as a whole.
