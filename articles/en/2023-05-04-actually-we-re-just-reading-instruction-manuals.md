# Actually, We're Just Reading Instruction Manuals

Any concept can be understood with three words: What (what it is), Why (why it is that way), and How (how to use it). Li Xiaolai, in his course on family education, further breaks it down: What is the subject (including time, place, and characters), Why is the mechanism, and How is the application. So, what are we mostly learning?

Take What for example. We spend a lot of time studying and defining things, like meditation. It has a thousand-year history, but the secret behind it remained unclear to humans for a long time until the development of modern neuroscience and brain science. At the level of What, it's basically what the creator or inventor is doing. For most people, it might just be a definition to be memorized for exams or a definition from a Wikipedia search.

Exploring Why is also not easy. Why do people experience flow? Why is learning so difficult? Why does reading make people sleepy? In childhood, we often ask, "Why?" We are curious about everything. But as we grow, we lose the motivation to ask "Why." It might be due to parents' impatient attitudes, teachers' rigid refusals, or the repeated torture of exams. For adults, "Why" seems to be less spoken. The task of exploring Why is mostly left to scholars and experts.

From this perspective, most of us focus only on How—how to use it, how to apply it, and that's it. Even the demand for using it well is not common. But it's sad that the simple word "use" can make some people hesitate.

Those who know me well know I've done English teaching and training. Not only do students rarely apply what they learn, but even some teachers don't use English. For example, the lesson plans for middle and high school English teachers are supposed to be written in English, but some teachers mix Chinese and English, or even write only in Chinese, with English vocabulary and grammar listed simply. During my teacher training, I found that many teachers don't even know basic teaching terminology, let alone professional content like teaching methods. For instance, assigning homework is actually a dedicated topic for assessment, which has developed into a research direction, but most teachers don't understand it and assign homework arbitrarily. Worse still, some teachers teach so-called learning techniques that have been debunked, lack scientific basis, and haven't even used them themselves. The real useful methods are never used.

English learning and teaching all come down to one word: use. Use it repeatedly and extensively. If you want to do well on exams, focus on exam content, strengthen reading, and repeat application. If you want to improve your teaching ability, prepare lessons entirely in English, use second-language research to optimize teaching, and communicate and practice more.

When it comes to How, we must pay attention to one essential ability—self-learning. I highly recommend reading Li Xiaolai's book *Self-Learning Is a Craft*, which is also available online at [www.lixiaolai.com](https://www.lixiaolai.com/). Self-learning is always important, as it affects our lives and helps us live better. How to self-learn? Li Xiaolai has a more systematic approach, such as programming and English. Self-learning isn't as hard as people think; it's just defeated by the initial illusion, which may come from yourself or from others' criticism. For example, some teachers always say a subject is too hard for you to learn, that you'll never succeed, and how you'll end up in a certain way... In reality, self-learning is an individual act. Most of what we want to learn can be found in books. If one book isn't enough, compare it with others and read several related books together.

Take WeChat Channels as an example. I started from scratch, took a course, joined a community, and read various books. In the end, I found that most content isn't necessary unless you plan to research WeChat Channels and offer consulting or training like me. You just need to apply it where you're good at it. Show your true self, and the rest—skills or operational strategies—can be left to the WeChat Channels team, who provide various resources in the Creator Center. Just go check it out; there's no need to spend money on so-called information.

The usage guides or help centers of platforms and products are well-structured and clear. If you don't read them and instead go to listen to some lectures, you're not worth the effort. Self-learning is a craft. We should all practice it together. How to improve self-learning? Start by reading the user manuals carefully. In fact, self-learning is basically about reading user manuals.

Li Xiaolai once shared that he himself is essentially writing user manuals, such as time management, English learning, investment, self-learning, family education, and so on. The essence is writing his own user manual.

In fact, every product's user manual is very detailed. Most people don't read it, possibly because the comprehensive and detailed instructions are overwhelming. Creating simple and easy-to-understand manuals can be applied in various fields, such as WikiHow.

I often do consulting on WeChat Channels and owned audiences. My shallow understanding of the WeChat ecosystem helps users to some extent. Thinking about it, it's because I help them solve problems, and that communication is effective. When I first started consulting, I often advised people to read books and study cases. Later, I found that most people don't follow through, as they don't care about the process and just want solutions.

Honestly, most problems, especially operational ones, can be solved by carefully reading the official product manual. If you're lazy, just search online—there's a high chance someone has the same issue. If you still can't solve it, contact customer service. There's always a solution.

I rarely bother others. When I have a problem, I search for the answer myself. Of course, today we have AI, which is your assistant. It will listen patiently and help you.

Speaking of AI, what do you need to learn? The underlying principles? Training data to assist AI? In reality, we're just reading the user manual carefully. If you don't know how to use it, just ask it.

I'm increasingly realizing the importance of reading user manuals. Often, when we use products, to save time or because we think we're smart, we skip the user manual and just start using it, which increases the blindness and risk of using the product.

For electrical appliances like ovens and microwaves, the user manual clearly explains temperature and time limits. Without following the manual, food may be overcooked, burned, or even cause a fire.

For cosmetics, medicines, and health supplements, the user manual includes detailed instructions and precautions. Not following them can pose health risks, even leading to irreversible damage.

For smartphones, computers, and other electronics, the user manual explains various functions and operation steps. Correct operation can prevent common mistakes.

For vehicles like cars and bicycles, the user manual includes important information on usage, maintenance, and care. Not following the manual increases the risk of traffic accidents.

To use products safely and reasonably, we need the habit of reading user manuals. Whether new or old, we should read the user manual to deepen our understanding and avoid blind usage that leads to danger.

Reading user manuals not only makes product use more reasonable and safe but also increases our understanding of the product and improves our quality of life.

Reading thoroughly is the secret. The key might be this one sentence: "Although I didn't understand it, I read through it"—Li Xiaolai. Read it several times, and you'll understand naturally.

Most of the time, we are defeated by our own ignorance, thinking something is too complex or challenging, but ultimately, we just don't want to read it thoroughly.

We are often asked if doing something is against the rules. Have you read the platform rules carefully? The relevant regulations and common questions for WeChat Channels are detailed in the Creator Center, but most people don't read them.

Or maybe you think you've read it, but when you face a problem, you still don't know why? Have you read it thoroughly?

Help documents, user manuals, and FAQs are all essential and should be read repeatedly. Why not read them carefully? Or pay for courses and books, which are essentially the same rules?

Don't be blindly confident or unnecessarily self-deprecating. Read it word by word. Believe me, you might have missed the whole world.

If you still have questions and can't understand the rules, search for answers, visit the official Q&A section, like the WeChat Open Community, or seek customer service help. Of course, you also have an AI assistant to help you. Most of the time, we're just applying knowledge, and the common scenario for application is the user manual.

We are extremely unreliable and irresponsible when it comes to reading user manuals. If you don't believe me, have you ever read the user manuals for the electronics you bought? Have you read the instructions for various apps and websites? Or even for medicine and medical advice?

Take the news about a Tesla car accident, where the car couldn't stop, causing damage. Many voices questioned Tesla in the US. But looking at the car's backend data, the driver mistakenly used the accelerator as the brake. The electric vehicle's records are there. More importantly, the driver didn't know that Tesla cars will automatically brake after 3 seconds in P gear. The driver switched to P gear several times but didn't know this, which is a failure to read the car's user manual carefully.

Today, it's easier than ever to access relevant usage information. Simple information only requires basic reading comprehension, and then it's just about using it.

Believe that you can learn anything you want. It's not just motivational talk—it's a fact, and most people just don't believe it. How to learn? Just read the user manual carefully.

Always remember the existence of the user manual. Keep developing and evolving it.

*Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* is a book I've focused on reading in the past two years. It's an interesting phenomenon, especially in developed countries, where men are expected to know how to fix things, as labor is expensive. They are trained in practical skills by their environment. Some people even enjoy fixing things, like the author of this book.

The process of repair requires reading the user manual, sometimes repeatedly. During the repair process, the author explored philosophy more deeply. It gives us a path to explore a better life, which is very interesting and has some reading difficulty. But don't worry, we understand the secret of reading. Read it through first, and then some things will become clearer.
