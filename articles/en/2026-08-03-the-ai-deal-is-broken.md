# The AI Deal Is Broken

> 发布日期:2026-08-03 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-03-AI推不动是因为交易坏了.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-03-the-ai-deal-is-broken.md)

---

The strangest thing about AI at work is not that some people resist it.

The strange thing is that we expected them not to.

If a company says, "Here is a tool that will make you faster," the first question in a normal human mind is not technical. It is not "Which model is this?" or "Does it support file upload?" or "Can it call tools?"

The first question is: Faster for whom?

That question sounds small, almost impolite. But it is probably the center of the whole AI adoption problem.

In 2026, WRITER and Workplace Intelligence surveyed 2,400 knowledge workers, split evenly between C-suite leaders and employees. The results were uncomfortable: 97% of employees said AI benefited them personally, but only 23% of companies reported significant ROI from AI agents. Even more striking, 29% of employees, including 44% of Gen Z, admitted to sabotaging their company's AI strategy in some form, such as using unapproved tools, putting company information into public tools, or refusing to use AI.[^1]

It is easy to read that and think: these employees are irrational, afraid, or behind the times.

Some of them may be.

But a better reading is this: the tool has arrived before the deal has been renegotiated.

## The Technology Works, the Contract Doesn't

AI is not failing because people cannot see its usefulness.

ADP Research's People at Work 2026 data says half of workers globally use AI multiple times a week, and one in five use it almost daily. In China, the numbers are much higher: 73% of workers use AI multiple times a week, and 26% use it nearly every day.[^2]

That does not sound like rejection. It sounds like adoption moving faster than management language can handle.

The person at the desk already knows AI helps. It can draft, translate, summarize, compare, search, plan, debug, simulate, and explain. It can turn a blank page into a first version. It can make a mediocre worker look competent and a strong worker move at a speed that would have looked ridiculous two years ago.

The problem begins when the company translates that speed into a one-sided contract:

You finish faster, so we give you more work.

You learn AI, so your old skill premium disappears.

You become productive, so the team can be smaller.

You share your workflow, so your method can be standardized, audited, and eventually detached from you.

Then people start doing a very human calculation. If I reveal exactly how much faster I have become, do I gain status, pay, autonomy, and better work? Or do I simply lose the buffer that used to protect me?

This is why "AI adoption" is the wrong phrase. Adoption makes it sound like the problem is whether people will click the button.

The real problem is absorption. Can the organization absorb the new capability without breaking trust?

## Shadow AI Is a Symptom

One of the most revealing phrases in this whole debate is "shadow AI."

It usually means employees using personal or unapproved AI tools outside official channels. From a security perspective, this is a real problem. Company data should not be pasted casually into public systems. Regulated work needs governance. Sensitive customer information needs protection.

But if we stop there, we miss the signal.

Shadow AI often exists because the official tool is too slow, too generic, too locked down, too disconnected from the actual workflow, or too obviously designed for control rather than help.

The employee does not wake up wanting a governance violation. They wake up wanting to finish the work.

So they use the tool that remembers context, responds quickly, accepts messy inputs, and helps them think. They do not wait for an internal portal that requires six permissions, forgets the business background, blocks half the useful tasks, and then reports usage to a dashboard nobody trusts.

This is the same pattern we have seen many times before. When the official road is blocked, people build desire paths. The worn path across the grass is not merely a violation of the architect's plan. It is evidence that the plan ignored how people actually move.

Shadow AI is a desire path.

The right response is not to romanticize it. It can be unsafe. It can leak data. It can produce inconsistent quality. It can create legal and compliance risk.

But the wrong response is to see only disobedience.

A good manager should ask: What job is the forbidden tool doing better than our approved system? What friction are people avoiding? What fear makes them hide the most useful parts of their work?

Because the hidden workflow is often the real workflow.

## Wage Compression Is the Quiet Story

The deepest anxiety around AI is not always replacement.

Sometimes it is compression.

Apollo published an analysis in July 2026 arguing that workers in AI-exposed occupations were seeing slower wage growth while employment levels remained broadly unchanged. The authors compared 321 occupations using Anthropic's Economic Index and labor data, and reported that high-exposure occupations saw real wage growth 6.7 percentage points lower than low-exposure occupations after 2023, without a detectable employment decline.[^3]

This is not the Hollywood version of automation.

In the Hollywood version, the machine enters, the worker exits, and everyone knows what happened.

The quieter version is harder to notice. You keep the job. Your tasks become faster. Your output expectation rises. Your raise becomes smaller. Your old expertise is treated as less scarce. The company's margin improves, but the person doing the adapting cannot quite point to the moment the deal changed.

That kind of change creates a special resentment because it is hard to protest clearly.

If you say, "I don't want to use AI," you look backward.

If you use AI well, you may train the organization to expect more from you for the same money.

If you hide your use of AI, you protect your buffer but increase risk.

If you share everything, you may help build a system that makes your own advantage less valuable.

None of these choices are morally pure. They are just the choices people make when incentives are unclear.

This is why the corporate story "AI will free people for higher-value work" is not enough. It may be true in some places. It is also often unproven. The worker hears a promise about higher-value work, then sees a calendar with more tasks, a team with fewer people, and a performance system that still measures yesterday's outputs.

You cannot ask people to trust a future that has not been designed.

## Use AI to Make People Stronger

There is a simple test for whether an AI strategy is serious:

After AI enters the workflow, do the humans become stronger, or merely more extractable?

Stronger means they gain better judgment, more leverage, more optionality, clearer feedback, and a larger surface area for meaningful work.

More extractable means their tacit knowledge is harvested, their pace is increased, their discretion is reduced, and their reward is delayed or blurred.

The same tool can do either.

A coding assistant can help a junior developer understand a codebase faster, or it can turn them into a ticket-closing machine that never develops taste.

A writing assistant can help a teacher refine explanations, or it can flood the classroom with generic worksheets.

A customer service agent can remove repetitive burden, or it can become a surveillance layer that turns every human pause into a productivity defect.

The moral quality is not inside the model. It is in the arrangement around the model.

This is where many AI strategies are still immature. They focus on access, training, model selection, security rules, and ROI dashboards. Those matter. But they are not the whole system.

The missing layer is the social contract:

If a person becomes 30% faster, what happens?

Do they get better work, or just more work?

Do they get time to learn, or is every saved minute immediately consumed?

Are AI improvements visible in promotion and compensation, or only in management expectations?

Can workers admit that a tool helped them without making themselves seem replaceable?

Can they refuse a bad automation without being labeled anti-progress?

Until these questions are answered, employees will answer them privately. Some will become super-users. Some will hide. Some will resist. Some will comply on the surface and protect themselves underneath.

That is not a tooling failure. It is a broken deal.

## The Manager's New Job

The old managerial instinct is to make people adopt the approved tool.

The new managerial job is to make it rational for people to reveal how work is actually changing.

That means several things.

First, reward disclosure. If someone finds a better AI workflow, sharing it should increase their status, not erase their advantage. Otherwise the smartest people will keep the best methods private.

Second, separate safety from control. Data protection is real. But if every security rule feels like a productivity punishment, people will route around it. A safe tool must also be good enough to use.

Third, redesign work instead of stuffing AI into old scoreboards. If a task becomes faster, the answer is not automatically "do more of the same." Sometimes the answer is deeper work, more customer contact, better documentation, higher quality, or more experimentation.

Fourth, protect learning time. AI changes the shape of competence. People need time to compare outputs, build judgment, test workflows, and understand failure modes. Treating learning as unpaid overtime is a fast way to create resentment.

Finally, be honest about distribution. If AI savings all flow upward, people will notice. They may not use economic language, but they will feel the deal becoming unfair.

The future of work will not be decided only by the best model.

It will be decided by which organizations can turn AI from a private advantage into a shared system without punishing the people who discovered the advantage first.

## The Real Choice

I do not think the answer is to reject AI. That is a losing strategy.

I also do not think the answer is to worship adoption. That is lazy strategy dressed as progress.

The real choice is what kind of human AI is allowed to produce.

One version makes people passive. It asks them to follow tools they did not shape, surrender methods they are not rewarded for sharing, and accept productivity gains they do not participate in.

Another version makes people stronger. It gives them leverage, asks them to think more clearly, rewards them for improving the system, and treats their judgment as the thing AI should amplify.

This is why "AI makes people lazy" and "AI makes people powerful" are both incomplete.

AI makes the existing deal more visible.

If the deal is extractive, AI accelerates extraction.

If the deal is developmental, AI accelerates growth.

So when employees resist AI, the first question should not be, "Why are they afraid of technology?"

The better question is:

What are they afraid will happen if the technology works?

That question is harder.

It is also where the real work begins.
