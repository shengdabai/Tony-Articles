# Change the Verb, Not the Label

> 发布日期:2026-08-30 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-30-别改名改动词.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-30-change-the-verb-not-the-label.md)

---

Every boom creates a premium on nouns.

When a market begins to reward "AI", "robotics", "physical AI", "low-altitude economy", or "agentic workflow", people rush to put those nouns on themselves. Companies rewrite their annual-report introductions. Teams rename old departments. Individuals update their bios. The nouns change first because nouns are cheap. A noun can be edited in an afternoon.

But real transformation almost never begins with a noun. It begins with a verb.

The interesting question is not whether a company is now a robotics company, an AI company, or a future-industry company. The interesting question is much smaller and much harder: what can it now do that creates value in a different setting?

Can it sense? Cool? Control? Move? Certify? Deliver at scale? Reduce failure rates? Integrate with an existing customer workflow? Survive a hostile physical environment? Turn one-off craft into repeatable output?

Those are verbs. They describe causal capacity. They are harder to fake.

This is why the recent wave of "de-automotive" language in China's car supply chain is useful beyond the car industry. It is not just a capital-market story. It is a mirror for everyone trying to reposition themselves in the AI age.

The public backdrop is easy to see. China's 2024 Government Work Report named the low-altitude economy, along with biomanufacturing and commercial spaceflight, as a new growth engine. The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued guidance on humanoid robots in November 2023, with targets for an initial innovation system by 2025 and deeper industrial integration by 2027. A LatePost analysis, later reposted by Bianews, looked at 296 automotive supply-chain listed companies and found a broad rush to attach new labels such as robotics, embodied intelligence, energy storage, data centers, and low-altitude economy.

The noisy version of the story is that labels moved faster than revenue. Some companies changed names, formed subsidiaries, signed framework agreements, or disclosed future directions long before the new business became material. That part is familiar. When attention has a price, language becomes inventory.

The more useful version is this: the companies with a real chance are often not abandoning their old capabilities. They are moving old verbs into new objects.

A supplier that understands sensing does not become valuable merely by saying "physical AI". It becomes valuable if its sensor can work on a robot, pass the new reliability requirements, and reach customers who will pay. A company that knows thermal management does not become new because it says "data center". It becomes new if its cooling capability can survive the economics, standards, and delivery rhythm of server rooms. A battery company does not enter energy storage by changing a slide. It enters when its chemistry, manufacturing, quality control, and service system can be retuned for a different duty cycle.

In other words: the noun may open the door, but the verb has to walk through it.

This distinction matters even more for individuals.

AI has made identity inflation extremely tempting. Someone can spend a week with tools and call themselves an AI builder. A writer can call themselves a systems thinker after producing a few diagrams. A business owner can call a folder of prompts an operating system. A developer can stop writing code and start calling themselves an agent orchestrator.

Sometimes the new label is directionally honest. It points toward where the person is trying to go. Labels can attract collaborators, give a project a surface, and help other people understand the intended category. I do not think we should despise labels.

But a label is a loan from the future. The repayment is repeated behavior.

If the verb has not changed, the label is only costume.

So the real self-check is simple. Do not ask, "What should I call myself in the AI age?" Ask:

What can I now build, verify, teach, sell, maintain, connect, explain, or improve that I could not do before?

This question is uncomfortable because it removes the romance. It does not care whether we sound advanced. It cares whether our range of action has expanded.

For a teacher, the upgraded verb may be "diagnose": finding a learner's specific misunderstanding faster, generating better exercises, and seeing whether practice actually changed behavior. For a writer, it may be "argue": turning scattered notes into a defensible claim, then checking whether the strongest counterargument still breaks it. For a small business owner, it may be "standardize": taking a messy repeated task, making the steps explicit, and letting AI handle the boring parts while the human keeps responsibility for the outcome. For a developer, it may be "verify": using AI to create more attempts, then raising the density of tests, reviews, and real-world feedback.

Notice the pattern. AI does not erase the old craft. It gives the old craft more surfaces to touch.

This is a quieter and more durable way to think about cross-domain transformation. The glamorous story says: leave the old field, enter the new track, acquire the new label. The engineering story says: decompose the old field into verbs, find a new object, then prove the verb still works under new constraints.

The engineering story is less exciting at first, but it is much more useful.

Because most people do not actually need a new identity. They need a better adapter.

An adapter has three parts.

First, it preserves what is already real. Your accumulated judgment, customer sense, writing muscle, teaching experience, operational discipline, taste, patience, and scar tissue are not waste just because the market invented new nouns. They are the old engine.

Second, it changes the interface. The old engine must now speak to new tools, new channels, new feedback loops, and new expectations. A person who can think clearly but cannot express requirements to an AI agent has an interface problem. A company that has manufacturing depth but cannot describe its reusable capability for a new customer has an interface problem.

Third, it produces proof. Not a promise. Not a positioning statement. Proof: a working prototype, a paying customer, a repeated workflow, a public artifact, a measurable improvement, a lower error rate, a saved hour that stays saved next week.

This is where long-term learning becomes practical. Learning is not the collection of newer words. Learning is the steady expansion of verbs.

If you learned something, you should be able to do something differently. You can explain a concept with less fog. You can make a judgment with better evidence. You can build a smaller test before committing a large budget. You can notice a weak signal earlier. You can recover from failure faster. You can move a skill from one domain to another without losing its substance.

That is self-evolution in a usable sense. Not becoming a different person every season. Not decorating the same person with fashionable terms. It is making more of your existing capacity portable.

There is a strong counterargument: in fast markets, names matter. The right label can bring capital, distribution, talent, and permission. Without the label, nobody listens long enough to discover the verb.

I think that is true. A new noun can be a bridge. It can make an unfamiliar transition legible. It can buy attention before the proof is fully visible.

But a bridge is not a home.

If the noun never turns into a verb, the borrowed attention becomes debt. The market may forgive vague language for a while, especially during a boom. Users, customers, and reality are less patient. They eventually ask the same cold question: what changed because you were here?

That question is not cruel. It is clarifying.

The next time a new wave arrives, whether it is agents, robots, AI workbenches, new education, new media, or some word we have not yet learned, we can run a small ledger before changing the label.

What old nouns am I carrying?

What verbs are hidden inside them?

Which new object has real demand, not just attention?

What is the smallest proof that the verb still works there?

What feedback will tell me to continue, revise, or stop?

This ledger is not glamorous. It will not make anyone look upgraded overnight. But it protects us from the easiest mistake in a transition: confusing the language of the future with the work that earns a place in it.

AI should make people stronger, not lazier. That means it should not merely help us decorate ourselves with better descriptions. It should help us migrate our real capacities into new terrain.

So, change the label if you must. Sometimes the world needs a new word before it can notice an old capability in a new place.

Just do not stop there.

Change the verb.

That is where the transformation begins.
