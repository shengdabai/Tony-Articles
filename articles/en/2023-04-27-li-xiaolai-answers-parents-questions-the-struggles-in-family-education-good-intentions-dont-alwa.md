**01**
**The Struggles in Family Education: Good Intentions Don’t Always Lead to Good Outcomes**

Last week, we focused on breaking down an issue: **the most difficult part of the entire family education system lies in the fact that when we try to explore cause and effect, we often encounter not a one-to-one relationship, but a many-to-many one.**
So we find that **when we try to explore cause and effect, the difficulty lies in the fact that if it's many-to-many, we can't give a direct answer**, which is our major dilemma.
Going a level deeper with this topic, when we try to explore cause and effect, many-to-many is hard to handle, but even if we simplify it, it's still difficult.
Why?
Because every action you take might lead to many different results, and it might also lead to uncertain outcomes. These outcomes could be good or bad, depending on your luck.
So what do I mainly want to talk about today?
It's the common dilemma we face as parents or teachers in the process of education: **good intentions don’t always lead to good results.**
What does that mean? It means **when you make a decision, you might be doing it out of good intentions, but that good intention itself doesn’t guarantee a good outcome.**
This phenomenon doesn’t just appear in the field of family education—it actually permeates our entire lives, and it's also common in society.
Let me give you a few examples.
I’m a Korean, from a minority ethnic group. Did you know that? Up to now, there's still something called minority subsidies in universities.
I went to university from 1991 to 1995. At that time, the monthly minority subsidy was two yuan. In a semester, I could get about ten yuan in subsidies.
What could ten yuan do back then? Basically, nothing.
So it was pretty absurd. There were several minority students in the class, like five or six of them. When the semester was about to end, and the minority subsidies were distributed, these five or six students would gather together to have a meal, and bring along one or two good friends.
In other words, even thirty years ago, that ten yuan subsidy was enough for just a few people to have a meal.
This policy was originally designed to support minorities and promote national unity.
Over time, this policy was no longer part of the Ministry of Finance's adjustment plan. So whether it was inflation or changes in the socio-economic situation, the amount of two yuan never changed, which led to the situation I described earlier. This minority subsidy became almost meaningless.
That's how it was. As minority students, we received this subsidy, and we were often envied by those who didn’t get it, and there was always some kind of resentment.
But that’s not a big problem.
Besides the subsidy, minority students also had a bigger preferential policy—extra points in the college entrance exam.
In the era when I took the college entrance exam, the city where I took the test was Yanji City, in the Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture of Jilin Province.

There are two types of schools in our area: Korean schools and Han schools. Korean students automatically get a 20-point bonus on the college entrance exam.

But that's not all. There's something even more extreme: **Korean students take the Japanese language section for their college entrance exam. If you know Korean, you'll find Japanese is simply easy as hell.**

The Korean language uses a purely phonetic writing system. As long as you can speak it, you can write it. Japanese might be a little harder to learn than Hangul, but the difficulty doesn't increase that much. It only has five vowels and fewer consonants than many other languages.

In other words, if a Korean student takes the Japanese language section on the college entrance exam, their score might be 20 points higher than if they had taken English.

So in our hometown region of Yanbian, the college admission rate for Korean schools is extremely high—high to an unreasonable degree.

Why is that? Because you have to think about it: **their college entrance exam scores automatically get a 20-point bonus. At the same time, they can almost effortlessly score full marks in the foreign language section (because it's Japanese).**

Meanwhile, students from Han schools take English for the college entrance exam. 85 is considered a high score, 75 is common. Korean students, however, take Japanese and score 97, 98, or 99. 95 is considered a low score. The difference is huge—by dozens of points. That's why the college admission rate in my hometown's Korean middle schools is so high.

So from this perspective, the country's support for ethnic minorities is really intense.

Okay, obviously this was a well-intentioned policy.

So, **please ask me, does this well-intentioned policy actually lead to good outcomes?**

I'm Korean, I've lived in a Korean area for a long time, but I went to a Han school for most of my education. I only attended a Korean school for a short time during early childhood. I went to elementary, middle, and high school in a Han school, and when I took the college entrance exam, I took English.

Once we get to university, almost every university has some Korean students from Yanbian.

Do you know what these people who grew up in ethnic minority areas and benefited from preferential policies face when they get to university?

**The results are pretty bad.**

Why?

**First, they don't study hard in high school.**

Because of the preferential policies for ethnic minorities: their college entrance exam scores are artificially boosted, and the difficulty of the curriculum is reduced. As a result, these Korean school students don't study hard in high school. They have no pressure and can just casually get into college.

**Second, their scores are inflated.**

They might not have been qualified for a better provincial key university, like Jilin University, but because of the bonus, they end up attending such a university. When their actual ability is insufficient, and they go to a good university, do they have pressure? Yes, they can't keep up. But that's not all.

**Third, they have to learn English and Chinese in university.**

Korean students can learn Japanese in high school and take it for the college entrance exam. But Chinese universities don't offer specialized Japanese courses for these students. So once they get to university, they have to start from scratch with English.

So you can imagine: **they never studied hard, their scores were inflated, and now they're in a good university. Now they have to learn English on top of everything else. Can they keep up?**

Not yet. Because they didn't study Chinese well in high school, their Chinese level is only sufficient for basic communication. You can go to a store, watch a movie, no problem. But once you want to have a deeper discussion, their ability to express themselves is almost nonexistent. They can't communicate clearly, accurately, or coherently.

So they can't even do well in exams.

You have to memorize a lot of material for exams. If they never memorized Chinese, and suddenly they have to take an exam in Chinese, how can their scores be good?

Not yet. This leads to a sense of inferiority.

You know, **when someone can't speak a language, the resulting inferiority is really hard to overcome.**

So in universities across the country, minorities who have their own languages all share a similar trait: they're easily irritable.

Because they perform poorly and don't do well, they're looked down upon, right? That's uncomfortable, but how can they argue with others?—they resort to violence.

I don't know about the situations of ethnic minorities like the Tibetan or Uyghur, but I know that the rate of Korean students being expelled or held back in Chinese universities due to fights is extremely high.

Among male Korean students who get into university from Korean high schools, the rate of being expelled or held back is astronomically high. That's a fact.

Okay, note that I'm not bringing this up to criticize the preferential policies for ethnic minorities.

I'm using this example to show you: **the causal relationships in this world are very complex. It's not as simple as you think. If you plant a good cause, you don't necessarily get a good result. Far from it. The longer you think about it, the less it makes sense.**

Good things can grow bad things, and bad things can grow good things. Although good things are more likely to grow good things, and bad things are more likely to grow bad things, there's still a certain probability that they can be different.

At the same time, a good cause can lead to many different results. So the causal relationships are extremely complex. When people discuss cause and effect, they need to understand the complexity of these relationships.

Did you all understand my example?

As for solutions to these bad outcomes, to be honest, Li Xiaolai doesn't know, and I can't come up with anything either. But I do know that "good intentions don't necessarily lead to good outcomes, and good intentions don't guarantee good results."

Let me give you another example. There was a news story recently.

The pandemic has kept people cooped up for so long, so travel spots have become somewhat popular.

In Chongqing, there are many mountains, and in the tourist spots, there's a group of people who do a job called "lifting sedan chairs," which is essentially carrying a sedan chair. You can pay to ride in a sedan chair up the mountain, which is a local specialty.

But then something happened.

Ainfluencer paid to ride in a sedan chair, and then thisinfluencer was cyberbullied. Some people couldn't stand it and said, "You have money, so you're special? Why do you think you can trample on others' dignity just by spending a little money?"

In the end, theinfluencer kept apologizing, and now young people are basically too scared to ride in a sedan chair anymore.

But do you know what happened to thesedan-chair carriers? They used to make about 5,000 yuan a month, but now they're basically unemployed. Because before, there were more than 20 people taking thesedan chair a day, but now there are only two or three. These people can't do anything else, so they're out of work.

So you see, when we discuss public policy, we also face this issue: **you have a good intention, you make a decision, but the results are very complex. This is one of the sources of the complexity in the formulation and implementation of public policy.**

**02**
**Don't Take Away Children's Ability to Solve Problems**

The same logic applies at home as well.
Let me give you an example to answer a question from this week's parent group.
Student question: I have two daughters, aged 8 and 3. My current issue is that when I'm at home, they compete for my attention. It shows up as them vying for my companionship, which disrupts many things from proceeding normally. For example: 1. The older daughter wants me to read a story with her, and the younger one, who was doing something else, sees this and starts crying, holding a book, demanding that I read to her first. 2. While I'm helping the older daughter with her homework, the younger one uses all sorts of tricks to get me to leave. 3. The older daughter can even see through the younger one's intentions and gets angry, even bullying her (mildly hitting or scolding her).
In summary, when I'm at home, they're constantly trying to prove and confirm who I love more. They're always interrupting me. If I have important things to handle, I end up hiding in the office to finish them before coming home. But staying late isn't an option either, as I still need to ensure daily time with the kids. I've also tried setting a fixed reading time where each child reads separately, but this only works when only one child is at home. When both are together, it doesn't work. I've also made sure to treat both children equally, without favoring either or taking sides, letting them handle their conflicts themselves. But I still feel the root cause is me—some behavior of mine must be making them feel insecure, so they need to constantly verify who I love more. How do multi-child families handle the issue of sibling rivalry?

There are countless solutions online about sibling rivalry, but I want to share my perspective, which is based on the phenomenon I just took a long time and a lot of detours to explain: "Good intentions don't always lead to good outcomes."

I have children, and not just one.
So, do my children compete for my attention?
Of course they do.

So, after thinking it over, what solution did we come up with? It might be very different from what you imagine. Our approach is: **Let them compete, we don’t interfere. You don’t even have to mention sibling rivalry—just if they fight over toys at home, we pretend we don’t see it.**

Actually, I want to tell you that if you've raised more than one child, you'll know for sure that the older one will bully the younger one.
Even if adults stop them, kids, before they're very old, already learn to hide it. They'll be friendly to the younger sibling in front of you, but as soon as you're not around, they'll sneak in and kick the younger one. Such things are natural.

And I want to tell you, **don’t mistakenly think that by participating in management, you can directly eliminate their unhealthy competition. In fact, your involvement might push them in a worse direction.**

For example, if the older brother secretly hits the younger brother and you catch him, you’ll scold him, right?
Of course, you might even hit him or punish him. The child can't stand up to you, so when you ask him if he was wrong, he'll say yes. But the consequences are complex.
First, the older child might feelunwilling to accept defeat (unjustly treated), and the younger one might feel proud.
You already know they might not be willing to accept it, and the younger one is still showing off, so when you're not around, the older one will look for an opportunity to retaliate. At this point, you can see how your involvement has made the older child even more cruel. That's really scary.

**There's also another side effect you might not have considered: you might be robbing the children of their ability to solve problems.**

Assume you don’t get involved. If you carefully observe, you'll see that the younger child is being bullied by the older one. But do you think the younger child will just accept being bullied? No, he'll think about it, he'll figure it out.
This time he couldn't win, so next time he'll try a different method to win. For example, he'll go to the scene first.
That's him thinking, **because he's facing adversity, he's encountering a problem, so he's trying every way to solve it.**

Isn't that exactly what problem-solving ability is?

So after thinking it over for a long time with my wife, we finally figured it out. **Fight for it, whoever wins is the one who gets it. If you can't win, you figure it out yourself.**

**If we step in to take justice, one day, when you go out into society, will someone take justice for you?**
No. So what do you do? Fight for it.

Another thing, please rest assured, **siblings are different. Don’t think that just because the older child bullies the younger one, he’ll fight back if someone else bullies the younger one. That’s a natural reaction. So there are many times when you can relax.**

So my advice is: **Don’t interfere, let them figure it out themselves.**

First, you can choose not to interfere. Second, parents should manage themselves well.
For example, the student who asked the question earlier described this situation: the older daughter comes to ask the father to do something, and the younger one comes to compete for attention, trying to disrupt the father's work.
If I were you, my approach would be simple: **The older daughter came first, so I need to finish her task first before handling the younger one's. I have my own principles, and at home, it's first come, first served, right? So I will definitely finish the older daughter's task first.**

Meanwhile, the younger one will cry and make a fuss. You cry, you make a fuss—does that solve anything?
I don’t care why, I’ll tell them I have my own principles.
Then the children will have to figure it out themselves.

Note that **don’t think that just because children face any problem, they can’t handle it. There’s a bigger principle behind it: when they face a problem, they’ll think about how to solve it. If it doesn’t work the first time, they’ll try again, and again, until they find a solution. This process is the best opportunity for their cerebral cortex to develop. You can’t take that away from them.**

Okay, that’s all for now on this question.
In summary, it's just two sentences: **1. When children compete for your attention, don’t interfere; let them figure it out themselves. 2. Parents should focus on managing themselves and having their own principles.**

**03**
**Don’t give children unthinking, overly cheap kindness**

## So I was saying today that I saw many kinds of family issues and parents' worries, all are the result of early overly cheap and thoughtless kindness.

In the era when I grew up, there was no moral pressure for parents to beat their children, including during my growing-up process, I was beaten with various tools, switches, brooms, bamboo switches, which were unimaginable to you.

But I've seen most children who were beaten as kids grow up not becoming psychopaths. They also didn't develop low self-esteem or social anxiety just because they couldn't stand up to it as children; even if they did, it's hard to establish a causal relationship.

However, over the past 30 years or so, in mainland China, beating children and corporal punishment have been widely condemned. **In fact, I don't think the core reason behind this is whether or not to beat children; it's the one-child policy. You only have one child, so even if no one condemns it, you might not be willing to beat them. That's the truth.**

A momentary glance shows that so many years have passed; those who were never beaten as children have grown up. Today, there are indeed many "infantile adults" in society.

Why is that?

It's exactly because of many rules that can't be established without punishment.

You don't mean children, even adults, I ask you, if running a red light and speeding don't deduct points, and if they don't fine you, then, can it be eliminated?

Adults' world, can we talk sense now? But even under the circumstances of fines and points deduction, and serious cases even leading to criminal punishment, there are still people speeding and running red lights. So what's going on?

**So the thing of rules needs to have a punishment mechanism.**

But I can't promote the idea to society that children must be beaten. However, I must tell you the truth: what I can tell you is that there's no way without a punishment system. You can choose not to hit or scold, but you have to come up with an effective punishment system other than hitting and scolding.

**Children who have never been punished or scolded for doing wrong things from a young age, how could they possibly have discipline?**

I'll tell you another particularly amusing phenomenon: for little kids, scolding them before the age of three is useless. Before the age of three, maybe the only effective way besides hitting is that I guess you can't find any other effective method.

Because before a child is three years old, he is not unable to understand what you say, but the way he understands your words is different; if you haven't established rules for him before he is three, how can you expect him to follow rules?

Before the age of three, where can you hit the child? Can you just slap him? You can only slap him on the foot once, and that's considered hitting him; you can only go that far.

So I purely look at it from the rational perspective, it's better to hit the child a few times earlier.

Humans' brains have a strange pattern: after adulthood, they tend to erase memories from age 3. Scholars have not been able to clearly explain why my brain has this strange mechanism.

You can't remember things from before the age of 3, but many behavior patterns established before the age of 3 will stay with you all along, which is my brain pattern.

Yes, so here's a particularly funny rational explanation:

> If you want to discipline your child, the earlier you do it before the age of three, the better, because he won't remember it. The key is that you won't be able to bring yourself to hit him, and at that time, scolding him won't work either. His understanding of language is in a different way. You can scold him and make him understand that it's wrong, or even hit him, since you just can't bring yourself to use force.

People really shouldn't mistakenly think that children cannot be scolded or punished. I really think that many people have been misled by this matter.

Of course I know that in your eyes it doesn't sound like a solution yet. But after all, having a solution is better than having none. To put it another way, **even if you don't agree with corporal punishment, you still need to establish a punishment mechanism. If you don't establish a punishment mechanism, you won't be able to help a person grow into someone who follows rules.**

There is a scholar in the United States named Thomas Sowell, who once said the following:

> Every generation's newborn is a savage's invasion into civilization, I must educate him before it's too late.

What does it mean?

What I mean is that many of the rules I abide by in my daily life as a modern person were not born with my genes, but were gradually formed over many years of human development.

For example, following traffic rules, do you think there were any 100 years ago?

Now not only do we have them, but they are also very important; these things have developed over many years of accumulation and have nothing to do with human genes. At the moment a child is born, he only possesses the most basic human genes, so he has no difference from a savage.

So I often joke: as you watch the process of a child growing up, you'll find that all the things he does all day can be summed up in one sentence: **frantically testing the boundaries of acceptable behavior.**

You see, it's like the invasion of savages on civilization.

So Thomas Sowell's next sentence says very well:

> I must enlighten him before it's too late.

I want to tell everyone that I am under certain pressure when advocating this view in public, because I know how society thinks, so I cannot tell you that my way is correct, and you must also do it that way.

**But I'll tell you a principle, that you must establish a punishment mechanism.**

**On the other hand, be cautious in using reward mechanisms**, most parents do well-intended but end up causing harm in this aspect. When children misbehave, parents say, "Do this as I tell you, and I'll buy you something as a reward." The children hear the reward and just go through the motions to trick the parents into giving the reward.

So once again, he did not receive education, did not develop the habit of following rules, and that reward itself canceled out all the rules. Therefore, pay attention: what I need is not a system of rewards and punishments, but a system of punishment.

**Do not reward recklessly; from a young age, let him understand that doing so is right.** Seriously examine every piece of kindness you have, seriously think about the possible side effects of every piece of kindness you have, rather than naively believing that kindness will bring good results—only then is true kindness.

In my family education classroom, classmates often hear me say: "I really can't bear to be so harsh with my own child."

In my eyes, what does it mean to really go hard on a child? **Hug them for everything, tolerate everything, pamper them for everything, give them whatever they want, comfort them when they cry—that's really going hard on your child.**

This is not only a harsh hand on one's own child, but also a harsh hand on oneself; think about it, what will happen in five years or ten years from now?

Therefore, one must be careful with every act of kindness, **being careful about the side effects of every act of kindness is the greatest kindness.**

This is the main content of my today's Q&A session, although I answered few questions, the underlying principles, if everyone thinks carefully, will be found to have many applications in daily life.
