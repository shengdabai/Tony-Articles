# The Proof You Use AI Is What You Stop Doing

> 发布日期:2026-09-22 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-22-真正用上 AI 的证据，是你停止了什么.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-22-what-you-stop-doing.md)

---

What did you stop doing after you started using AI?

I came across that question today, and it stayed with me.

Most of us can quickly list what AI has helped us do. Draft an email. Summarize a document. Generate an image. Write a piece of code. The list can sound impressive.

But ask what has actually disappeared from the old routine, and the answer becomes less obvious.

Sometimes nothing has disappeared. We still collect the same material, open the same documents, make the same decisions, and perform the same checks. We have simply added a chatbot somewhere in the middle. There is now one more tab, one more copy-and-paste step, and one more output to inspect.

That may still be useful. It is not yet transformation.

My judgment is simple: trying a new tool is additive; adopting it is subtractive. AI becomes real in your life when an old default action is removed, compressed, or moved to a better place.

## Trying is addition; adoption is subtraction

Imagine someone who prepares a weekly update.

The old routine is familiar: open several chat threads, find the relevant messages, copy progress into a document, rewrite everything into a consistent format, and send it.

Now add AI without changing the routine. The person copies all the messages into a chatbot, explains the required format, corrects missing context, checks invented details, pastes the result into the same document, adjusts the formatting, and sends it.

Typing may have decreased. The workflow has not.

In fact, a new kind of work has appeared: translating the old process for the machine, then translating the machine's answer back into the old process. I think of this as the overlay tax. It is the cost of placing a new technology on top of an unchanged habit.

Real adoption looks different. Perhaps the relevant updates are already captured in a stable structure. The machine produces a first pass from that structure. The human checks exceptions, resolves ambiguity, and approves the final version. Manual collection and reformatting are no longer the default.

The important change is not that AI wrote some sentences. It is that a recurring piece of attention has been released.

This is why a blunt question from a [recent conversation about consumer AI](https://a16z.com/podcast/whats-next-for-consumer-ai-josh-elman-joins-a16z/) is so useful: after a product enters your life, what do you stop doing? It cuts through novelty, feature lists, and impressive demos. It asks for behavioral evidence.

## A new machine inside an old factory

We have seen this pattern before.

When factories first adopted electricity, many did not immediately redesign production around small motors. They attached electric power to layouts inherited from steam and water power. The old shafts, belts, and arrangements remained. Electricity was present, but the factory still carried the shape of the previous technology.

The larger gains arrived when factories could be reorganized around distributed power: machines could be placed according to the movement of materials rather than the location of a central shaft. The technology mattered, but the redesigned system mattered just as much. This history is described in the classic paper [“The Dynamo and the Computer”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2006600).

AI is encountering the same problem on our desks.

We put a powerful model inside a workflow designed for a world without it. Then we are disappointed that the result feels like a faster version of the same work.

A [2017 paper on the AI productivity paradox](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24001) made the broader point: the full effects of a general-purpose technology often arrive only after complementary changes in skills, organizations, and processes. The lag is not necessarily proof that the technology is useless. It may be evidence that the surrounding system has not changed yet.

This also explains why buying a better model is often less important than drawing the workflow on paper. If the old chain remains untouched, more intelligence enters through a very narrow door.

## Stopping does not mean surrendering judgment

There is an obvious objection.

Must a useful AI tool always replace something? What if it helps us do more, do better work, or attempt something that was previously impossible?

That counts too. The old action does not have to vanish literally. The old constraint may disappear instead.

A creator may still research, but stop opening twenty nearly identical pages before finding a useful lead. A programmer may still review code, but stop writing routine scaffolding from scratch. Someone learning a difficult subject may still read the original material, but stop spending the first hour turning scattered notes into a usable map.

The default has changed. Attention has moved.

A field study of 5,179 customer-support agents offers a concrete example. Access to a generative AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 14 percent on average, with larger gains among newer and lower-skilled workers. The researchers suggest that the system helped spread practices associated with more capable workers. It did not eliminate the conversation with the customer; it changed how much effort was needed to reconstruct a good response from scratch. The result is specific to that setting, so it should not be treated as proof that every AI deployment will work the same way. But it shows what task-level integration can look like. [The study is available here.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)

There is also a dangerous kind of subtraction.

If AI makes a beginner stop wrestling with evidence, stop noticing contradictions, or stop forming a view before seeing an answer, the saved effort may be the apprenticeship itself. That is not leverage. It is skill debt.

The goal is not to remove the human. The goal is to remove the least valuable friction while keeping human responsibility where it belongs: defining what good means, checking evidence, handling exceptions, and carrying the consequences.

AI should take away a repetitive motion and return a stronger person, not take away practice and return a dependent one.

## Give AI one verb, not a vague wish

Here is a test I plan to use more often. It takes seven days and requires no complicated system.

Choose one task you repeat at least twice a week. Not “improve my work.” Choose something visible: prepare a meeting brief, process reading notes, answer a common category of email, or turn a rough idea into a publishable outline.

Then write the current process as verbs. For example:

- collect
- sort
- compare
- decide
- draft
- verify
- send

Mark each verb as either friction or judgment.

Friction is work such as copying, reformatting, deduplicating, transcribing, or retrieving known information. Judgment includes setting the goal, deciding what counts as evidence, resolving a real tradeoff, and accepting the result.

Now give AI one bounded verb from the friction side. Do not ask it to “handle the whole thing.” Ask it to sort the notes, extract the claims that need evidence, compare two versions, or turn a voice memo into a structured outline.

Before starting, write down three things:

- the old action you expect to stop doing;
- the quality check that must remain;
- the ability you still want to practice yourself.

Run that version for seven days. At the end, compare elapsed time, rework, and output quality. Then ask the decisive question: did an old default actually disappear?

If the answer is no, you have learned something useful. Perhaps the model is wrong for the task. Perhaps the input is too messy. Perhaps the workflow needs a stable structure before it needs more intelligence. Or perhaps the supposedly boring step contains more judgment than you realized.

Either way, you now have evidence instead of enthusiasm.

## Reinvest the attention you recover

There is one final trap.

Even when AI successfully removes an old action, we can waste the gain by filling the space with more low-value output. Ten adequate articles instead of two considered ones. More reports that change no decision. More code than anyone can maintain.

Time saved is not automatically capacity gained.

The recovered attention has to be reinvested. Use it to speak with a real user, test the fragile assumption, read the primary source, practice the skill that still feels awkward, or sit with the question long enough to form your own view.

That is the version of AI I want: not a machine that keeps me busy at a higher speed, but a system that removes mechanical effort and moves me toward harder, more human work.

So before adding another AI tool, I want to ask two questions.

If this works, what will I stop doing?

And with the attention I get back, what will I finally become able to do?
