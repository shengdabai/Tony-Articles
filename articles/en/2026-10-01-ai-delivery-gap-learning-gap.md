# AI Can Narrow Delivery Gaps in Some Tasks Without Ensuring Learning

> Published: 2026-10-01 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-01-AI%20缩小的是交付差距，未必是学习差距.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-01-ai-delivery-gap-learning-gap.md)

---

Imagine taking on your first job building a small tool. The program throws an error, so you paste it into an AI assistant. It fixes the code. The program runs, and you hand it over. A day later, the person using it says the result is wrong. Nothing crashed; the tool counted a category of data that should have been excluded. The next problem is no longer finding code that runs. It is working out how you understood the request and which conditions you never asked about.

A collected account of someone using AI to build and deliver projects makes the distance between fixing an error and being answerable to a user concrete. It is a secondhand account of another person's experience, not something I did myself, and I cannot treat its income or outcome details as verified evidence. On a task suited to the tool, AI might help a beginner produce something sooner. Will that person be better at judging a similar problem next time? Those are different questions.

My view is that AI can narrow today's delivery gap when the task suits the tool and the result can be checked, but it does not guarantee a narrower learning gap tomorrow. The second kind of progress depends on whether a person can compare an initial judgment of their own with the machine's suggestion and with what happens in the world. This is a judgment drawn from bounded settings, not an estimate of how often it holds across jobs or people. A record of completed tasks alone cannot tell us what the person learned.

## Why can access to the same AI move gaps in different directions?

One study followed the staggered introduction of a generative AI assistant at a customer support workplace. Using data from 5,179 agents, the researchers reported a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour. The increase was 34% for novice and lower skilled workers, with little change for experienced, highly skilled workers. One possible explanation in the paper is that the tool passes along practices used by stronger colleagues. The authors are careful about a further claim: they call the evidence that workers themselves learned suggestive. More output while the assistant is present and lasting ability after it is gone require different measurements. [The study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)

A different experiment asked 211 undergraduates to read about an unfamiliar subject and write an analytical essay. Researchers randomly assigned access to generative AI during the learning phase, then gave assessments without it. The group allowed to use AI performed better on the immediate knowledge test, and the difference persisted roughly a week later. How students used the tool mattered: asking it to explain a concept and asking it to draft the text were associated with different later outcomes. The paper also reports that students with stronger prior academic performance tended to benefit more. This does not establish that AI widens every learning gap. The students came from one college, the task was specific, and the later assessment covered about a week. [The experiment](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849)

It would be easy to set these results against each other and announce that AI either favors beginners or favors the already able. That would confuse what each study measured. The support study chiefly measures work produced with the tool available. The student experiment deliberately tests performance after the tool has been removed. The participants, tasks, and outcomes differ. Together they force a more useful question: when we say AI made someone stronger, do we mean they finished more today, or that they can handle more on their own tomorrow?

I would keep both gains on the table. When a task is urgent and the result can be checked, faster delivery at an acceptable cost is real value. But if the task will recur and you will again have to set the goal, notice exceptions, and explain tradeoffs, delivery is not the whole story. One score belongs to the task; another belongs to the person doing it. A strong score on the first does not fill in the second.

That distinction also helps explain a tension. A beginner may need examples more than anyone else. An experienced person may be better at spotting where an example stops applying. The beginner can catch up rapidly on today's output while the experienced person uses the same tool to test a wider range of ideas. That is not a fixed ranking of who is suited to AI. What matters is whether the task lets people compare, judge, and correct. Without that opportunity, catching up may be superficial. With it, even a slow start can become the beginning of an independent method.

## At what point does an answer become a skill?

Suppose someone asks you to summarize public documents. AI produces a tidy account; you check the spelling and send it. Days later, the reader asks why a planned capacity was described as an existing one. If all you can do is ask AI to check again, you acquired a finished paragraph, not a way to judge how certain the evidence was.

A useful learning step happens before the answer appears: write down a judgment that could be proved wrong. Does the number describe a plan or current use? Is a conclusion backed by the full paper or only by an author's abstract? Make a call, then check the original material and the AI response. Now you can see whether you missed a condition, confused units, or promoted a conjecture into a fact. Without an initial judgment, revision may amount to little more than moving better sentences into a document.

Think of tasting a soup while learning to cook. You can tell whether a soup someone else seasoned tastes good, but that does not mean you will know whether to add salt or reduce the liquid next time. Tasting it yourself, making a small adjustment, and tasting again lets you connect action to result. Your initial judgment in a work task is the first taste; outside feedback is the later one. This comparison explains why feedback needs to connect to your own action. It is not evidence that work is as easy to diagnose as a pot of soup. Real feedback is often late, ambiguous, and shaped by many other causes.

In their review of the original meaning of deliberate practice, K. Anders Ericsson and Kyle W. Harwell emphasize something more demanding than accumulated hours. Practice has to target a person's weak point, provide informative feedback, and allow revised attempts. In its strict research meaning, deliberate practice also involves guidance from a qualified teacher. When I propose reviewing my own work, I am borrowing part of that mechanism. Keeping a review note does not make an ordinary task a formal instance of deliberate practice. [The definition and its limits](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)

This is why a user's brief complaint can sometimes teach more than an AI assistant's long compliment. Users do not have to behave as you imagined; the obstacle they meet can expose a condition you failed to ask about. Yet feedback is not a ready made diagnosis. If someone says a tool is hard to use, perhaps the entry point is hidden, the instructions are unclear, or the need has changed. Accepting every comment at face value loses information, just as dismissing every comment does.

## Why can making your own mistake beat reading the right answer?

In a study of learning transfer, undergraduates read explanatory science texts and used different ways of studying them. Transfer here means taking an idea learned in one setting and using it to explain a problem in another setting that looks quite different. One group deliberately wrote a plausible but conceptually wrong explanation and then corrected it. Other groups generated correct explanations or found mistakes made by someone else. Of the three low stakes experiments, Experiment 1 tested how much students remembered. Only Experiments 2 and 3 tested cross domain inference; students who made and corrected their own errors did better on those questions. The questions named the relevant concept from the reading, so the result does not show that people would spot the connection on their own in real work. [The paper](https://www.sarahshihuiwong.com/_files/ugd/8b1a2f_bac9b3e68f954bcf8bad07d724c63fc4.pdf?index=true)

I do not take this to mean that more mistakes in real work are better. The experimental mistakes were designed, checkable, corrected soon afterward, and cheap. Sending a wrong bill or publishing an unchecked claim is no learning exercise. The useful piece is smaller: before a draft is delivered, write the tempting but incorrect explanation you are most likely to accept, then use evidence to take it apart.

For example, while learning to read a performance report, ask yourself whether rising page visits necessarily mean a product is more valuable. Write a plausible wrong answer: more visits must mean users like it more. Now find exceptions. A promotion can drive a one time click. A search result can attract the wrong visitor. A slow page can even cause repeated reloads. The point is not to perform clever wrongness. It is to find out which visible measure you mistook for the thing you actually wanted to know.

AI can help here. Ask it for counterexamples, missing evidence, and alternative explanations. That can train judgment better than asking for a polished conclusion straight away. But an AI generated counterexample may contain invented facts, and its criticism may miss the situation on the ground. A counterexample becomes a correction only when it can be checked against an original source or an observable result. Otherwise one elegant paragraph has merely replaced another.

## The strongest objection: we need to finish the job

This objection has force. People facing a deadline, newcomers trying to earn their first job, and small teams with a user waiting for help cannot be expected to reconstruct every answer from first principles. Turning each step into an independent exercise would make learning an expensive ritual. Plenty of work calls for a reliable result, not another proof that you can build it from scratch. If AI helps a beginner reach a deliverable standard and leaves more time for communication, checking, and looking after the user, use it.

Early startup experience offers a related warning against rehearsing a perfect product only in your head. Paul Graham describes how Stripe's founders personally helped willing users install their product and how the Viaweb team manually built online stores for merchants, discovering missing features through the work. These are firsthand or close range startup examples, not statistical evidence that every small business should copy their playbook. They show how participating in delivery can reveal information unavailable through imagination alone. If AI removes low value repetition, it might free up more time to meet users and learn what matters to them. [Graham's essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)

So I would not switch off AI and take a long, inefficient route merely to prove independence. A more practical division is to give it execution that is checkable, reversible, and clear in its consequences, while keeping decisions about goals, exceptions, and tradeoffs in human hands. Nor does every action need to become a lesson. For a one off, low risk task you will never need again, one reusable judgment may be enough. If you expect to rely on or sell the ability repeatedly, you have a stronger reason to reserve time for feedback.

There is another serious objection: feedback in real work may be unfair or slow. A support team can improve its resolution speed while missing quality. Writing can attract more readers without helping them understand. If you turn an outside number directly into a learning grade, you may learn to please the metric. I would ask three separate questions: Was the result useful to the user? Can I explain why it worked? Could I do something similar again under changed conditions? Leave any one out and the picture of learning becomes distorted.

## What can the next delivery leave with you?

The small routine below is my proposal, not a method tested in the studies above. It does not require putting the whole job on hold for a course. Before starting a task likely to recur, write one sentence predicting the likeliest failure and how you will check it. Make it specific enough for reality to contradict: the definition behind a report's number, a step in a website flow, or what a tool does when the input is blank. One sentence is enough. Preparation should not become an excuse to delay.

Then use AI as you normally would. Before delivery, compare its suggestion with an original document, an actual run, or a user's specific feedback. Record one useful gap. Did I ask the wrong question? Did AI give a wrong answer? Did the world change in the meantime? Where other people's interests or irreversible consequences are involved, check before delivery. Do not turn a user into an unwitting practice subject.

When a similar task comes along, reread that note. You do not have to refuse AI. First say what you will check, then let the tool help execute. If you notice an important condition sooner, you have gained something that can travel to the next task, even if AI still performs most of the work. If you always have to wait for the tool to supply the first judgment, you have at least located the step that needs practice. A count of completed tasks cannot tell you that.

Return to the small tool from the opening: it runs, but it counts the wrong data. The program needs fixing. The more valuable thing to carry away is a new question for the next request: which records should be excluded before we calculate anything? Think about the last task you completed with AI. Is there a condition you missed then that you would now ask about before starting? I would count that earlier question as real progress.
