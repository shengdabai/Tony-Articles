# A Feature Request May Name a Stuck Moment

> 发布日期:2026-10-02 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-02-用户要的功能，可能只是一个卡住的瞬间.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-02-a-feature-request-names-a-stuck-moment.md)

---

A customer asked Basecamp for a calendar. Taken at face value, the next steps seem obvious: draw a monthly view, choose colors, add drag and drop. Instead, the team asked when she had last wanted one. She had been at home when a client called to arrange a meeting. The available meeting rooms were marked on a wall calendar at the office, where she could not see them. What she needed in that moment was a way to see open slots remotely. This [firsthand account from the product team](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03) is a reminder to ask more about a request that sounds perfectly clear.

A secondhand account of a discussion about product work raises a related question today. If AI lets us try several solutions quickly, should we get straight to building? My current answer is yes to moving faster, once we can describe the step where the person gets stuck. The name of a feature is a clue the user gives us. It is not necessarily the problem. As making a feature becomes cheaper, the cost of choosing the wrong problem becomes easier to hide.

## How one noun can conceal a real difficulty

Calendar, dashboard, automatic summary: each sounds like a precise need, but each may already contain the user's guess about a solution. People reach for the tools they know when they describe a difficulty. Someone asking for a taller ladder may simply be trying to reach a book on a high shelf. A ladder and a software feature are alike in one limited respect: each is a means to an end. The analogy stops there. Permissions, habits, and collaboration make software considerably more complicated than reaching for a book. The comparison reminds me to ask another question; it cannot decide for the user.

The same Basecamp chapter describes a request for more complex permission rules. Someone had archived a file without realizing it would disappear for everyone else. After looking into that incident, the team saw that a warning at the moment of archiving could be a smaller alternative to a new permission system. The book contrasts a small change with a much larger project; it does not say the warning was implemented. It is one company's account, so we cannot know whether the same idea would serve every organization. It does show how the question changes the set of possible answers. The issue shifts from a broad question about who may do what to a narrower one: does the person taking this action understand its consequences?

I think of this as translating the request back into the moment that produced it. The user supplies a feature name; we recover the action, the point of failure, and the workaround available at the time. That does not mean distrusting users or claiming that designers understand their lives better than they do. Users know their own trouble. The designer's job is to help both sides describe it precisely enough. Ask what kind of calendar someone wants, and you may get a list of controls. Ask when they last needed to see an open slot and what they did instead, and you may see the work in progress.

This is why a problem cannot remain a broad wish such as “make things more efficient.” Almost any button can claim to serve it. Based on the account above, I would describe the specific problem this way: at home during a client's call to arrange a meeting, the customer cannot see which meeting-room slots are free. That gives competing ideas a common test: can the person finish that booking in that situation? The answer could be a calendar or a much smaller view of available times. Keeping the old workaround beside the proposed solution reveals which effort disappears and which new burdens the design introduces.

## The faster we build, the easier it is to mistake a detour for progress

AI may make prototypes quicker to produce. A prototype is an early version people can try, such as a reading tool that can already find a passage but lacks other features. Today's secondhand account raises this writing question; it does not measure costs for every team.

Imagine, explicitly as a hypothetical, that you make a reading tool and a user asks for automatic categorization. You could try versions with topic folders, search using everyday words, and an AI assistant that answers questions about a book. If you compare only which screen people like best, you might choose an attractive folder view without learning where they actually lose track of what they read. This is a hypothetical example, not a completed test.

Here is my proposed trial, not a research finding: write the experiment around one task. When someone needs an argument they read earlier, can they find the right passage without remembering its exact wording, and can they confirm where it came from? The prototypes would then compete to solve the same problem. Categorization might help. Search might help more. Perhaps the real gap is that the person left no useful trace while reading. If the tasks differ, three prototypes become three impressive demonstrations that offer little help deciding what belongs in the product.

In a [2016 retrospective](https://buffer.com/resources/top-10-learnings-growing-to-10-million-arr/), Buffer cofounder Leo Widrich acknowledged that the company had built features without enough customer conversation or meaningful validation, wasting effort. The team later returned to customer interviews, predictions that observations could disprove, clickable prototypes, and early working versions. This is a company's account of its own experience, not a controlled demonstration that the process caused its growth. It supports a narrower point: even a team with an existing product and customers can become too confident about what to build next.

Building quickly matters because it can expose mistakes sooner. But we have to say what we believed before the result can correct us. Otherwise, an unpopular version can be blamed on its colors, model, prompt, or promotion; a popular version can be mistaken for proof that the original difficulty has been solved. The most useful record can be short: who was trying to do what, how they did it before, what step we expected to improve, and what actually happened. When the tool or model changes, that record offers a basis for comparison. We need not start over with every new screen.

There is an uncomfortable symmetry here. The easier a feature is to make, the easier it is for the requester and the maker to go around the real difficulty together. One person sees the noun they asked for. The other has a visible piece of work to show. Both feel progress. Whether the user's work has become easier remains a separate question. A good-looking interface may have real value, but the interface alone cannot establish which part of someone's work it improved.

A trial also needs a reason to stop. Suppose several versions can all recover the passage. The next comparison is no longer whether the product uses AI. It is whether people can tell where an answer came from, return to the source when it is wrong, and justify the cost of maintaining the feature. Writing those conditions down in advance makes it easier to resist adding another feature whenever a new model produces a more dazzling demo. Without an exit criterion, a cheap prototype quietly becomes a long-term maintenance commitment. The criterion is a judgment about the task and its costs, not a fixed threshold supplied by research.

## Write down your explanation before asking the tool

How do we keep the first convenient solution from carrying us away? I want to borrow a small move from research on learning: write an initial explanation of your own, then consult an outside suggestion. In a [randomized study of 97 graduate students](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19931), researchers compared writing hints for faulty code alone, writing with AI available on demand, and writing a hint independently before revising it with AI help. The authors' abstract reports that the delayed-assistance group produced the highest-quality hints and identified some mistakes they had trouble finding alone. The measured outcome was hint quality. The available abstract does not establish that the students would later program better without AI.

Applied to product work, I would first state in my own words what the user is trying to do, where I think they are stuck, and what observation would change my mind. Only then would I ask AI for several possible solutions. I am not claiming this sequence has been proven superior for product teams. Its practical value is to preserve a starting point that can be corrected. If the machine's suggestions reveal that I never asked what the user was doing when the problem occurred, the next useful action is to ask about that situation, not to treat a polished solution description as evidence of demand.

A [study of transfer in learning](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full) adds an important qualification. Transfer in learning means using something learned to answer a new kind of question. Researchers compared repeated reading with retrieval practice: after reading, students tried to answer from memory and then saw feedback. Retrieval practice performed better on later questions in a different knowledge domain. But its advantage shrank, while remaining present, when another group reread and also received focused exposure to the key information. The researchers concluded that seeing the information needed for the task helped explain part of the result. The participants were students in learning experiments. Their results cannot tell us that a particular meeting routine will make a product team successful.

What I take from the study is more modest: “we ran an experiment” is not a magic phrase. A team can view versions repeatedly and ask which one people prefer while repeatedly exposing itself to the same attractive screens. What it needs to draw out is where the user became stuck and which change helped them move forward. Reviewing a football match offers a rough analogy. Counting laps says less than locating where possession was lost. Football has stable rules; users and product goals can change. The analogy points us toward specific actions, not toward a universal law after one review.

## The strongest objection: stop asking and let people use it

The strongest counterargument I see is that users often cannot articulate what they need. Interviews are vulnerable to poor memory and politeness. If prototypes are cheap, why not release one and watch what people do? I agree that, when risk is low, the change is easy to reverse, and the task outcome can be observed, letting people try something can beat another meeting. Asking alone will also struggle to reveal a possibility users have never encountered. The useful distinction is whether the trial takes place around a real situation.

Show someone three versions of a reading tool and ask for a favorite, and novelty may influence the answer. Ask the same person to recover a passage they read last week and need now, and we can at least observe their attempts, where they stop, and whether the final passage is the right one. That observation is imperfect too. The sample may be small; we may have chosen an easy task; being watched may change behavior. I would put the history described in conversation alongside the actions seen in use, without making either the sole judge.

There is another difficulty: the person making the request may be only one of the people affected. One person wants tighter permissions. The person archiving a file wants fewer confirmations. Others simply want to find their files again. If we reconstruct only the first speaker's experience, we may hide the cost imposed on everyone else. For a collaborative feature, I would inspect the state before the action, the warning seen by the actor, and whether affected people can find the file afterward. Those who bear the cost of a mistake belong in the trial. This is a check I infer from the archiving case, not a general rule that Basecamp validated.

Buffer's retrospective suggests asking how people do the work today and what happened before and after the last time they did it, instead of asking whether they would use a proposed feature and potentially leading their answer. Basecamp's calendar story shows that asking “when?” can sometimes get closer to the actual work than asking what the screen should look like. These practices do not settle every industry's needs. Safety requirements, legal obligations, and processes that must execute exactly cannot be settled by voting among a few prototypes. A need that is already clear and verifiable does not require a ritual round of interviews either.

I propose a decision rule that has not been validated in a study of product teams: can I rewrite the feature request as a sentence containing the situation, the blocked action, and an observable result? If I can, I can try the smallest reversible change and see whether that task improves. If I cannot, I should first recover an instance of what happened. This will not choose the final interface for us. It can help us check whether a sequence of quickly built screens answers the right question. It may be especially useful for a creator or small team with limited resources: each new branch may later need explanation, repair, and integration. Costs absent from the prototype may return during maintenance.

## Give the feature name back to the moment

This is something you can try today. Pick one recent feature request and, for a moment, write the feature name on the back of a sheet of paper. On the front, write when the difficulty last happened, what the person was trying to finish, how they worked around it, and what completion would look like. If you cannot answer, ask the requester to reconstruct one real occurrence. If you can, ask AI for a few small options that can be compared on that same task. After the trial, record the change you actually observed and what remains uncertain. Resist declaring a universal method from one attempt.

This requires a willingness to overturn the original solution. The customer may truly need a full calendar, or she may only need to see open slots from home. Asking about the moment will not deliver the correct design automatically. It will tell us which difficulty we have taken responsibility for. When I next hear a persuasive feature name, I want to see the person's unfinished action before I see the button.
