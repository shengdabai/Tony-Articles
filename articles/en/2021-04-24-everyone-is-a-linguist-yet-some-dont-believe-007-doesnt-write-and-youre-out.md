Introduce the book

The journey of a linguist is a journey of language learning. Now it's commonly said that the world is a smaller place. We don’t think much about hopping on a plane to another country or continent anymore. The most exotic places are now mainstream tourist destinations. Small business owners are crossing borders and language barriers like never before. The internet brings various languages and cultures into our fingertips. English, a hybrid language from the western end of Europe, seems to hold an unparalleled position in international communication. But cultural and economic dominance has never lasted forever. Are we not losing something by relying on the widespread use of English rather than discovering other languages and cultures? As citizens of this shrinking world, would we be better off if we could speak several languages besides our own? **The answer is clearly yes.**

Of course, Steve Kaufmann believes exactly that. Amid his busy life as a diplomat and businessman, he managed to become fluent in nine languages and personally observed some of the major cultures in Europe and Asia. Why aren’t more people doing this? Steve provides some answers in his book *The Linguist’s Way: A Language Learning Journey*. Steve believes that anyone can learn a language. He points out some of the obstacles that prevent people from moving forward. As a student and businessman, he used his adventures in Europe and Asia to describe the benefits of understanding language. He recounts his development as a language learner both abroad and after returning to Canada, and explains the attitudes that would enable others to achieve fluency in a second language. Many have taken on the challenge of language learning, but have become discouraged due to a lack of success. This book provides detailed suggestions for achieving language breakthroughs. Steve developed an online language-learning system.

Listen to the author’s story

Steve Kaufmann was born in 1945 in Sweden, to Jewish parents from the former Austro-Hungarian town of Prosztejov, now part of the Czech Republic. His parents spoke German and Czech. In 1951, at the age of five, his family moved to Montreal, Canada, where he grew up. In June 1962, Kaufmann quit his job in construction and hoped to find a ship that would take him to Europe in exchange for work. After three attempts to inquire, he found a job on the German steamer *Gerda Schell*, which had lost a sailor in Quebec City. Ten days later, he arrived in London. A week later, he took a ferry from Dover, landed in Ostend, Belgium, and then went to France. After living in Grenoble for a year, he continued his studies at a political institute in Paris, where he learned French.

After completing his studies, Kaufmann traveled across Europe, learning basic language skills in Spain, Italy, and Germany. He later joined the Canadian diplomatic service and began studying Cantonese in Hong Kong in 1969, achieving fluency in a year of full-time study. In the early 1970s, when he was reassigned to the Canadian embassy in Japan, he had to learn Japanese. From his role as a trade commissioner, he applied his language skills in business deals, living in Japan for nine years. Eventually, mainly in his later years, he began learning more languages.

“**The best way to learn a language is to absorb it through listening and reading. Listening and reading are so powerful. If you can read books, you will know the language. To understand a language requires a lot of time and effort. Engage with it extensively—many of these hours must be decided by you. I believe it’s best to work on understanding and vocabulary without the pressure of reproducing the language through speaking.**”

Kaufmann has spoken about his language-learning techniques and abilities at conferences. He also runs several social media channels discussing his language-learning experiences and technical advice, all focused on language-learning techniques and helping people who are learning languages. He is one of the founding organizers of multilingual workshops in North America. Kaufmann travels to learn languages and has been interviewed multiple times in various TV channels around the world in Chinese, Russian, and Ukrainian. He has been a regular contributor to *The Huffington Post*.

Kaufmann has studied languages for over 50 years. He advocates for complete immersion in the learning process. He strongly emphasizes absorbing language through reading texts rather than worrying too much about unfamiliar words, believing that repeated exposure will gradually help you learn them. Although he advocates using flashcards to remember difficult words, most of his learning time is spent listening to native speakers and reading texts. He especially enjoys reading books in the language of the country or region where the language is spoken. He is an advocate for older adults learning languages and points out that age is not necessarily a barrier to learning a language. He also recognizes that mistakes are a natural part of the learning process and believes that even with some errors, people can still be considered fluent.

Kaufmann started learning his ninth language, Russian, at the age of 60. By 2020, he had learned 20 languages, though his spoken and written abilities had reached a high level. He said he rarely writes in languages, and interacting with native speakers can be challenging when trying to use a language he’s not used to. Besides English, the languages he speaks include: French, Mandarin (Putonghua and Cantonese), Japanese, Korean, Russian, Swedish, German, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Ukrainian, Czech, Slovak, Romanian, Polish, Greek, Turkish, Arabic, and Persian. By 2020, he said that after learning Turkish, he now focuses on learning Arabic and Persian, spending time listening to Arabic TV series and Al Jazeera news, and reading books about the history of Arabic and Persian.

Talk about the book’s topics

The content of the book is rich, and we’ll focus on the most interesting topic: grammar rules, their importance, and how we should deal with them. This has always been a topic of debate in language learning. Some say grammar is a waste of time and we can ignore it. Others say you must first learn the basics, as if it were possible. I’m in between, hoping to get an overview of what to expect from a new language without trying to remember anything. Then, as I learn more about a new language, I like to occasionally refer to grammar resources.

**Grammar doesn’t teach me a language; the language itself teaches me.**

Grammar resources can help me notice things in the language, especially patterns that surprise me or catch my attention in some way. I think traditional language teaching overemphasizes grammar as a means of learning a language, but this is done incorrectly. It introduces complex explanations and rules from the start, then forces you to practice what you just learned. But all these rules and exercises are still strange patterns to us, and if we continue to read and listen in the language, they will become increasingly familiar over time.

Most beginner language-learning books contain 70% grammar rules and related exercises. The content on texts, learning materials, stories, etc., rarely exceeds 10% of most beginner books, which is backward. Learners need more interesting texts and vocabulary lists, and they need to focus on key basic patterns shown in the texts. This will help learners complete the lesson and hope to understand a large part of the text so they can continue reading the next one. Introductory foreign language books should minimize explanations, as these explanations are in a language we are not yet familiar with, and are usually hard to understand and remember. It’s better to highlight some key patterns or phrases in each lesson but minimize explanations, rules, and exercises.

**Grammar rules are just for reference.** Once you have a lot of exposure, even if you say a few words, you’ll naturally feel what is correct and what is not. You start using the words and phrases you’ve read and heard. Your brain is following the model of what you’ve encountered. Your brain is constantly adjusting, which is exactly what your brain does with all the phenomena you encounter in life. The process of your brain creating patterns to deal with uncertainty and novelty is what enables you to speak naturally and freely and fully engage with the language. If you rely on memorizing grammar rules, you’ll always doubt yourself. Even if you say something correctly, you’ll doubt yourself. You should rely on the language habits you develop through speaking. However, even when you’re speaking, you might sometimes realize you want to clarify certain usages. You’ll have some language-related experiences that raise questions for you. “Is it this way or that way?” That’s the best time to refer to grammar rules. You can flip through a short grammar book or just search on Google or another translation platform.

Of course, you’ll almost forget what you saw there, just like using a dictionary. But this activity, combined with continuous listening, reading, and speaking, will gradually improve your grasp of the new language. Therefore, the focus of language learning should be on input, especially comprehensible input, which will provide you with a growing database of words and phrases and a real experience with the language. Once you have the experience and vocabulary base of the language, grammar can provide useful reference points that allow you to gradually resolve your own language or other difficulties. But don’t expect to master grammar until you’ve learned many languages.
