# Before You Share a Method, Find Its Hidden Helpers

> 发布日期:2026-09-27 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-27-把方法交给别人之前，先找出隐藏的帮手.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-27-hidden-helpers-in-a-method.md)

---

Imagine that you have a smooth AI workflow: gather material, make a draft, check it, deliver it. You write down every step and give someone the prompts as well. They follow the instructions, yet you keep having to repair the result. Perhaps the problem is not a lack of effort. You handed over the visible steps, but you may have kept the decisions you make when information is unclear, the people you consult when something looks wrong, and the judgment to stop when the output is not good enough.

Two clipped summaries about working with AI point to the same question: why might a tool that works for its maker fail as a method for someone else? One describes the gap between a tool that works for its maker and a product other people can use. The other points to the expert help and careful task selection that can sit behind a beginner's successful delivery. These are summaries of other people's accounts, not my own experience or proof of a general law. They suggest a judgment worth testing: whether a method can be handed to someone else depends less on how many steps we document than on whether we can identify, assign, and test the help its creator has been providing out of sight.

## You thought you shared a recipe; you also lent out the cook

A recipe makes the gap easy to see. It says to heat the pan before adding the ingredients. An experienced cook may read the oil, listen to what happens when food touches the pan, and adjust the heat. A first-time cook reads the same instruction but may not know when the pan is hot enough, what to do if it is too hot, or when to turn off the heat. The two people have the same sheet of paper and very different working conditions.

The recipe stands for the visible procedure; the cook's judgment stands for support that never made it onto the page. The shared mechanism is that steps describe how to proceed under normal conditions, while experience also helps a person recognize when proceeding is a mistake. The analogy has limits. Software leaves logs and can be reviewed remotely, whereas cooking often depends on immediate sensory cues. Software may also involve permissions, data, and handoffs among several people. I use the recipe to explain a missing piece, not as evidence.

Now imagine an AI writing workflow. Its creator notices a missing source and checks it; distinguishes a pleasing sentence from one that answers the reader's question; and knows what material cannot be made public. A new user may receive only four verbs: collect, generate, polish, publish. The process looks effective while everything is routine. When sources conflict or the material contains private details, the judgment outside the process is what keeps the work sound.

That is what I most want to ask of a success story: who narrowed the problem, prepared usable material, spotted the errors, and made the final decision? If we count all that assistance as one person's portable skill, the next person may think they merely need a better prompt. They may actually need someone who knows when to stop, or a task designed to expose errors early.

I am not asking for a huge manual of every bit of experience. An oversized manual can obscure the few decisions that matter. First make the support visible. Then we can decide what to teach, what a team must continue to provide, and what judgment belongs with the user. A method has a chance of leaving its creator only after its hidden helpers are found.

## Why more instructions may still leave a new user unsure

The usual repair is to add instructions: more prompts, a flowchart, a table of common questions. These can help, but they may miss the hardest moment. A beginner is often stuck not because the next step is missing, but because they cannot tell whether the situation in front of them still falls within the method's range.

[A survey of generative AI use in knowledge work](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) collected 936 examples from 319 workers. What participants described as critical thinking often involved checking sources, evaluating an output against the task's standards, and deciding how to integrate it into actual work. Participants with greater confidence in AI on a particular task were less likely to report doing such thinking. This is an association in a self-report survey, not causal evidence that AI has reduced anyone's ability.

The lesson I take is not that every sentence deserves the same level of scrutiny. Checking has prerequisites: you need an idea of what a good result looks like and access to something outside the answer against which to compare it. Giving a workflow to someone unfamiliar with the task and telling them to verify the output may merely rename the hardest step. The knowledge its creator supplied has not appeared just because we added the words “check carefully.”

There is a similar misunderstanding about practice. In [Ericsson and Harwell's account of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), the original idea is not to repeat an activity for as many hours as possible. A qualified teacher identifies a particular improvement goal, the learner attempts it, gets feedback, and revises the next attempt. In plain language, this means working on a recognizable error: for example, hearing where a short passage of music is unsteady and practicing that passage. The authors also distinguish this from purposeful practice done without regular guidance from a particular teacher.

It would go too far to apply music training directly to every AI task. Its conditions are different from writing an article, building a spreadsheet, or delivering software. I draw a narrower lesson: to teach judgment, it is not enough to display the successful final version. A learner needs to see a detectable gap and have a chance to revise. A handoff containing only successful outputs may erase precisely what the next person most needs to learn.

## What does one success actually prove?

Product builders have dealt with a related problem for years. In [Paul Graham's essay on early startup work](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), he recounts Stripe's founders setting people up as soon as they agreed to try the product. He also describes Viaweb building online stores for merchants by hand. These are the author's startup examples, showing that early service may contain a great deal of human work. They do not prove that every company should follow the same route.

What interests me is not how hard the founders worked. By helping people manually, they could solve an immediate problem and watch where users got stuck. If the story is retold only as “the tool was easy to use,” that helping hand disappears. A personal workflow has to cross the same gap before it becomes a product for others. Should the judgment that a person currently supplies become an interface cue, training, a human review step, or an explicit decision that this task should not be automated?

[Superhuman founder Rahul Vohra's account](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/) offers another angle. He describes asking users how they would feel if they lost the product, identifying the people who truly benefited, and using their feedback to change the product. The reported share of surveyed users choosing “very disappointed” was initially 22%; it was 33% after the team narrowed its target group, and reached 58% within three quarters of further product work. The account does not give the actual number of responses behind either 22% or 58%. It also says the team kept surveying new users rather than asking the same people again. These figures therefore do not track a change in the same group, and cannot by themselves show which action caused the increase. This is one company's account, not a universal target.

Still, it illuminates a neglected move: the team did not assume everyone handed the same product was trying to solve the same problem. Some valued speed most; others were held back by the lack of a mobile app. Feedback had to return to the actual context of use. Sharing a method raises the same issue. One person already understands the task and mainly wants to save effort. Another cannot tell whether the output is trustworthy. A third is handling material that should not be given to an AI tool at all. A single screenshot of success erases these differences and the conditions under which the method applies.

I would read a success story as a list of questions, not a warranty. At most, it shows that something worked for those people, with those supports, at that time. The next question is whether it still works for a different person, with different material, and without the creator's reminders. If it fails, did the training fail, did the product fail, or was this the wrong task to choose?

## The strongest objection: some work should be copied directly

The obvious objection is also a good one. Many jobs are simple enough to standardize: naming files consistently, organizing public materials, formatting content to a fixed template. They do not need to become training projects. Mature tools have already packaged up a great deal of expertise; a new user can get a reliable result without understanding everything inside. Making everyone repeat the expert's whole learning journey would be absurdly expensive.

I agree. A method need not turn every user into its creator. What matters is the cost of an omitted judgment and how easily a mistake can be undone. When it is easy to see whether the result is right, easy to reverse an error, and no sensitive information is involved, clear steps plus a spot check may be enough. Asking people to repeat low-value decisions wastes time. Removing that work is exactly what tools are for.

Other tasks are different. The output may read smoothly while omitting a crucial fact; an error may be passed on to someone else; or the new user may have no independent way to notice what is wrong. In those cases, faster copying makes hidden conditions easier to mistake for nonexistent ones. If the creator keeps quietly rescuing the work, the apparent success rate may look excellent while the new user's actual ability remains untested.

So the argument is not over whether methods can be copied. It is over what must be copied. For a simple task, a sequence of actions may suffice. For a task with forks in the road, the handoff needs decision rules and a route for asking for help. For a task where errors are hard to repair, it also needs clarity about who approves, who reviews, and when work stops. These are different responses to different costs of error, not a ranking of people or a vote for or against automation.

I also have to watch myself here. Once a creator tries to write down the hidden help, they may discover that they cannot yet explain their own choices. That is not the new user's failure. It is a sign the method is still taking shape. Instead of rushing to name it, I would keep a few specific failures and see whether my account explains them. If it does not, retaining human review is more honest than announcing that the method is ready to replicate.

## Let the creator step away while the tools stay

I propose a small, as-yet untried test of a handoff. It is a suggested procedure, not a research result. Start with a low-stakes, reversible task, such as turning a few pieces of public material into a short explanation for readers. The creator writes a one-page handoff: what counts as input, what counts as done, which sources must be checked, and who decides when sources conflict. Include only details that change what someone does.

Then have someone who did not design the method try it. They can use AI normally; the creator stays quiet for the first pass. Watch both where the person stops and where they fail to stop. Record the new questions they raise. If they deliver something that looks complete but is wrong, record where the process should have exposed that error. Review the result together afterward, and decide whether to change the instructions, change the tool, or add a human review step.

Finally, try again with material that is similar but not identical. Repeating the original item might show that the user remembers this answer without showing that they can recognize a changed situation. In [Andrew C. Butler's experiments on testing and transfer](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf), participants read prose passages, then repeatedly studied them or tried to answer questions from memory with feedback. A week later, Experiment 1a repeated the original questions; Experiments 1b, 2, and 3 asked new questions requiring participants to reason from what they had learned. In those latter experiments, repeated testing led to better performance than repeated study. Transfer simply means using something learned on a new question. This laboratory result informs my suggestion to test understanding with slightly changed material. It does not show that the proposed handoff test improves performance in real work.

The most useful outcome may not be a more complete flowchart. It may be a clear division of responsibility: which decisions the new user can already make, which require visible evidence, and which must go to the creator or another accountable person. At the next handoff, see whether requests for help decline and whether mistakes surface earlier. If both attempts work only because the creator silently corrects them, call it a service with ongoing support, not a method that already works independently.

The AI workflow from the opening is still useful. Before passing it along, though, I would ask myself one concrete question: when it runs smoothly in my hands, what decision am I making for the next person without their seeing it? Finding that decision may not let us automate it right away. It can make the next handoff less confusing. To me, that is closer to real progress than adding ten more polished lines to a prompt.
