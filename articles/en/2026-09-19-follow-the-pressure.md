# Don't Predict the Event. Follow the Pressure.

> 发布日期:2026-09-19 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-19-别预测未来会发生什么，先看什么正在逼它发生.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-19-follow-the-pressure.md)

---

Today I was revisiting an old note about the future, and two sentences kept pulling against each other.

Short-term events are almost impossible to predict. Yet some long-term directions can feel surprisingly clear.

My first reaction was resistance. How could the farther future be easier to see? More time means more variables, more feedback loops, and more chances for a small surprise to change the path.

The contradiction disappeared only when I noticed that the two sentences were not predicting the same thing.

One was trying to predict an event: what will happen, to whom, and when. The other was noticing a pressure: what keeps pushing the system, even when the exact outcome remains unknown.

That distinction has become useful to me. We often do not need a better prophecy. We need a decision that still works after the prophecy fails.

## The farther future is not easier to predict

Let me make the boundary clear first.

Long-term prediction is not generally more accurate than short-term prediction. A longer horizon usually creates more uncertainty about specific outcomes.

Weather offers a clean example. According to the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, a five-day forecast is accurate about 90 percent of the time, a seven-day forecast about 80 percent, while a forecast ten days or more ahead is right only about half the time. The exact numbers depend on what is being forecast, but the direction is intuitive: precise forecasts decay as the horizon expands. ([NOAA](https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/weather-forecasting/how-reliable-are-weather-forecasts))

The same caution applies when we talk about AI, careers, products, or learning.

Which model will lead next year? Which interface will become standard? Which profession will be renamed, split, or absorbed by another one? A confident answer may sound intelligent, but confidence does not remove branching paths.

So I no longer ask, “What will the world look like in five years?” as if there were one photograph waiting to be revealed.

I ask a different question: “What pressures are already shaping several plausible versions of that world?”

## Events wobble; pressures persist

When I build a small AI workflow, I can spend hours guessing which model will be best six months from now. That guess affects architecture, data formats, prompts, and sometimes the whole way the work is organized.

But the model ranking is an event-level forecast. It is fragile.

The pressures are easier to see.

Generated output is becoming more abundant. When output becomes abundant, checking, selecting, and connecting it to real work take a larger share of the value. Tools will change, but stale context will still produce bad decisions. A machine may draft the answer, but a person or organization will still carry the consequence.

None of these pressures tells me which model will win. They tell me what kind of system to build.

I can keep the model replaceable. I can preserve source links and decision records. I can maintain a small evaluation set based on real tasks. I can require a human check where the cost of a mistake is high.

If my favorite model loses, those investments do not become useless. In fact, they make switching easier.

The same pattern appears in creative work.

I cannot predict which article will travel far. I can see that the supply of acceptable content is rising much faster than the reader's attention. That pressure makes firsthand observation, evidence, trust, and a recognizable point of view more valuable.

It also appears in learning.

I cannot know which software will still matter in three years. I can see that interfaces will keep changing, so the ability to define a problem, test an output, explain a decision, and learn a new tool will transfer better than memorizing one screen.

An event tells me what to bet on. A pressure tells me what to become good at.

## Replace one future with several

There is a serious field of decision-making built around this idea.

Scenario work does not claim to reveal the future. The IPCC, for example, defines scenarios as plausible descriptions built from coherent assumptions, not as predictions or forecasts. Their purpose is to explore consequences and test whether a policy remains robust and adaptable under different conditions. ([IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-1/))

Robust Decision Making goes one step further. Instead of beginning with “Which future is most likely?” it evaluates a strategy across many plausible futures, looks for the conditions under which it fails, and improves the strategy around those vulnerabilities. A robust choice is not the perfect choice for one imagined future. It is a choice that performs well enough across a wide range of them. ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/tools/TL320/tool/robust-decision-making.html))

I like this shift because it turns anxiety into design work.

Suppose I want to build a personal knowledge system with AI. I could try to predict the winning app and move everything into it. Or I could test my plan against three worlds:

- models improve quickly, and the app stays strong;
- models improve quickly, but another app replaces it;
- progress slows, while my archive keeps growing.

A system based on exportable files, clear metadata, recoverable history, and replaceable model calls survives all three better than a system whose value exists only inside one interface.

I still make a bet. I simply refuse to make the whole future depend on that bet being exactly right.

## A four-line practice

This does not need a complicated framework. For a decision that is costly or slow to reverse, I now try to write four lines:

**The event I am tempted to predict:** What exact outcome am I acting as if I know?

**The pressure that persists if I am wrong:** What is becoming cheaper, more abundant, scarcer, slower, or more accountable across several futures?

**The move that works in at least three futures:** What skill, asset, interface, or habit remains useful if the event goes another way?

**The signal that tells me to change:** What observable result would make me revise the plan, and when will I look again?

Here is a simple AI example.

The event: one model will dominate my work next year.

The pressures: model capabilities will keep moving; generated material will remain abundant; verification and integration will remain costly.

The move: own the task definitions, test cases, source material, and workflow; treat the model as a replaceable part.

The signal: another model consistently performs better on my real evaluation set after switching costs are included.

The fourth line matters most. Without it, “following the pressure” can become another way to defend a fixed belief. A pressure is not a law. Incentives change. Bottlenecks move. Feedback loops can reverse a trend.

We should therefore ask for a mechanism, not just a line on a chart. Why does this pressure exist? What would weaken it? What evidence would prove that I have mistaken a temporary fashion for a durable force?

## Robust does not mean optimal

There is a cost to this way of thinking.

A robust decision may not produce the highest return in the one future that happens. A modular workflow can be less elegant than a system built entirely around one tool. Keeping an exit open costs time. Maintaining tests and source records feels slower than generating more output.

That cost is real.

So I would not use this method for every small choice. If a decision is cheap and reversible, try it and learn. The method becomes valuable when a choice is expensive, slow to unwind, or likely to shape many later choices.

The goal is not to remove uncertainty. That would be another fantasy of control.

The goal is to stop paying for precision we cannot have, and start investing in adaptability we can build.

AI makes this distinction more important, not less. It can generate ten confident futures before lunch. It can also help us test a plan against ten different conditions, find weak points, and keep a record of what changed.

The first use makes prediction look cheap. The second makes revision cheaper.

I know which one I want in my own system.

Perhaps maturity about the future is not seeing farther than everyone else. Perhaps it is knowing which part is not worth guessing.

Do not ask only what will happen.

Ask what will still be pressing on your decision if your favorite story turns out to be wrong.
