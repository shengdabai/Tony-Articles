On the weekend, I watched a movie, *My Sister*, directed by Yin Ruoxin, written by You Xiaoying, and starring Zhang Zifeng and Xiao Yang. It was released in mainland China on April 2, 2021. The film tells the story of a sister who loses her parents and faces the dilemma of pursuing her own independent life or taking care of her younger brother. I don’t know how to summarize this movie, but I have one feeling—real and complex. The main plot revolves around Anran (Zhang Zifeng), whose parents lied about her being disabled to get a son. When the truth is uncovered, her father beats her. During college entrance exams, her parents secretly change her major from clinical medicine in Beijing to nursing in her hometown, reasoning that “girls should earn money early to support the family.” Going to Beijing for postgraduate studies becomes the bright path for her to escape her parents. But her parents die in a car accident, leaving behind a six-year-old brother. Originally clear about her life plan, she now faces the difficult choice of whether to care for her emotionally distant younger brother or pursue her own ideals.

I took my younger brother to see this movie. The cinema was almost empty, and I couldn’t help crying a few times. Why do good movies move audiences? Because they create resonance—emotional, environmental, and intellectual. I gained a multi-faceted understanding of the sister character and became more rational in viewing the relationship between the siblings. I am also an older brother, and I am 12 years older than my younger brother, similar to the age gap in the film, but the nature of our relationship is very different. There are many differences between sister-brother and brother-brother relationships. A sister’s sacrifices reflect maternal love, but they also carry remnants of old ideas, such as gender bias. The relationship between siblings sparked deep thoughts in me, which is quite different from my relationship with my younger brother. Males often have playful fights, which help them grow.

I first watched the movie because I was drawn to Zhang Zifeng, who was taking the college entrance exam last year. She appeared on the show *Xiangwang de Shenghuo* (The Life We Want), where she got up early every day to study. Her efforts and dedication surely will pay off. I’ve always liked her—simple and pure—and her acting is also quite good. Especially in this movie, she transformed from the typical “national daughter” role. When her parents die in a car accident, she has to take care of her younger brother. Why isn’t this normal? It turns out it’s far more difficult than I imagined. If it were a brother, taking care of his younger sibling would be easier, and there would be fewer considerations. The sister is in a relationship, and she will eventually build her own family, which seems understandable. But the more important issue is that she doesn’t know her younger brother, and the plot escalates from there.

I gradually understood the plot and had some deeper thoughts about the sister character. Why don’t they know each other? It’s due to this special period—family planning. The strict enforcement of population control affected us too. We were fined for having a second child. Family planning was a policy during a special time, and even though the policy has relaxed, it’s still not easy to have a second child today. The cost of living, especially the cost of raising children, is very high, and the birth rate is declining. The number of newborns is also decreasing. Medical advancements will exacerbate the aging population, leading to changes in the social structure. For the sister, the impact of family planning is that her parents wanted a second child but were restricted by policy, so they reported her as disabled. When the staff checked, she was wearing a skirt, which exposed the truth. Her father beat her, and she developed resentment toward the new favorite child in the family.

The movie realistically portrays the various situations of people around her, highlighting many real-life issues. Many people want the sister to raise her younger brother, but she refuses, wanting to give the child up for adoption. As the plot progresses, their relationship moves from strangers to close friends. I also felt the outdated ideas in life and society—sisters should silently take on the responsibility of raising and caring for their younger brothers, even at the cost of sacrificing their own happiness. The sister’s aunt, who is the brother’s father’s sister, reflects the past sibling relationships. Her inner turmoil and sadness are palpable.

Did the parents communicate with the older child when a new family member arrived? When I was in elementary school, I learned that a new member had joined the family. I had no joy, only fear and worry. Would the love I received decrease? Would this new sibling take away my things? These thoughts kept coming to mind, and I was afraid and anxious. My younger brother still came, and through playful interactions, I gradually developed new feelings for him, almost like a parent. I chose to educate him in the hope that I could help him, and I only wish he has better choices!

Anran experiences her menstrual period, and her younger brother thoughtfully brings her red sugar ginger tea. The maturity is so abrupt that the only childishness is the use of a whole piece of ginger, which is a tool imagined by adults. The young actor’s performance is excellent, and to some extent, the expressions and tone neutralize the awkwardness. Still, it can’t fully mask the strong presence of the script and director, making the child feel like a small voice transmitter.

Once the story’s agency is fully given to the younger brother, the sister-younger brother plot becomes obsessively symmetrical. Every prop—laces, a soccer ball, a meat dumpling—is arranged with precise fore-and-aft correspondences. The younger brother learns to tie laces, and the sister learns to steam dumplings. Through these bonds, the struggle and hesitation about whether to raise the younger brother are confined to the sister-younger brother relationship, becoming a personal ethical dilemma in family affection. But the actual depth of this issue is far more complex. Even if the younger brother is a well-behaved child, the economic, academic, and future marital and childbearing issues the sister must consider when deciding whether to raise him are not helped at all. And that’s without considering legal, adoption procedures, and social assistance on a larger scale. There are two moments in the movie that subtly reveal the presence of unfairness. One is when Anran wears a skirt, exposing her not being disabled in front of her parents’ colleagues, and one of them leaves a comment: “Come with me for an IUD.” The other is after the sister-younger brother relationship eases, the younger brother asks: “Why didn’t Mom give birth to me earlier?” The sister briefly answers: “They couldn’t.” The younger brother asks again: “Why couldn’t they?” The sister is amused by his innocence, and the conversation ends abruptly.

Li Yanying wrote in her film review that *My Sister* “reflects the intense collision between individualistic values and traditional family-centered values in the process of China’s modernization.” This is a very common issue today. We cannot stop asking: where is the ethical boundary between an individual’s (not just women’s) pursuit of self and their responsibility to the family? Is there a true balance point in the gray area between individualism and family-centered values? These questions require ongoing public discussion and personal practice. This generation of women is destined to grow up in intense change and fragmentation, and thus, they need the courage to understand and reconcile themselves and to change the world.

I recommend you go to the cinema and watch this movie. I really like it!
