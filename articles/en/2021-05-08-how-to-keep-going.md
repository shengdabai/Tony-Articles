> A Casual Talk About Keep

> Keep adheres to three principles in product development, and to this day, these principles continue to influence the company. They not only permeate the product team but also serve as a reference for company decisions. These principles are: focus on the most, not the least; prioritize depth over lightness; and specialize rather than scatter.

Let’s start with the first principle: focus on the most, not the least. This means identifying the most common actions users want to take as the core of the product, and postponing the least common ones. Making such decisions at critical moments ensures the product evolves quickly. Most Keep users are fitness beginners, and the product can’t solve 100% of their problems. Solving 60-70% of the issues is manageable, but if we insist on perfecting the remaining 30%, it would spread the team’s focus too thin. Keep’s product strategy was to concentrate efforts on the most important user group and their needs, ensuring those needs were met effectively.

The second principle is to prioritize depth over lightness. The product should be built with enough depth so that users can feel light. It’s not about having many features that make the product heavy, but rather about the depth of the product logic and content. Keep’s approach was to build deep content and solid logic behind the product, not just a lot of features. This way, users can feel light—just bring their problem, search, and get an answer immediately. Whether they want to slim down or enhance their bust, they can get solutions right away. Keep’s product is deeply built with a large number of courses, but users feel it’s very light. They come in with a problem, and it’s solved right away.

The third principle is to specialize rather than scatter. The product should clearly communicate what it does—it’s a tool. From the start, Keep positioned itself as a “mobile fitness tool,” emphasizing its tool-like nature. For example, the Keep Community was designed so users couldn’t post any updates on their own at first. The reason was to encourage users to focus on their training. Every post had to be preceded by a completed workout. Over time, this helped users develop good exercise habits and also raised the bar for posting, filtering out some spam and laying a solid foundation for future community openness.

In addition, product and operations are inseparable. Without good operations, even the best product can’t succeed. Regarding how Keep launched without spending money to acquire the first batch of users, Wang Ning shared two key experiences. One was new media operations. Wang Ning said this was the “inner power” of product cold start. Keep’s Weibo account operated for about two years and had nearly 2.7 million followers. The key during Weibo operations was the speed of the operation team’s response, whether to hot events or user inquiries. The other was Keep’s “Burying Bombs Plan,” which was the “outer power” of product cold start. The “Burying Bombs Plan” refers to the preparatory work done before the product launch. First, they ran a “Chief Beta Tester” activity to identify a group of influential fitness bloggers on Weibo as core seed users. These users responded positively, and through the beta version, they achieved secondary spread, bringing in over 4,000 users. These 4,000 users loved Keep and became strong advocates once the product officially launched. Additionally, the Keep team deeply engaged with various communities, posting valuable content in places like Baidu Tieba and Douban Groups where users were concentrated. They also had Keep’s operations staff in various QQ and WeChat groups. Once all these were in place, the product’s launch was a natural and smooth process.
