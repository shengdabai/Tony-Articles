# The Fascination of Groupthink

I once tried to study the pricing models of different knowledge learning platforms, and there are roughly two types: one is the annual membership model, and the other is the course-based payment model.

For example, Chaos Academy, Fan Deng Reading, overseas platforms like Masterclass, and Mindvalley all choose the annual membership model.

Meanwhile, platforms like *Ge De* (a course-based payment model) represent another category. Interestingly, some of the annual membership platforms also offer individual course charges, which makes this model particularly intriguing.

During one of my readings about *Ge De*, I discovered that the company had once internally discussed whether to launch an annual membership model, and even reached a collective consensus at one point. But why did they ultimately not proceed with it?

This relates to an interesting phenomenon known as groupthink, a social psychological phenomenon in which people in a group tend to seek consensus rather than correctness, potentially leading to irrational decisions and behaviors.

Under the influence of groupthink, individuals may suppress their doubts and innovative thinking to maintain group harmony. However, in today's diverse and rapidly changing world, breaking free from groupthink and maintaining independent thinking is crucial.

Groupthink is not merely a psychological concept; it profoundly influences politics, economics, and culture. Under its influence, people may ignore or reject information that contradicts the group's opinion, which can lead to serious consequences, such as flawed business decisions, political misjudgments, or even social conflicts.

To break free from groupthink, the first step is to recognize its presence. Common signs include unquestioned authority, suppression of dissent, and isolation from external information. When you observe these phenomena, you should remain vigilant and be aware of potential limitations in your thinking.

A healthy team or society should encourage diverse voices and criticism. This not only provides multiple perspectives but also helps avoid blind spots in decision-making. As individuals, we should be brave in expressing our views, especially when they contradict mainstream opinions.

Cultivating the ability to think independently is key to breaking free from groupthink. This means developing critical thinking, learning to analyze information independently, and making judgments based on facts and data, rather than simply relying on group opinions.

Avoid relying solely on a single source of information. Diversified information sources can help you view problems from different angles, forming a more comprehensive and balanced perspective. Whether through reading, traveling, or communicating with people from different backgrounds, you can broaden your horizons.

Within a team, implementing a structured decision-making process can reduce the impact of groupthink. Clearly defining the steps and roles in the decision-making process ensures that everyone has the opportunity to voice their opinions and that decisions are documented and evaluated.

Maintaining independent thinking in a group is challenging but necessary. Only through constant self-reminders, courage to voice different opinions, and encouraging open communication can we avoid the traps of groupthink.

Every individual's thinking is valuable—collectively, they form a more colorful and wise world.

Overly cohesive thinking, where people agree more and more, is not a good sign.

Groupthink is a social phenomenon that also occurs in corporate decision-making scenarios. In company meetings, the ideal situation is for all participants to freely express their views. However, sometimes an excessive pursuit of consensus can lead to the suppression of real issues and dissenting opinions.

This excessive cohesion not only erodes individual judgment but can also lead to serious decision-making errors.

Imagine a company planning to launch a new product. In the early stages of product development, the management team held several meetings to discuss the product's direction and features. Although some team members had doubts about the product's market positioning, their concerns were not fully expressed or considered due to the company's culture that encourages consensus and harmony.

As the meetings progressed, more and more people began to express support for the product, even if they had internal doubts. The positive attitude of senior management and the emphasis on team consensus further weakened the opposition. Gradually, the entire team fell into a false consensus, believing the product would surely succeed, while ignoring the lack of market research and potential risks.

The company eventually launched the product, but soon discovered it did not meet market needs. Due to overconfidence in internal consensus, the company neglected the importance of conducting thorough market testing beforehand. Ultimately, the product's failure led to significant financial losses and damage to the company's reputation.

This case offers some insights: an excessive pursuit of consensus and unity can lead team members to yield to group pressure, suppressing their own insights and concerns. During the decision-making process, it is essential to encourage diversity and critical thinking to ensure all potential issues are thoroughly explored.

The case of groupthink in corporate decision-making reminds us that true team strength comes from honest communication and intellectual exchange among members. To avoid similar decision-making errors, companies should foster a culture that encourages independent thinking and open communication. In such an environment, companies can not only reduce the risk of erroneous decisions but also stimulate team creativity and innovation.

Reimagining Brainstorming: How to Break Groupthink While Respecting Differences

Team brainstorming is supposed to be an activity that sparks creativity and innovation. However, when it becomes a formality, where every new idea is immediately evaluated, it subtly introduces elements of groupthink.

In such an environment, participants may hesitate to propose unconventional ideas and instead tend to support those that seem more likely to be accepted.

To break this pattern, teams need to relearn how to conduct effective brainstorming while respecting individual differences.

The original intent of brainstorming is to allow ideas to flow freely, not to evaluate them immediately. Establish a rule that during the initial phase of brainstorming, all ideas are welcome, regardless of how bold or unusual they may seem. This "no-judgment zone" can encourage team members to feel comfortable sharing their ideas freely.

Divide brainstorming into two phases: idea generation and idea evaluation. In the first phase, team members focus solely on generating as many ideas as possible without any form of evaluation. In the second phase, they discuss the feasibility and potential value of each idea one by one.

Using anonymous methods to submit ideas can reduce the personal risk associated with sharing ideas, making team members feel safer in expressing their creativity. In this way, even the most unconventional ideas have a chance to be heard and considered.

Encourage team members to embrace and respect diversity, recognizing that different backgrounds and experiences bring unique perspectives. In such a culture, the team is more likely to appreciate and encourage unconventional ideas.

Consciously assign one or more team members to play the role of "devil's advocate" during discussions. Their task is to question and challenge mainstream ideas. This role helps prevent premature consensus and maintains critical thinking.

Groupthink can subtly seep into every corner of team interactions, even in brainstorming sessions meant to spark innovation. By establishing clear rules and a phased approach, we can once again make brainstorming a true catalyst for creativity and diversity.

Respecting each person's unique perspective not only helps break free from groupthink but also cultivates a healthier and more dynamic work environment.
