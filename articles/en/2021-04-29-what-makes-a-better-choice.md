A Flow Experience

Looking back on my university days, I majored in civil engineering, but in my final semester, I chose to enter the education industry and became an English teacher. Here, I analyze the situation of completing my graduation project using the flow model.

1. Setting a Goal: To complete my graduation project, create a BIM-infused architectural model, and submit it to a national design competition, thus giving a perfect ending to my civil engineering major.

2. Learning New Skills: During these two months of the graduation project, I was fully immersed, working from 7 AM to 11 PM, with only half an hour for lunch. I was in a state of flow, tackling one design problem after another, testing and refining. First, I learned the importance of communication. In my usual work, I tend to be dominant, taking charge myself, but in design projects, it's a team effort. With five partners, communication is essential. Even though I had already finished the model, I had to clearly assign tasks to others and sometimes help them solve problems.

Being content with solitude and able to endure. When in a state of flow, I paid little attention to the outside world. After completing a task, I felt a great sense of satisfaction. Sometimes, I would look up and find myself alone, with everyone else leaving on time. But for me, that was the only thing that mattered. Other things were secondary, and I could handle them during my lunch break. Sometimes I felt uncomfortable, wondering why only I was working so hard on the model. But gradually, I accepted it. This was my choice, my own standard, and it had nothing to do with others. Being present in the moment, enjoying the beauty of the work, and letting the flow of my mind run freely.

Third, setting clear goals. Often, I set many goals for myself out of dissatisfaction with the status quo, but given the limited time and energy, setting detailed, small goals is crucial. I also experience anxiety and worry, questioning why I'm not more efficient. Over time, I came to understand my own limits. A little effort at a time, and going with the flow, might be more helpful for me.

3. Establishing Internal and External Feedback: Internally, I often overlooked my own feedback, focusing more on external input. However, in this design project, I was constantly reflecting and adjusting. For example, I could use my professional knowledge to verify whether my design of a single floor was reasonable. I needed to compare and validate the internal layout to see if it was optimal, using my experience to continuously adjust. From an architect's perspective, I focused on aesthetics and spatial layout. From a civil engineer's perspective, I considered structural design, earthquake resistance, and coordination with the building. I also needed to think from the perspective of electrical and water design. But that's not enough. I also had to consider the user experience, the needs of real estate developers, and the regulations from government supervisors. I needed to analyze and think from many angles, using internal feedback to help me stay in a state of flow.

Externally, I also needed to refer to outside information. For instance, feedback from team members. Sometimes I was limited by my own understanding, but the help from my partners gave me new perspectives. During the design process, based on my limited knowledge, the guidance from teachers, online resources, and the advice from professional software engineers were all essential external feedback. Some problems that I thought about for a long time could be resolved with a single piece of advice from the outside. This was a continuous process of trial and improvement. I shouldn't blindly trust authority, nor should I dismiss everything. Beyond design, I also needed to consider human factors. For example, the grief from a family member's passing, the anxiety of entering a new field after graduation, and the pain of a breakup—all these external feedbacks had different impacts.

Keep going and cherish the moment. I'm grateful for that period of focus and immersion. Flow is very precious to me. In work, it's easy to feel lost and tired, and flow seems out of reach. This analysis has made me realize I can do better. I can enter a state of flow for each task, and by analyzing and thinking more, I can work better. Leaving the engineering industry, I'm grateful for the engineering mindset and the valuable experience and knowledge. In the education field, I'll combine what I've learned with the present, and push toward my goals. Keep going, Changchun!

Not an Ordinary English Teacher

Tony received a suggestion from a sister he met during university. She hoped his video content would have less of a how-to feel and more specific content, so that his videos could serve as learning materials for children's English learning. I really appreciate her support, but I want to say that I'm not an ordinary English teacher, or at least, I don't do the same things as most teachers. Honestly, her suggestion touched me, reminding me of my original intention.

There are many reasons why I became a teacher. I wanted to teach my younger brother, to share knowledge, help more people learn English easily, and improve my overall abilities. Today, I feel a bit nostalgic because I don't want to focus on detailed, specific teaching. For example, can I teach English phonetics? Yes, I can. If I do, each video could cover one phoneme, and with 48 videos, I could cover all the phonemes, and then move on to phonetic combinations. That would give me enough content for several months. Stop, I don't want to do this. I'm willing to share methods for learning phonetics and how to start phonetic practice. I hope to combine my teaching experience with the principles of learning and share them with others. More importantly, there are plenty of resources for phonetic learning, such as pronunciation methods. Bilibili's English Rabbit is very good. I don't need to create new materials; there are plenty of professional resources available. I don't think it's hard to find materials or resources. The real challenge is motivation. I want to help more people understand the value of English for us. For example, most of the world's information comes from English. If you want to get first-hand information, do you need English? I think the answer is yes. Maybe you think translation software is convenient, but after translation, information can be lost, and the accuracy is still limited.

What I want to convey through my videos is my values, Li Xiaolai's one-character advice: English is just one word—use it! But most people can't learn it well because they don't use it. Without the need, they can't learn it well. The more the need, the better they learn. If my videos are just about courses, bringing offline content online, and lecturing on what users might need, I think the long-term value is limited. Short videos give me the opportunity to reach more people and share good principles of learning, not just a lot of techniques. There are so many techniques, but learning is just repetition, right? If you're willing to put in the effort and have the motivation, how hard is it to learn? I don't want to create anxiety or show off my skills. I also don't want to use fragmented knowledge to attract people. I've watched many videos, read English quotes, do voiceovers, explain small points, and live-stream lessons. I still remember Li Xiaolai's words: why pay money to hire a teacher? Because we're too lazy to look up words. Teachers have already looked them up or know them, and they pass them on to us, encouraging us. If I can help more people get motivated and start practicing, and provide them with self-study methods, I think that's more meaningful.

I'm taking one step at a time to help more people "use" English. I'm slowly walking, practicing, and trying to find the right model. Honestly, I still haven't refined my content positioning enough, and I'm still exploring. I need more feedback to gradually improve my content. Keep going, step by step, and don't be just an ordinary teacher (ง •̀_•́)ง
