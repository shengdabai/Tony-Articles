**01** **Be Cautious with Reward Systems in Family Education, and Must Establish a Punishment System**

In our last Q&A session, we discussed: **Establishing a punishment system is essential in family building.**

This idea conflicts with the popular educational beliefs in our country for many years.

Many people may not agree with my view, but I won't just state my opinion—I'll also present the evidence supporting it, and you can judge whether it's reasonable and whether it sufficiently supports my conclusion.

**Establishing a relatively simple yet sufficiently comprehensive punishment system is a way to construct a realistic world for children.**

During a child's growth, we should not build a false world but rather expose them to the real world as much as possible.

This is a concept frequently mentioned in sociology, philosophy, and economics: **necessary evil**—*necessary evil*.

What is a "necessary evil"? Simply put, we distinguish between good and evil, but eventually we find that we cannot only have good things.

**Some evils are necessary evils, and without them, the world would become worse.**

A classic example is the rule of law. Living in a legal society is far better and safer than living in an illegal one, right?

Rule of law means governance through law. But what exactly is law?

Law is actually a form of violence, although violence is considered bad and evil. But we cannot live without law; in fact, law is an essential evil.

Imagine someone kills another person and commits a crime. Killing is wrong, but according to legal rules, he must be executed. This kind of evil is a "necessary evil," right?

Real life has light, darkness, goodness, and evil, just as we have day and night. The real world is like that, so **we shouldn't construct a non-real world for our children, a world without a punishment system.**

The reasoning is simple: if you want certain rules to be maintained and society to function normally,

would you rely on punishment or rewards?

The answer is clear: **if law enforcement is lax, then law becomes meaningless.**

If law becomes meaningless, then the rule of law is undermined, and civilization may be threatened, right?

So the real world is like that, **so in your family, you must simulate the real world to a large extent. That means having a punishment mechanism is essential, even though it's an evil—it's a "necessary evil."**

That's the first reason.

The second argument comes from a psychologist's experiment.

This psychologist organized three groups of students.

Group A received rewards for completing tasks.

Group B received random rewards after completing tasks.

Group C received no rewards at all.

Guess which group performed best?

The result may surprise you.

The group with random rewards performed best.

The group with no rewards came in second, while the group with rewards performed the worst.

Why?

Because when students know they can get rewards for completing tasks, **they lose their intrinsic motivation.**
**They complete tasks just to get rewards, becoming passive.**
The difference between active and passive effort is significant.

This is one of the core reasons why exam-oriented education often fails in the long run.

Earlier, we said that the group with rewards performed the worst, but the best wasn't the group with no rewards, right? It was just in the middle. The best was the group with random rewards.

You might think, as a parent, "I get it now. I shouldn't always reward my child. I'll give them random rewards instead."

Don't rush to conclusions. I'll tell you, **the best outcome might actually be no reward at all, not random rewards.**

Because random rewards also have side effects: they can erode a child's intrinsic motivation.

This experiment was conducted over a short period. If you extend the time, you'll find that the group with no rewards gradually improves.

Why?

Because without rewards, they always complete tasks.

What's the result?

Their intrinsic motivation grows stronger.

Random rewards may look good at first, but in the long run, they have similar side effects as regular rewards—they can erode your intrinsic motivation, especially when a reward is particularly large.

Think about it from another angle: **why do people believe in superstitions?**

Because superstitions offer occasional random rewards. Sometimes, even though the thing they believe in is useless, the expected result appears, so they can't tell if it's useful or not. Thus, they keep believing.

What's the best analogy?

**A broken clock that doesn't move at all. No matter where it stops, it's 100% accurate twice a day.**

You see, a broken clock can be right twice a day. So, occasionally being right doesn't mean much—sometimes a blind cat catches a mouse.

So, many things you see as causing the desired result are actually not the real cause.

Superstitions persist because of random rewards.
**Bad habits are the same. You know the habit is bad, but occasionally you feel the benefit it brings. The dopamine released in your brain is the same.**

So, random rewards seem good at first glance, but they have many side effects.

For example, they might encourage bad habits, or if a reward is too large, it can cause significant distortion. This is why people who buy lottery tickets and win a big prize often feel a sudden change in their lives.

But does their life really improve?

No, right? Statistics show that people who win big prizes through the lottery often end up unhappy. This is an extreme example of the consequences of a large random reward.

**So, both rewards and random rewards have side effects.**

**If a child can complete a task without rewards, they will strengthen their intrinsic motivation through repetition, which is actually a good thing.**

That's the second argument I'm providing.

The third argument is something only those who have attended our classes can understand deeply: **good family education must be based on production.**
So, when you, as a parent, reward your child, the things they receive are not the results of their own production, which is very concerning.

Education based on production has a key point: **everything you get must be earned through your own production.**

At first, you can provide some guarantees, but you must tell your child that as an adult, you also earn through production.

If my production efficiency is not high enough, or the quality is not good enough, and I can't earn enough income, I won't be able to provide these things for you.

This is the biggest difference between our education system and others.

**The reason is that I believe every individual is naturally a consumer from birth to death.**

Eating, sleeping, going to the bathroom—what isn't expensive? Even going to the bathroom isn't free.

Surviving in a modern civilized world means using public facilities like sewage systems, which are supported by the state through taxes, which are your money.

So it's actually quite expensive. Therefore, every individual consumes from birth to death.

**All the problems stem from the fact that if your production income is less than your consumption, you are poor.**

You see many so-called rich kids, but in reality, they are poor. Don't think they're rich just because they wear shoes worth hundreds of thousands, or eat a meal for tens of thousands, or buy gifts for millions.

You may think they're rich, but if you subtract their consumption from their production income, you get a negative number.

So, have they actually earned anything?

They're spending money that isn't their own production. They're spending others' production.

So, are they rich? They're just a big eater, right?

**If your production income is greater than your consumption, then you are rich.**

## Our entire curriculum system emphasizes this point: **One day, every child of yours will become a qualified producer.**

What does a qualified producer mean?

I don’t care what you produce.

Take Li Xiaolai, for example. He produces courses, books, and earns enough to cover all living expenses, with plenty left over. That’s great, isn’t it?

You shouldn’t think that Li Xiaolai’s writing and teaching are somehow more advanced. There’s an old lady, with only a primary school education, no MBA, named Tao Huabi.

She just makes chili sauce, and that sauce is delicious, affordable, and highly profitable. Her production income is way higher than Li Xiaolai’s lectures and books.

Do you really think Li Xiaolai’s teaching is more advanced than her kind of production?

Honestly, I don’t.

I’ve given many such examples before. There’s a lamb skewer chain store in Beijing called Fengmao, which we often visit. Their chain has even expanded to the U.S., with branches in Los Angeles.

Who started it? A neighbor from my hometown in Yanbian. When I was in junior high school, he used to sell grilled skewers on a stove in the alley below my house. Later, he saved up money to open a shop, then more shops, and eventually many stores across the country.

Eventually, he even opened branches in Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seoul, and Tokyo.

He also had only a primary school education, just like them.

Do you really think Li Xiaolai’s kind of production is more advanced than theirs?

So we say that education based on production is a reliable form of education. Because as long as you can produce, no matter what you produce, as long as your production income exceeds your consumption, you are a rich person.

But the problem is that from a young age, you give your children non-productive income. Do you think that’s reliable?

If children get used to receiving non-productive income, will they still want to improve their productivity? Therefore, **giving children rewards at will is actually depriving them of their productive capacity.**

This is my third piece of evidence.

To summarize, I’ve explained three points.

**1. In family education, you need to simulate the real world and have a punishment system.**

**2. Psychological experiments prove that rewards can deprive intrinsic motivation.**

**3. Giving rewards at will is destroying children’s productivity. As long as your productivity exceeds your consumption, you are a rich person.**

This is my view.

You can judge whether my evidence is sufficient and whether it can reasonably support my conclusion. That’s the core content I want to share with you today.

**02**

**How to establish a punishment mechanism?**

In my opinion, hitting a child should be done early.

The earlier, the better, before the age of three. After that, they’ll forget anyway. But the repeated pattern won’t be forgotten.

So the old saying that “children remember food but not punishment” has some psychological basis.

Rules should be established as early as possible. When you feel it’s difficult to establish rules, it’s usually too late.

Personally, I think hitting is better than scolding. **Because humans are especially sensitive to language, and as time goes on, children’s understanding of language deepens. Therefore, verbal abuse can cause more psychological harm than physical punishment.**

If it’s hitting, I think the earlier, the better. Once you can’t hit anymore, it’s too late, right?

But scolding should be avoided from the start. Criticism is fine, but don’t use language to provoke them, don’t label them, and don’t say harsh things.

Another method is to put them in time-out.

Many parents think that time-out causes psychological harm, like claustrophobia.

Why would you put a child in a dark place when you can let them stay in a bright room? Right? So time-out might be more effective than hitting. That’s my opinion, and I’m still exploring it myself.

But one thing is certain: **a punishment system is necessary, and scolding is the least desirable.**

**The main danger of electronic products comes from their gradual erosion of children’s attention and concentration. There are also a lot of psychological studies that have been maliciously applied in commercial contexts.** Sometimes we lament that there has never been such a large company with so much money specifically designed to make so many people addicted.

Of course, I don’t intend to criticize them. It’s just the way things are. **In this real world, every individual will do everything possible to earn more money, and the freedom of business is, in a sense, a form of freedom.**

So I don’t intend to criticize the short video platforms or game platforms for harming children. **In my eyes, the world has always been dangerous.**

The world has always been like this, full of traps, bad people, and bad things. The world always has some bad things that we need to resist. You can call short videos spiritual opium, but real-life opium and drugs are also things we need to resist, right?

It’s not hard for us. For example, in our home, there is no TV, no electronic products, no toys that light up or make sounds when touched. None at all.

I just want to keep my children away from current electronic products as much as possible during their growth. I don’t think they’ll be unhappy without them.

After all, we grew up without electronic products and still had a lot of fun.

If one day, **I think they should learn to use computers, I won’t buy them an iPad. I’ll definitely buy them a computer with a keyboard.** That’s also the result of my consultations with many programmers.

Many programmers are especially careful about electronic products in their children’s education.

Why?

Because the addictive games that hook kids are often designed by them. They know exactly what’s going on. So the programmers I know are almost all the same—no electronic products for their children. If they have any, it’s a computer with a keyboard and mouse, and they teach them programming languages.

So, the interest in electronic products is indeed a huge problem for this generation of parents.

You don’t even have to mention electronic products—TV is also a big concern.

During children’s growth, many parents find that their children suddenly start saying things they never imagined before. What’s going on? Who taught them? Basically, it’s the TV.

Because **children’s learning during their early years is random and all-around. They are curious about anything they see and imitate it, not just what you want them to learn. At this point, the information they get from TV is completely out of your control.**

Many parents end up giving in without thinking, like when they come home from work exhausted and the child is still crying. They know TV isn’t good, but once they turn it on and play an animated show, the child immediately stops crying. Just for a little peace of mind, they give in and let the child watch TV.

But this really has irreversible harm on children’s growth, and it’s hard to predict what kind of damage it could cause.

Especially the domestic TV shows are so terrifying, and the content on short video platforms is even worse. The values they promote are so unimaginable that they could have a huge impact on children.

So in this aspect, it really takes a lot of effort and struggle. One day you'll realize that when your child is constantly scrolling through their phone, it's really too late, so you have to be careful.

Is there a way to resist this?

Yes, **I'll explain this systematically in the family education community later, because this really isn't something that can be explained in a couple of sentences. But one thing is certain: you have to put in a lot of effort, and you might even have to pay some price.**

Li Xiaolai also watches TV shows, and I'm not not watching them, right? So what do we do?

My solution is pretty bold: I rented a separate house next to my residence to use as my studio.

I watch TV shows and movies there, and my home doesn't have a TV. The environment where my children are doesn't either. I have a lot of electronic devices, but I never bring them there. I do carry my phone with me, but I try not to use it in front of my kids.

Our family's basic education is exactly as we discussed in class: it starts with property education from the very beginning. The kids know from the start that the phone is my father's, the phone is my mother's, and without my father's and my mother's consent, it's not theirs to touch.

Sometimes they are curious and want to touch it or point at it, then they look at us.

If you really leave her alone, she might not finish her summer homework and go to school, and the school teachers will still report back to the parents. She doesn't care. Please, Li Xiaolai, is this sentence also applicable when doing homework?

We have a specific foundational concept called "responsibility." So **who bears the consequences, who makes the decision**, right?

As a parent, the key communication with your child should be: some decisions, although you bear the consequences, you can't fully assess the consequences now, so it's better to listen to your parents' advice.

This is one method.

Of course, it's very difficult to directly and effectively explain this reasoning, so a more effective method is to think carefully about why your child doesn't want to do homework. It might be that he has no interest in learning itself. If he had an interest in learning, he would do his homework even if you didn't make him. So don't assume that he doesn't want to do homework means he lacks the ability to take initiative in learning. Reflect on whether you might have relied on rewards in the past, and now rewards don't work anymore.

Another method: **take your child to observe the real world.**

On one hand, let him understand how what he's learning now will be useful in the future.

On the other hand, let him understand what happens to those who didn't study well in the past.

For example, take your child to see Beijing at four in the morning. At four, there are already many people working. Why do they have to choose such work?

Some are forced by life circumstances. But the social environment determines that people without higher education end up doing such jobs. We don't say which is better or worse, nor do we make any moral judgments, right?

Another example is taking your child to the countryside or to a construction site. These are all ways to help them understand the real world.

Of course, I can't offer a one-size-fits-all solution, because the current results are usually the result of many causes, and this is something I've emphasized repeatedly. So you still need to think about meaningful ways to solve the root of the problem.

**05**

**Should you force your child to attend interest classes?**

> Student question: My daughter is 7 years old and will start first grade in September. She has been taking painting classes for three years, but recently she doesn't want to go anymore. I agreed to let her stop. Previously, Li Xiaolai said that forcing is useless. I'm wondering if my approach is correct.

My view is this: as long as the economic conditions allow, you can sign up if you want. But I don't think many interest classes can bring you much benefit.

Why?

Signing up doesn't help; it's only when the child wants to learn that learning becomes effective. The most fundamental reason for effective learning is intrinsic motivation. If you can afford it, sign up; if not, just tell your child sorry.

I took interest classes when I was a child, like the basic programming class at the Youth Palace, which was just a summer interest class.

In 1984, my mother could give me ten yuan to sign up, which was already the maximum she could give. My parents didn't hesitate and gave it to me.

But later, one day I wanted to sign up for a model aircraft class. There was a classmate in my class who was always enviable. His family was the first one I knew with a personal computer, an Apple II. His father was an engineer at the power company, and he loved model aircraft. He had many aircraft models and an interest class. I was envious back then.

My mother directly said sorry. Those expenses are too much for our family. You have an interest, but it's not useful. We can't afford it. I told her, if I can't afford it, that's okay.

To be honest, you should emphasize your child's initiative, but you shouldn't fear your child giving up halfway.

**No child can maintain interest in something from the start.**

Often, having a wide range of interests is a normal condition for all normal people. Giving up on something is also a normal condition for normal people. Don't blame them, especially not by treating the economic loss as a loss.

In the course, we clearly explained that **except for necessary expenses, the rest can be told to the child as unnecessary** and that they can't afford it.

You might think, you decided to pay, so you use economic pressure to pressure your child. This is a very strange mistake I've seen many parents make.

Either don't pay, or pay. Don't say anything about it.

You also often give up, right? If you hadn't given up on learning something when you were young, you wouldn't be a master today, would you?

Why are there so few masters in the world? That's just normal people. If the child is normal, shouldn't you be happy, right?

So, in terms of interest classes, my general attitude is this: if the child is proactive, it's fine. But if the economic cost is too high, that's okay. It doesn't mean that learning something will be the only way to survive in the future. It's just one option among many.

Think about everything in a relaxed way, right.

There's no consensus on this. The best age for childbearing varies for each person, and there's no universally agreed-upon standard.

So, if conditions allow, it's definitely better to have children earlier.

Freezing embryos or eggs in advance gives you more options for the future. In my opinion, this is a good thing.
