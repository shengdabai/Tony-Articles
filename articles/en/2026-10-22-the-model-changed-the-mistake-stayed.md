# The Model Changed. The Mistake Stayed.

> Published: 2026-10-22 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-22-模型换了，错误还在原地.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-22-the-model-changed-the-mistake-stayed.md)

---

When a model costs less to use, it becomes easy to assume that the thing we lack is the next model. I think the most useful moment in switching models is when a mistake survives the switch. Perhaps the task was underspecified. Perhaps the source material was incomplete. Perhaps we never decided what a good result would look like. A machine generated summary of a personal talk about working with different AI models raised a related possibility: an affordable model may already be capable enough for ordinary tasks, while the user's own ability becomes the limit. That is the summary's proposition to test, not a measured verdict on the industry. The comparison method below is my proposal, not a research finding.

This matters in ordinary work. Today you might ask AI to write an event description, organize some information, or build a small website. The result looks complete until someone tries to use it. Paying for a stronger model may help. But if you cannot describe where the result failed, you may end up paying to repeat the same mistake. Here is my proposal: treat a model switch as a diagnosis. Write down the error first, then see whether it moves when the model changes.

## Give Both Models the Same Task and a Way to Judge It

Imagine making a registration page for a neighborhood swap event. This is a hypothetical example, not something I claim to have done. You hand the event rules to an AI model and ask for a page. The first version has a clear heading and a working button. Then someone asks what happens to an item that nobody takes. You look back at your materials and discover that the rule was never settled.

A more expensive model might add an elegant sentence. It might also invent a rule. Neither response settles the underlying question. The organizer must decide what happens to leftover items and what promise the page can make. In this example, appearance is one check. Whether the rules are complete and whether a participant can act on them are two more. If we collapse all three into “Does it look good?”, even an excellent model will struggle to show us where the trouble lies.

I would write a short acceptance note before asking either model to work: a visitor should find the time and place, what items are allowed, and how to register; unsettled rules must remain marked as unsettled; the page must be readable and usable on a phone. The note need not sound technical. Its job is to fix the standard before a polished answer tempts you to change it. Without that standard, the winning version may simply be the one that flatters your taste.

Giving two models the same task is a little like asking two students to answer the same exam. Keep the question, supplied material, and grading standard as similar as possible, and differences become easier to interpret. Real work goes beyond an exam, though. Participants ask questions you did not anticipate, and event rules can change. A desk comparison can catch visible defects; it cannot replace watching someone use the result.

There is another small trap: do not let the two answers copy each other. Save each model's first result separately before asking follow up questions. If you give one model the other's page to repair, they are no longer answering the same task. And after the comparison, do more than circle the prettier version. Write down which one you would publish and the single fact that made you hesitate. You may discover that the event rules, rather than the font size, need checking.

## Did the Error Move?

Suppose both models state an unsettled rule as if it were final. I would not immediately conclude that both models are useless. First I would inspect the input: did I distinguish confirmed facts from ideas under discussion? Then I would inspect the acceptance note: did I say that the model must not decide a rule for us? The same mistake in both outputs often points to the same missing instruction or missing fact. It could also reveal a limitation shared by the models. One comparison cannot tell us which explanation is right; we need better material or an answer from the person responsible for the event.

Now suppose the cheaper model drops the registration deadline while the stronger model preserves it, even though both received exactly the same information. That looks more like a difference in model capability or reliability. I would try another comparable set of materials before ranking them from a single result. Which model fits depends on the task, the inputs, speed, and the cost of a mistake. Public leaderboards can suggest candidates. They cannot approve your registration page.

The same error can also return in a new costume. One version presents the unsettled rule as a fact. Another tucks it into a vague help box. The problem did not disappear. Watch what a visitor will end up believing and doing, instead of searching only for repeated wording. Conversely, if both models ask you to confirm the rule, there is little reason to declare one a winner because its phrasing sounds more impressive. Both stopped at the right point.

You might object: if the stronger model tends to make fewer mistakes, why not always use it? For urgent work with costly errors, especially when you cannot judge the output yourself, that is a strong argument. Do not use real people and real consequences as a price experiment. Ask a qualified person to check the work and slow down if necessary. My method fits tasks that can be redone, have limited consequences, and come with basic facts and a usable standard. It does not aim to prove that a cheaper model always suffices. It asks when an upgrade actually changes the outcome.

A [survey paper published in 2025 by Microsoft researchers and collaborators](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) adds a caution. The researchers surveyed 319 knowledge workers who regularly used generative AI at work and retained 936 self reported task examples. Greater confidence in AI's ability on a particular task was associated with a lower likelihood of reporting critical thinking on that task. Here, critical thinking means actions such as deciding what the task requires, checking an AI answer against facts, and judging whether the answer fits the task. The survey asked workers whether they thought they had taken such actions; it did not directly measure their thinking ability. It found an association in reported behavior and did not follow skill loss over time or compare models. My practical proposal is to write a standard before seeing the answer, so “looks fine” does not pass for “has been checked.”

## Recognizing the Error Tells You What to Practice

Imagine that the page works technically, yet several people trying it keep asking what happens to items nobody takes. Another welcoming sentence will not fix that. You must confirm the policy and put it where a visitor needs it to decide whether to register. A model can rewrite the sentence. It cannot make the organizer's decision or confirm that decision happened. The person making the page is learning more than page building here: they are learning to spot missing information that changes what another person will do.

Two public startup accounts help explain why contact with actual users matters. In [Paul Graham's 2013 account of Stripe's early user recruitment](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), the founders helped people set up Stripe on the spot once they agreed to try it. He also describes founders using their own software on behalf of users and discovering missing features while doing so. These are accounts of founders' experience, not evidence that every project should copy the method. The transferable action is simple: let someone use the thing, so you can see obstacles that a comparison between models never exposed.

But building every feature someone requests can lead you astray. In [a case Steve Blank published in 2012](https://steveblank.com/2012/02/27/killing-your-startup-by-listening-to-customers/), a team added requested features and attracted people to try its product, yet paid upgrades and continued use did not follow. It is one case, not a measured failure rate. Back at our hypothetical registration page, “add a chat box” is a request. The more useful questions are where people abandon registration and why they need to ask a question at that point. Hear the request, then inspect the obstacle behind it. Otherwise AI merely helps you change the wrong page faster.

This also tells you what you need to learn. If the same question persists after a revision, I would practice writing rules and placing information. If the rules are clear but the form will not submit, I would inspect the implementation. If the page passes those checks and nobody registers, I would ask whether the event offers something people want. Each failure calls for a different next move. Blaming them all on a model that is not clever enough is convenient and expensive.

## A Working Page Does Not Prove That You Learned

A usable page is a real delivery. It does not tell us what its maker learned. The recording summary also expressed a wish to gain productive experience while making things with AI. I would make that wish testable: when the next similar task arrives with new material, can you spot an important gap earlier and name the fact that would settle it? If you cannot, the work may still be valuable. Just do not count the number of finished artifacts as a direct measure of your own growing ability.

In [their 2019 discussion of the original definition of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), K. Anders Ericsson and Kyle W. Harwell emphasize individualized goals set with a qualified teacher, informative feedback, and repeated attempts to correct a specific weakness. Making many pages does not automatically meet that definition. If you want to practice spotting a missing rule, isolate that error and try to find its counterpart in new material before the AI shows you a finished page. And without trustworthy feedback, do not treat a model's praise as a teacher's assessment.

There is a reason to use new material rather than admire the same finished page again. In a [2016 study with two experiments](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full), psychology undergraduates read short texts and studied them in different ways. Students who answered recall questions and received feedback did better than repeated readers on questions applying the ideas in another knowledge domain. When the researchers also showed the rereading group the key information needed for the questions, the gap narrowed. My practical reading is modest: attempting to find a gap yourself and then getting feedback may help a later attempt; seeing the crucial information clearly matters too. The experiments tested learning from short texts, not website creation, so they do not promise the same result for our page.

Checking has a cost that is easy to ignore. Suppose assessing a small task takes much longer than making it. Comparing models may not pay off. But if the same omission repeatedly affects other people, time spent checking may help you find a gap in your judgment. This is an example of a tradeoff, not a measured return on time. Ask whether the error returns next time, not only how much time you saved this time. A model that costs less to use lowers the cost of another attempt. It does not automatically turn that attempt into learning.

Someone could fairly say: “I need a website, not a lesson every time I work.” Agreed. When delivery is the goal, deliver. A strong model can be a sensible way to work faster if someone checks the consequences. The extra practice is for people who also want to build a skill, and only for selected tasks. Keep two records separate: did the artifact help someone do what they came to do, and can you make a better judgment when unfamiliar material arrives? Both can improve. They can also move in different directions.

## Spend the Savings on Your Next Judgment

To try this today, choose a small task you can redo without harming anyone. Before using AI, write down the facts the result must preserve, the action a user should be able to complete, and what the model must leave undecided. Give two models the same material. Record the most consequential usability error in each result; fluency alone does not decide the winner. Then let an actual user try the result, or repeat the task with new material. Which error disappeared when you switched models? Which one required you to supply a fact, change your standard, or understand the user better?

At the end of the comparison, I would leave a short record for this kind of task: which errors a stronger model might reduce, which ones require me to supply better material, and which decisions need confirmation from a person in the real world. The next time a similar task arrives, I would read that note before choosing a tool. It is not a permanent shopping list. Models change, and so do tasks. An old judgment needs another test with fresh material. Then the money saved can become an ability to spot the problem sooner.

Finally, reserve one error for your own practice. On the next task, before looking at AI's finished answer, try to find that kind of gap yourself and then check the feedback. There is no need to turn every email into an exercise. Low risk routine work can go straight to a reliable tool. Facts and promises that affect others need human confirmation. A skill you want to develop deserves a deliberate retry with feedback. As models become cheaper, we gain more than disposable answers. We can afford to ask one more question: whose change could prevent this particular mistake?

If the mistake stays put, change the way you work.
