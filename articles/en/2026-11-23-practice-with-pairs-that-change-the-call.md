# Give Each Practice Case a Neighbor You Might Confuse It With

On a math test grouped by problem type, students who had done more mixed practice answered 61% of the questions correctly on average. Students who had done more practice in batches of the same type averaged 38%. The test itself grouped similar problems together, and the order in which students had practiced still mattered. [The seventh-grade math trial](https://addvancemaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice.pdf)

In the Florida trial, researchers randomly assigned 54 seventh-grade classes to two groups and analyzed scores from 787 students. Both groups completed the same mixed review assignment about a month before the unannounced test. The trial measured the effect of doing more mixed practice in regular assignments. [Trial design and results](https://addvancemaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice.pdf)

When you pick the material yourself, which two cases should sit next to each other? Here’s a practice exercise I’d try in video editing. Put two cases together when you’re likely to mistake them for the same problem even though they call for different actions. For each case, name the piece of evidence that would change what you do next.

## Treating the same visible mistake as the same problem

A clipping about making videos with AI gave me a question for an exercise. Suppose two shots fail to match. Would you first rewrite the instructions given to the model or check the character design or scene references? If every practice case gives you the same cause, you can get the answer right by remembering what you fixed last time. I’d give the learner a piece of evidence to check before asking where to start.

Suppose you’re editing a short conversation indoors. In the front-facing shot, a cup is on the actor’s right. Then you cut to the reverse shot, which looks back from the other person’s side of the conversation. Use the actor and the table to orient yourself and you’ll see the cup has moved to the actor’s left. The cut reveals a continuity error, a detail that fails to match between shots. To decide where to look first, you need the material used to make the shots.

Make two practice versions of the clip. A storyboard is a set of pictures that plan what the shots should look like. In the first case, the two approved storyboard images put the cup in different places, and each generated shot follows its own reference. Compare the two images, agree on where the cup belongs, and then decide which shots to redo.

In the second case, the storyboard images agree on the cup’s position, and the production record confirms that both versions were used to generate the shots. Only the reverse shot departs from its reference. You could first check what else went into that shot, such as the written instructions to the model, and keep the initial repair limited to that shot. Both cases can show exactly the same continuity error. The one change is a piece of evidence in the production record that should change what you do next.

Now make a variation. The continuity error involves a jacket. The character reference and storyboard give it different colors, and the generated shots each follow one of those references. This looks quite different from a cup changing places, but it calls for the same first step as the first cup case. Check the conflicting references. Put those two cases together and you get to practice recognizing when the same action still applies, even after the obvious visual details change.

Both pairings earn their place. The same footage with different evidence lets you practice changing your mind when you should. Different footage with the same decisive evidence lets you practice recognizing when the same action still applies.

Using what you’ve learned on a new example is called learning transfer. For instance, you might learn to sort a set of shapes into categories, then work out where a shape you never saw in practice belongs. [Transfer tests in the visual classification experiment](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/papers/carvalho_goldstone_delayedgeneralization2014.pdf)

That’s what I’d look for in the editing exercise. The next time two shots fail to match, can you use the material in front of you to decide where to look first and say what you still need to find out?

## Shuffling the material and calling it mixed practice

When you switch back and forth between different kinds of problems in practice, researchers call it interleaved practice. For instance, you might decide where a cup belongs in one exercise and check whether a shot used the right reference image in the next. [How the math trial describes interleaved practice](https://addvancemaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice.pdf)

I’d also look at what neighboring cases give me a chance to compare. If you shuffle video editing, English spelling, and spreadsheet formulas together, you’ll switch between tasks. To practice telling two video problems apart, I’d pick two cases where you can make a decision by checking the production records. Then you can compare exactly which entry sends you looking somewhere else.

Two shirts can look almost identical and carry different laundry instructions. One tag allows machine washing and the other calls for hand washing. Put the shirts together and the tags are the thing to look at. A shirt and a pair of trousers can look quite different and both allow machine washing. For that particular decision, you can treat them the same way. Arrange practice cases in the same way. Vary the appearance and the appropriate response in turn, and say which piece of information you relied on each time. A laundry tag gives a direct instruction. A production record can have gaps, so your practice also needs to allow for checking the missing information before deciding.

Paulo Carvalho and Robert Goldstone ran an experiment in which undergraduates learned to classify irregular shapes created for the study. The researchers tested them immediately, then tested them again 24 hours later after a brief refresher. Alternating examples helped with categories that were easy to confuse, and the pattern held the next day. For the less similar categories, neither schedule emerged as the clear overall winner. The task was about classifying shapes. What I take from it is the value of deciding which kind of comparison I need before arranging the examples. [The visual classification and delayed testing experiment](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/papers/carvalho_goldstone_delayedgeneralization2014.pdf)

The same researchers later put a possible explanation into a computational model. When the previous picture came from the same category, its shared features could stand out. When the previous picture came from another category, small differences could become easier to see. A model that let the previous example change the importance of features in the current example reproduced some of the differences associated with study order in data from three earlier studies. The researchers were testing an explanation against existing data. In my own practice, I’d start by making one pair and seeing whether the first case helps me notice the decisive evidence in the second. [The model of study order and attention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9285726/)

If a beginner has trouble using the actor, table, and camera angle to tell where the cup actually sits, I’d start by showing them several views in a row. Change the camera angle and keep the cup in the same place relative to the actor. Once the learner can explain what the shots have in common, ask them to distinguish conflicting references from a single shot that departs from its reference. If you ask them to do both things from the start, a wrong answer makes it hard to tell which step held them up.

## Grading practice by how smoothly it goes

When you do several problems of the same type in a row, you can often keep using the method you found on the first one. For example, if several questions in a row ask you to find the slope, you can reuse the formula from the previous question. [The math trial’s explanation of reusing a method](https://addvancemaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice.pdf)

To test whether I can choose the method, I’d remove the chapter heading and take the previous problem off the screen. In an editing exercise, I’d keep the footage and the production records that belong with it, then look there for evidence. I can record how long it took and separately check whether I chose the right first step and can point to the evidence for it.

In two sections of the same introductory physics course at UCLA, students spent eight weeks doing homework and experienced both arrangements, with topics interleaved or grouped by type. They answered fewer homework questions correctly under interleaving and tended to find it harder and feel they were learning less. Yet they scored higher after interleaved practice on both surprise tests featuring harder, unfamiliar problems. On the individual portions of the graded midterms that followed, the score differences were too uncertain for researchers to establish which practice schedule led to higher scores. [The undergraduate physics homework and testing study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-021-00110-x.pdf)

Chisom Obasih and colleagues had young adults learn to classify artificial sounds that did not sound like speech. A frequency band is a range of sound frequencies. In this experiment each sound had a higher and a lower band playing at the same time, and only one carried the clue to the category. One group chose between categories that used the same band for that clue, and the other practiced with all possible category pairings. [Sounds and groupings in the auditory classification experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11415078/)

The group that practiced with categories paired by the informative band answered fewer training items correctly at first. By the end of about 40 minutes of training, the two groups were about equally accurate at classifying sounds they hadn’t heard before. The comparison measured accuracy within that session. It didn’t track whether the groups might differ after training ended. [Accuracy in the auditory classification experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11415078/)

Someone could reasonably say that making extra material just to practice catching a continuity error sounds like too much work. I’d do the arithmetic first. If a deadline is close and the same quick step makes sense in both cases, such as opening the approved storyboard, take that step. Extra practice needs a reason. Maybe even after consulting the storyboard, you keep sending whole sequences for regeneration when only one shot needs work. Or you keep fixing shots one by one when the references conflict and wind up redoing them anyway. That’s when it’s worth putting two often-confused decisions next to each other. You’re practicing the choice that costs you something when you get it wrong.

## Asking AI for the exercises and the answers together

I’d ask AI to draft versions of the same case with roughly the same length and presentation. I’d first write down which evidence would make me take a different action. If a draft turns a missing reference image into a claim that the wrong image was used, I need to check the production record before deciding what can go into the exercise. When you have checked records from past work, keep the facts those records establish. When you use a simulation, label it as a practice case.

1. Pick something you actually need to handle now or soon and might misjudge. Write down two first steps that would change the work. In the video example, one is to check and reconcile conflicting references, and the other is to check what a single shot actually received as input. Write down the evidence for each action in enough detail that someone else could find it in the material. If all you write is *upstream problem*, you may need to guess what you meant a few days from now.
2. Make both kinds of pair from the same case. Keep the misplaced cup and change whether the two reference images conflict. Then keep the conflicting references and swap the cup error for the jacket error. Ask AI to draft the versions if you like, and check each one for accidental hints. If one case gives you only the finished footage and the other comes with a long production history, you might guess the intended answer from the length. Give each case roughly the same space and the same kinds of information. Keep the answers separate.
3. Shuffle the cases and answer them one at a time. Record three things for each case. Where would you look first? What evidence supports that choice? What new information would make you change your mind? You can include a case in which the version of the reference actually used is missing. Even if the two storyboard images you have agree, you still need to find out which version was used to generate the shot. Afterward, compare your reasons with the evidence you set aside in advance. If you got the action right for a reason that doesn’t hold up, pair that case with one that puts your reason to the test.
4. Save one case you haven’t worked through and try it the next day. Consult the material you’d normally be allowed to check on the job and leave the practice answers closed until you’re done. If seeing the familiar error sends you straight back to the same fix, make another pair with the same visible problem and different evidence. If a different visual error makes you overlook the original clue, make another pair with different appearances and the same appropriate response. This check can help you decide what to practice next. What happens on subsequent jobs will tell you how well the practice carries over.

If you have a case handy, I’d start by writing one sentence. Think of the last time you nearly used the wrong approach. Which piece of evidence would have made you take a different action? If you can name it, use it to give the original case a neighbor.

The math test at the beginning grouped its questions by type. [The math trial’s test format](https://addvancemaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice.pdf) When you arrange your own practice, put the two cases you’re most likely to confuse side by side. Tomorrow, take out the new case you set aside and see which piece of evidence makes you pause and choose a different first step.
