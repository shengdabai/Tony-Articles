The reason I entered the world of logic was because I realized there were many biases in my thinking, and most of the time, I made decisions based on intuition rather than logical analysis. Looking back on my student days, I often didn’t make choices myself, and I would complain a lot. When I got to university, I had the chance to choose, but I didn’t know how. I just took things step by step, thinking, “It’s okay, things will work out when we get there.” Little did I know, the boat might have veered off course, and I wouldn’t know where I was going. After university, I truly began to enter the world of logic because I realized how powerful logic could be. I actually had logical thinking abilities, but I hadn’t received systematic training.

The trigger was when I joined the Chaos University and took the innovation and entrepreneurship course created by Professor Li Shanyou. That course was my initial motivation for learning. Later, it evolved into a four-stage learning journey: the first stage is the world of intuition, the second is the world of rationality, the third is the world of philosophical science thinking, and the fourth is the world of ideas. I was transitioning from the first to the second stage, learning how to think rationally and use multiple thinking models. Today’s logic class is more like the third stage, philosophical science thinking, exploring the essence from the source. Deductive and inductive methods are the foundation, first principles, axiomatic thinking, and so on. The door to logic opened for me, embracing uncertainty, and perceiving the surroundings with a clearer perspective. Just follow Liuda teacher.

How is the world of logic different from what I expected? I might have thought logic was complicated, with many formulas and principles to learn and think about, which made me feel intimidated. This might be because I didn’t understand it well, so I feared it. I always thought my logical ability was limited, only to express my ideas clearly. Now I realize that’s also part of logic. I should thank the two years I spent as an English teacher, which greatly improved me, making me think more. Joining the practice group, learning every day, doing it day after day, accumulating little by little, learning from them, and practicing it for life. Logic will improve, keep going, Tony (´-ω-`)

My first systematic learning of critical thinking was through a course on Dedao, titled *15 Lectures on Critical Thinking* by Teacher Cai Yu. The name of the course, "Critical Thinking," deeply attracted me. At that time, my understanding of critical thinking was that it meant not blindly believing what others say, not trusting experts, authorities, or textbooks. Of course, it wasn’t about being intentionally argumentative or disliking everything. That was my initial understanding. As I continued learning, I realized my understanding was quite superficial and hadn’t reached the core. This was due to insufficient thinking training and lack of reading related books. The three books recommended by Teacher Cai Yu are now, in retrospect, really necessary to learn: *Beyond Sensation*, *Asking Questions*, and *Reason and Position*. Reading a book a hundred times, its meaning becomes clear. Reading multiple times is essential.

> Overdoing critical thinking is, in fact, the enemy of critical thinking.

Most of the time, we don’t need to use critical thinking — in life, most things or arguments aren’t topics for critical thinking. For example, in daily life, you might like oil sticks, and I might prefer porridge. We each have our own preferences, and there’s no need to justify our choices with scientific evidence or say they’re good for our health. Another example is during the pandemic, when many of us were scrambling for masks, which was normal. But I even believed in double yellow honeysuckle, thinking it might be helpful, and opened the app — it was already sold out! Not every issue or irrational behavior of others requires you to step in and fight, or to prove you’re right. I’ve also seen many people who claim to be rational or critical thinkers, arguing with others online. There are countless mutual accusations, each with their own reasons. That’s why Li Xiaolai teacher told us not to worry about others’ affairs or jump into unnecessary arguments.

> Critical thinking is a way of thinking, while tolerance is a principle of dealing with people. Learning to be tolerant is also an application of critical thinking. For example, excellent martial artists are not competitive or prone to fights. I started practicing martial arts as a child, but my foundation was weak, and I kept training. When someone says you’re a martial artist, they either ask you to perform a set of punches or challenge you. I think I don’t need to argue with them; I can just politely decline. Being tolerant helps improve our critical thinking!

Critical thinking requires making a clear judgment. Here’s a question: you can start from your own or someone else’s perspective, examine the facts comprehensively, use logical analysis, and ultimately give a viewpoint. Critical thinking also considers the pros and cons — but these must be relevant to the current decision. In contrast, dialectics are good at inventing pros and cons, emphasizing one side or the other, but they don’t seem to care much about the effectiveness of each side. So dialectics are more like a strategy for writing or debating, reminding us to view problems dynamically and not to fixate on them. Similarly, critical thinking isn’t just about considering the present and not the future, or considering all factors — it just gives a clear result of thinking.

> Finally, let’s talk about the three stages of thinking. Starting from the certainty of ignorance, where we blindly believe, like in student days, when we were willing to believe in many things. Gradually, we move into the confusion of knowledge, where each has its own reasoning, and various models are used in thinking. A great person can have two opposing models in their mind at the same time. I think I’m struggling in this stage. As for the third stage, critical thinking, that’s what I aspire to. Calling it independent thinking feels more appropriate, avoiding the interference of literal language. Why should we learn independent thinking? It’s an important foundation of logic. Learning to think independently is our metacognitive ability, the cognition of cognition, allowing us to understand our thinking process from the very root. Independent thinking has two aspects: asking questions and controlling mental activity. Learning to ask questions has always been my required course. Asking questions helps us understand the situation more clearly. Sometimes, as we ask questions, things become clear naturally. Of course, you can also use questioning to control your emotions. For example, if you’re angry, ask yourself why, then what you did, and then ask more detailed questions. Gradually, you won’t be angry anymore. As for controlling mental activity, this might be a long-term ability cultivated by independent thinkers. For example, the practice we are currently doing: sitting and enjoying, to enhance our metacognitive abilities. I think this is the beginning of controlling the mind. It’s a process, and no one can do it perfectly from the start, right? (≧∇≦*) Let’s work together to improve our ability to think independently!
