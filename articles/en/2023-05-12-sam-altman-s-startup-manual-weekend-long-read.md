At YC, we spent a lot of time giving founders advice. Although one-on-one advice is always the most effective, we believe that if we can extract the most general parts of the advice we give to YC founders and turn them into a startup manual, it might help us scale YC further.

Later, we realized that this manual should be shared with everyone.

This manual is for new entrepreneurs. For many who are already familiar with YC's startup methodology, most of the content will be familiar. Our goal is to compile these pieces of advice.

As a startup company, your goal is: to create a product that users love. Once you achieve this, you need to figure out how to acquire a large number of customers. However, "creating a product that users love" is extremely critical. Think about today's truly successful companies—they all started with a product that early users deeply loved. Their early users were so passionate about their product that they would spread it to others.

If you cannot create a product that users love, you will fail. If you try to deceive yourself, pretending your users love your product when they actually don't, you will also fail.

There are countless startups that believe they can skip this step, but they all end up failing.

Creating a product that a small group of users genuinely love is far better than creating a product that many users only somewhat like. Although the total amount of positive feedback may be similar, it's very difficult to move people from liking to loving your product. Acquiring more users, on the other hand, is much easier.

Here's a warning for founders choosing to start a company: entrepreneurship is really painful! The most consistent feedback we've received from YC founders is that entrepreneurship is far more difficult than they imagined. Because before starting a company, they had no complete understanding of the work and intensity required. Usually, joining an early-stage but rapidly growing startup is a much better financial choice.

On the other hand, starting a company doesn't carry as much risk for your career. If you're really good at technology, you can still find a job even if you fail. Most people aren't that skilled at assessing risk. Personally, I think the more risky choice is when you have a truly passionate idea or project, but you remain in a safe, comfortable, and unsatisfying job.

To build a successful startup, you need: a great idea (including a very large market), a great team, a great product, and great execution.

**********01**********

## **Idea**

Our first question to YC companies is: what are you building? Why?

We want to hear a clear and concise answer.

This question is both an assessment of the founder and an evaluation of the startup idea itself. As a founder, the ability to think and communicate clearly is essential: you need this skill for hiring, fundraising, and sales. Generally speaking, clear ideas are easier to spread, while complex ideas are almost always a sign of confusion or false problems. If your idea doesn't make at least some people feel genuinely excited the first time they hear it, it's a bad sign.

Another question we ask is: who is the most desperate and in need of this product?

The best answer is that you are the target user. The second best is that you deeply understand your target user.

If a company already has users, we ask how many users they have and how fast the growth is. We want to understand why the user growth isn't faster. The most important question we want to know is whether users truly love the product. Usually, loving a product means users will recommend it to friends without any prompting. We also ask whether the company has generated revenue, and if not, why?

If the company doesn't have users yet, we try to determine the minimum viable feature to build to validate the assumption. In other words, if we work backward from the ideal user experience, we try to find the key point to start with.

To test an idea, you can: 1. Launch it and observe the results, or 2. Try to sell it (e.g., try to get a purchase intent contract before writing any code). The former is more suitable for consumer-facing ideas (because user verbal feedback can be dishonest), while the latter is more suitable for business-facing ideas (if a company tells you they will buy a product, go ahead and build it).

Specifically, if you are a B-to-B company, our first question to you is: do you currently have a purchase intent contract from a customer for the product you're developing?

For most biotech and hard tech companies, the way to test an idea is to first engage with potential customers and then identify the smallest subset of technical features you can build first.

When you receive user feedback, it's very important to adjust your idea quickly. Deeply understanding your users is essential for evaluating your idea, building a great product, and creating a great company.

As mentioned earlier, starting a startup is very difficult. It requires a long time and sustained high-intensity effort. Founders and employees need a sense of mission to support them. So we ask founders why they chose to start this company.

We also ask how the company will become a monopolistic market leader in the future. Although there are many different ways to say this, we use Peter Thiel's view. Of course, we don't want your company to use unethical means against competitors. Instead, we are looking for companies that become stronger and harder to imitate as they scale.

Finally, we ask market-related questions. We inquire about the current size of the market, its growth rate, and why it will become large within a decade. We try to understand why the market is growing rapidly and why it's a good market for startups. We like the early stages of major technological changes that most people haven't realized yet. ——Large companies often perform poorly in such situations. Counterintuitively, the best strategy is to capture a large share in a small market.

**Some other thoughts on ideas:**

We really like new things rather than derivatives. Most real giant companies started with some fundamental new things. (New things are roughly ten times better than what came before.) If ten companies have the same plan and are doing something very similar to existing things, we are skeptical.

An important counterintuitive reason is that doing something completely new and difficult is actually simpler than doing something old and easy. Because the former attracts people to help and join you, while the latter does not.

The best ideas are those that sound terrible but are actually great. Therefore, you don't need to keep your idea secret. If your idea is really a great one, it's likely not worth copying. Even if your idea sounds like it's worth copying, there are very few people in the world who are willing to put in a lot of effort to turn a great idea into a great company. Ideas, if you tell people what you're doing, they might offer help.

Speaking of telling people your idea. Although a good idea needs to make some people really excited the first time they hear it, almost everyone will tell you that your idea is terrible. Maybe they're right. Maybe they're not good at evaluating startup ideas. Maybe they're just jealous. No matter the reason, this happens often. You will definitely feel hurt, even if you think you can accept it, you'll still feel pain. You need to build confidence quickly and ignore the critics. No matter how successful you are, critics will always be around.

If you don't have an idea yet but want to start a company, our advice is that maybe you shouldn't. Having an idea first and then starting a company to realize it is a much better path.

We once did an experiment, investing in some very talented but idea-less founding teams. We hoped they would find a promising idea after receiving funding.

Every one of them failed. I think part of the reason is that great founders usually have many (even too many) good ideas. But the bigger problem is that once you have a startup company, you must quickly come up with an idea, and because it's now an official company, your idea can't be too crazy. In the end, you'll end up with ideas that sound reasonable but lack innovation. This is the danger of the transition.

So, it's best not to force yourself too hard to come up with a startup idea. Instead, learn about many different things. Train yourself to spot problems. Pay attention to things that seem inefficient and major technological changes. Get involved in projects you're interested in. Deliberately surround yourself with smart, interesting people. At some point, inspiration will strike.

**02**

## **A Great Team**

## A mediocre team cannot build a great company. One of the most important things we value is the founder's ability. When I used to make investments in later stages, I also valued the ability of the founders' hired employees.

What makes a great founder? **The most important traits include being unstoppable, determined, strong, and resourceful. Intelligence and passion are also very important.** These traits are far more important than experience and so-called "language X and framework Y expertise."

We noticed that the most successful founders are usually those who are easy to work with and give the impression of "being able to complete tasks no matter what the situation." Sometimes, success can be achieved purely through sheer willpower.

Good founders have many seemingly contradictory characteristics. One important characteristic is having a strong belief in the company's core and mission, while being flexible in other areas and willing to keep learning new things.

The best founders are exceptionally responsive, which is an indicator of decisiveness, focus, attention, and the ability to complete tasks.

Founders who are difficult to communicate are almost always bad entrepreneurs. Communication is a very important founder skill. Honestly, I think this is the most important of the rarely mentioned entrepreneurial skills.

A tech startup needs at least one founder who can build the product and service, and at least one founder (or someone who can learn to become) very good at sales and communicating with users. These two can be the same person.

When choosing a co-founder, consider the following—this is one of your most important decisions, but people usually make it carelessly.

**You'd better choose someone you're already very familiar with, rather than someone you just met at a startup party.** Learn as much as you can, so you can better assess whether you can work with this person. After all, choosing the wrong person can be fatal. Also, sometimes the value of a startup may drop significantly in the short term. If you've already been very familiar with your partner before starting the company, you won't easily give up, because you don't want to let the other person down. You'll work together to overcome the difficulties. Breaking up between startup partners is one of the most common reasons for a startup's failure. And we often see this happening among partners who met for the purpose of starting a business.

The best case is having a great partner. The second best is having only yourself. The worst case is having a bad partner. If things go wrong, you should split up quickly.

**Regarding equity issues, resolve them as early as possible**, because as time goes on, the discussion about equity distribution will only become more complicated. It's best to set the equity ratio as early as possible. Near equality is the best, but if there are only two founders, it's better to let one hold a little more, in case a deadlock arises when disagreements happen between the partners.

**03**

## **A Very Good Product**

## Here's a successful secret: having a very good product. This is the only common point among all great companies.

If you can't create a product that users love, you will eventually fail. Although founders always try to bypass this, there is no shortcut in entrepreneurship.

A very good product is the only effective way for long-term growth. Because even if you use some other growth methods, you will eventually find that as your scale grows, **all other methods will fail, but 'people really want to use your product' will never fail**.

This is the most important thing to understand about the super successful companies. You can think about it, all the most successful tech companies have achieved this.

You need to build a 'product improvement engine' within your company. You should talk to your users, watch them use your product, and find out which parts need improvement, so that your product can become better. Do this repeatedly. This should be the most important thing in your company and drive all other things. **If you can really improve your product by 5% every week, you will have massive compound growth.**

The faster this cycle, the better your company will be. At YC, we tell entrepreneurs to build a product and talk to users, and that's about it—except for eating, sleeping, exercising, and spending time with loved ones.

You need to be very close to your users to ensure the cycle is correct. You must stand beside them and watch them use your product. Remember what they say and what they actually do. You shouldn't put anyone between you and your users. You need to do sales, customer support, and other things yourself.

Make every effort to understand your users, try to truly find out what they need, where they are, and what really moves them.

"Doing things that don't scale" has become the mantra for startups. You need to manually recruit your initial users (Pinterest's founders went to a café and asked strangers to use their product), and then you need to create what they want. Many founders hate this part and want to launch their product through media. But this strategy almost never works. **Manually recruit users and then make your product really good, and your users will share it with their friends.**

You also need to break things down into very small parts, iterate and adjust quickly. Don't try to plan too far ahead, and never put everything into one big public version. You need to start with something very simple. The simpler, the better. And push out the product even faster than you imagine. In fact, simple things are almost always good. You need to always try to make your product and company as simple as possible.

For some problematic companies, we often ask these questions: Do your users use your product multiple times? Are your users passionate about your product? If your company disappears, would your users really feel upset? Do your users recommend you to others on their own? If you're a ToB company, do you have at least 10 paying customers?

If the answers are no, then the product is not good enough—that's the fundamental reason. I'm skeptical of most excuses for a company's slow growth. Because the most common and real reason is that the product is not good enough.

When a startup is unsure about what to do next with their product or doubts whether their product is good enough, we tell them to talk to users. This isn't always effective (users might tell Ford they want a faster horse), but it's very often effective. In fact, if there's any disagreement within your company, you should talk to your users.

The best founders often seem overly focused on product details, even if they seem unimportant. But this really works. By the way, a product includes everything that users and the company interact with. You need to have great customer service, great salespeople, etc.

Remember, if you haven't created a very good product, nothing can save you.

**04**

## **Very Good Execution**

## Although creating a very good product is essential, your task is still not over. You need to turn this very good product into a very good company. And you must do this yourself. The fantasy of hiring a professional manager to do this is extremely common and also extremely unsuccessful. You can't outsource this work long-term.

Although it sounds obvious, you must start making money. Now is the time to think about how to achieve profitability.

**As a CEO, your only general responsibility is to ensure the company wins.** Even if you're a founder and may have many shortcomings that make you unsuitable for the role of CEO, as long as the people you hire can compensate for your skill gaps and fully utilize their abilities, you can still do this job well. Compared to experienced CEOs with advanced MBAs, you may not have the same skills, but you know your users better, you have a sharper product sense, and you're more passionate about the company's growth.

## **Very Good Execution - Growth Part**

Sustained growth and a positive momentum are key to successful execution. Good growth (as long as it's not achieved by selling at a loss) can solve all problems, while the lack of growth can only be solved by growth. If you're growing, you'll feel like you're winning continuously, and everyone will be happy. If you're growing, you'll have new roles and responsibilities constantly, and people will feel their careers are advancing. But if you're not growing, you'll feel like you're losing, and people will become dissatisfied and leave. If you're not growing, people will start fighting for power and blaming each other.

Companies without growth are filled with a sense of despair, and founders and employees are all exhausted. It's hard to say how bad this really is.

Regarding execution, the most important guidance is: **never lose the momentum of growth.** But how do you do that?

I think the most important method is to **make maintaining growth your top priority.** What a CEO focuses on, the whole company will work to achieve.

Here are a few examples.

Airbnb's founders wrote their growth goals down on paper and posted them everywhere. They put them on their fridge, table, and toilet. If they reached that number, nothing happened. If they didn't, it would be the only thing everyone would talk about.

Zuck once said that one of Facebook's most important innovations was creating a growth team when growth hit a bottleneck. The growth team at FB was (maybe still is) the most respected team in the company. Everyone knew how important they were.

Make a list of all the things that are blocking growth, and discuss with your team how to grow faster. If you know the factors that are limiting growth, you'll naturally think of how to solve them.

For anything you're considering doing, ask yourself, "Is this the best way to optimize growth?" For example, attending a meeting is usually not the best way to optimize growth, unless you expect to make a lot of sales in the meeting.

Extreme transparency of internal data metrics (and financial status) is a good thing. For some reason, founders are always very afraid of this transparency. But it's very helpful for the whole company to focus on growth. The more employees focus on metrics, the better the company's performance seems to be. If you hide the metrics, people find it hard to focus on them.

When it comes to data metrics, don't fool yourself with vanity metrics. A very common mistake is focusing on registered users rather than retained users. But retention is just as important as acquiring new users.

It's also important to establish an internal rhythm to maintain the growth momentum. You want to "do things rhythmically," meaning continuously launching new features, acquiring new customers, hiring new employees, achieving revenue milestones, and building new partnerships, which can be promoted internally and externally.

You should set ambitious but achievable goals and check your progress monthly. Celebrate every victory! Keep discussing strategy internally and share everything you hear from customers. The more information you share, whether good or bad, the better your company will be.

Founders often fall into some traps. One of them is, if a company is growing very fast, but everything seems chaotic and inefficient, everyone will worry that things will spiral out of control. In reality, this almost never happens (Friendster is the most recent startup I can point to that died due to technical debt). Surprisingly, if your growth is fast but everything is not optimized, you just need to fix these issues, and you'll get even more growth! My favorite investments are those companies that are growing very fast but haven't been optimized much—they're severely undervalued.

Another related trap is **overfocusing on too far-off future problems**—for example, "How should we solve this in large-scale scenarios?" The correct approach is to consider this only when you need to solve it. Many startups waste too much time arguing about this instead of focusing on solving the current problem. A good rule of thumb is to consider only ten times the current scale. Most early-stage startups should have "Do things that don't scale" posted on the wall and in their minds. For example, good startups always have excellent customer service in the early stages, while bad startups worry that this will hurt their profitability and scalability. But great customer service brings passionate early users, and as your product improves, you'll need less customer service because you'll know which problems users are often stuck with and you'll solve them. (By the way, having excellent customer service is particularly important.)

One important issue with this strategy is that "Do things that don't scale" cannot be an excuse for avoiding profitability. Having poor unit economics in the early stages may be acceptable, but you must have a good reason to explain why unit economics will improve in the future.

Another trap is, even if your product's growth rate is good, the absolute numbers are not ideal, which can make you feel discouraged. However, humans are very bad at intuitively understanding exponential growth. Remind your team of this, and that all big companies started small.

Some of the biggest traps are that **many founders believe things that can bring growth actually almost never work, yet they will take up a lot of time**. Common examples include agreements with other companies and “major press conferences.” You need to realize that they actually don’t work. Instead, like all great companies, build a product that users love, manually recruit initial users, and then test many growth strategies (ads, referral programs, sales and marketing, etc.), and invest in the ones that truly work for you. Ask your users where they can find more people like them.

Remember, sales and marketing are not bad words. Although they can’t save you without an outstanding product, they can help you significantly accelerate growth. If you are a ToB company, you likely need to improve your capabilities in this area.

Don’t be afraid of sales. At least one founder on the team must be good at getting people to use your product and pay for it.

Alex Schultz gave a talk on growth for ToC companies*, which is well worth watching.

For ToB products, I think you should track monthly revenue growth and remember that longer sales cycles mean the first few months may not look great. (Taking startups as initial customers may help with this.)

*Speech URL: http://startupclass.samaltman.com/courses/lec06/

## **Excellent Execution – Focus and High Intensity**

If I could use only two words to summarize my operational advice, I would choose “focus” and “high intensity.” These words are very applicable to the most outstanding founders I’ve ever seen.

They will fully focus on the product and growth, not trying to do everything. In fact, they will frequently say “no” (which is hard, because entrepreneurs usually like to try new things).

Generally speaking, don’t let your company start on the next thing until you’ve mastered the first. Any great company I’ve ever known won’t do many things at the same time. They usually focus on one thing and stick with it.

You can do far fewer things than you imagine. One of the common reasons startups fail is that they do too many wrong things. Therefore, setting priorities is crucial, but also very difficult. (In addition to setting priorities for the company, it’s also important to set strategic priorities for yourself. I’ve found that the most effective method for me is to list about three main tasks and thirty secondary tasks each day, and compile an annual list of all goals.)

Great founders don’t do many big projects, but they focus intensely on any one thing they do. They complete tasks quickly and have strong decision-making abilities, which is very important in a startup because you’ll receive many conflicting pieces of advice. Some of these suggestions may lead you in different directions, and some are just wrong. Great founders listen to all the advice and then make quick decisions of their own.

Please note that this doesn’t mean you should strongly commit to everything—this is impossible. You must choose the right things. As Paul Buchheit said, find the way to get 90% of the value with 10% of the effort. The market doesn’t care how hard you work; it only cares whether you did the right things.

Great founders should pursue both product quality and rapid progress, but this is very difficult. It’s also one of the most distinctive characteristics of great founders.

**No founder who acts slowly has ever achieved real success.**

You are no different from other startups. You need to stay focused and move quickly. Even companies that develop rockets and nuclear reactors can act quickly. Failing companies often think they are special and don’t need to act quickly.

When you find an effective method, keep going and don’t let other things distract you. Don’t slack off; keep going.

Don’t be misled by early success, and don’t spend too much time on social activities and personal branding. Founders who achieve early success either keep working hard or get addicted to becoming a startup celebrity.

It’s hard to refuse meetings and media exposure—these activities feel good, especially when you see other entrepreneurs getting attention. But this situation won’t last long. Eventually, the media will find out who the real winners are. If your company is truly successful, you’ll get far more attention than you imagine. Founders who had their own PR teams early on (yes, there really are some) almost all failed.

In the long run, focus and high-intensity work will win. (Charlie Rose once said that things in the world are accomplished through a combination of focus and relationships, and this statement has always deeply impressed me.)

## **Excellent Execution – The CEO’s Work**

Earlier, I mentioned that the only universal description of a CEO’s job is ensuring the company wins. Although this is correct, I’d like to talk more specifically about how a CEO should allocate their time.

A CEO must: 1) set the company’s vision and strategy, 2) communicate the company’s mission, vision, and values to everyone, 3) hire and manage the team, especially in areas where they are not skilled, 4) raise funds, and 5) set execution quality standards.

In addition to these responsibilities, you should find the part of the business you love most and stay deeply involved.

As I mentioned at the beginning, this is a high-intensity job. If you succeed, it will take up a level of your life you can’t imagine—your company will be constantly on your mind. Extreme focus and high-intensity work mean it’s hard to balance work and life. Your concern for your family or other important things will never exceed your concern for the company. You must always be online, and there will be many decisions that only you can make, no matter how good you are at delegation.

You should strive to stay highly sensitive to your team and the outside world, always keep a clear strategy and priorities, be present for all important matters, and execute quickly (especially in things others can’t handle). You should also adopt a “no excuses” attitude—there will be a lot of unpleasant work to do. If your team sees you leading by example, they will follow closely behind.

Managing your own mindset is both difficult and important. Although this has become a cliché, it’s true—emotional swings are very intense, and if you don’t know how to keep things relatively stable, you’ll face difficulties. Being a CEO is lonely. So it’s very important to connect with other CEOs, so that when you face huge challenges, at least someone is there to call (one of the important roles of Y Combinator is that it accidentally discovered that it provides a peer support network for founders).

A successful startup takes a very long time—certainly longer than most founders initially expect. You can’t treat it as all-night work. You must eat well, sleep well, and exercise. You must spend time with your family and friends. You also need to work in a field you truly love—only then can you sustain high-intensity work for a decade.

You’ll be chewing glass shards while staring into the abyss—disaster comes in many forms and can be very severe. Your job is to fix them with a smile and comfort your team, telling them everything will be okay. Usually, things aren’t as bad as they look, but sometimes they really are. Either way, you need to keep going and keep growing.

As a CEO, you have no excuses. You’ll face many unfair and difficult situations. But don’t tell yourself or, especially, your team, “If we had more money” or “If we had another engineer.” Either find a solution or find a way around it. People who like to make excuses almost always fail, especially startup CEOs. You can be discouraged for a minute, but then you have to realize that you need to come up with a solution. “You can always think of some way to solve any problem” is what you hope people will say about you.

You need to distort others’ perception of reality, but stay honest with yourself. You need to make others believe you are the most important company of the next ten years, but you should remain vigilant about all possible problems that may arise.

Stay persistent. Most founders give up too soon or switch to the next product too quickly. If things aren’t going well, find the root cause and make sure you solve it. As a successful startup CEO, persistence is very important (although you don’t want to be stubborn to the point of being unreasonable—this is another obvious contradiction that requires difficult judgment).

Stay optimistic. Although there may be a very pessimistic CEO in the world, I haven’t met that person yet. You need to believe and make the whole company feel this belief: the future will be better, and your company will play an important role in it. This is easy in theory but very difficult when facing short-term challenges. Don’t lose sight of your long-term vision and believe that the daily struggles will eventually be replaced by yearly progress.

One of your most important jobs is to define your mission and values. This may sound cliché, but it’s worth doing early on. Anything you instill at the beginning usually remains effective for years. As the company grows, every new person needs to first align with the company’s mission and values before being able to pass them on. Therefore, write down the culture, values, and mission early on.

Another old tune I want to reiterate is: **building a company is somewhat like building a religion**. If people can’t connect their daily work to a higher, more meaningful goal they care about, they won’t perform well. I think Airbnb does the best in this regard within the Y Combinator network, and I strongly recommend you check out their culture and values.

A common mistake CEOs make is innovating in very mature areas rather than in the product and solutions themselves. For example, many founders think they should spend time looking for new methods in areas like human resources, marketing, sales, fundraising, and public relations. This is almost always a bad idea. Find mature and effective practices in these areas and focus your creativity on the product or service you are building.

Don't compromise on the quality of the talent you hire. Everyone knows this, but in times of urgency, everyone compromises. Everyone regrets it, sometimes even almost destroying the company. Both good and bad people are infectious. If you start with average people, the average tends not to rise. Companies that initially hire average employees almost never recover. Trust your gut about people. If you have doubts, the answer is no.

Don't hire people with a negative attitude. They're not suitable for early-stage startups, because the whole world is predicting your failure every day, so the whole team needs to be united and firmly believe you will succeed.

**For almost all positions, prioritize quality over experience.** Look for people with real wisdom and who have accomplished a lot. Look for people you like—you'll be spending a lot of time together and often in stressful situations. For people you don't know, try working on a project with them before they join full-time.

Strive to become a good manager. It's difficult and counterintuitive for most founders. But it's important. Find a mentor who can help you improve your management skills. If you're not good at managing, you'll lose employees quickly. If you can't retain employees, even if you're the best at hiring, it's meaningless. Most principles about being a good manager have been well discussed, but I want to mention a less common point: **don't be a solo hero.** Most new managers will, at some point, try to do everything themselves and become uncommunicative with their employees. This usually ends in disaster. Absolutely don't fall into this pattern—you can delay the project to build a well-functioning team.

When it comes to management, strive to have everyone in the same office. For some reason, startups always compromise on this. But almost all the most successful startups began by working together. I think remote work might be effective for larger companies, but it's not the secret to huge success for startups.

Finally, fire the wrong people quickly. Everyone knows this principle, but no one does it. But I still think I should mention it. At the same time, fire toxic people regardless of their work ability. Culture is defined by the people you hire, fire, and promote.

## **Excellent Execution—Competitors**

Regarding competitors, let me say a few words: **competitors are the phantom fears of startups.** Entrepreneurs believe competitors are the main cause of the death of 99% of startups. But 99% of startups die by suicide, not by being beaten by competitors. Instead, focus on all internal issues. If you fail, it's likely because you didn't build a great product and/or didn't build a great company.

In 99% of cases, you should ignore competitors. Especially when they raise a lot of money or hype themselves in the media. Don't worry about competitors until they release a real product that beats you. Press releases are easier than writing code, and writing code is easier than making a great product. As Henry Ford said: "The real competitors you should worry about are those who don't care about you but are constantly improving their business."

Every big company faced more severe competitive threats when it was small, and they all made it through. There will always be a solution.

## **Excellent Execution—Making Money**

Oh yes, when it comes to making money, you need to figure out how to do it.

In short, you must get more money from people than the cost of your product/service. For some reason, people always forget to consider the cost part.

If you have a free product, don't try to grow by buying users. It's very difficult for companies that monetize through ads. You need to create something people will want to share with their friends.

If you have a paid product where each customer's lifetime value (LTV) is less than $500, you usually can't afford the cost of sales. Try different user acquisition methods like SEO/SEM, ads, email, etc., but try to break even on customer acquisition cost (CAC) within three months.

If you have a paid product where each customer's LTV is higher than $500 (for your net profit), you usually can afford direct sales costs. Start by selling the product yourself to figure out what methods work. *Hacking Sales* is a useful book.

No matter what, try to achieve "ramen profitability" as quickly as possible—that is, make enough money to live on ramen. When you reach this goal, you're in control of your own destiny, no longer at the mercy of investors and financial markets.

Keep a close eye on your cash flow. Though it sounds unbelievable, we've seen many founders run out of money without realizing it (see Paul Graham's article*).

*Article URL: http://paulgraham.com/aord.html

## **Excellent Execution—Funding**

Most startups will go through fundraising at some point.

Fundraise when you need the money or when you have good terms. Be careful not to lose your sense of frugality or start using money to solve problems. Being underfunded is bad, but being overfunded is almost always worse.

The secret to successfully raising funds is having a good company. All the other deliberate optimizations founders do to raise money may only account for 5% of importance. Investors are looking for companies that will be very successful regardless of whether they invest or not, but external capital can accelerate growth. The scale of success is very important—because investors' returns mainly depend on large successful cases. If investors believe you have a 100% chance of building a $10 million company but almost no chance of building a bigger one, even with a very low valuation, they might not invest. You need to keep explaining to investors why you might achieve massive success.

Investors are driven by two fears: missing out on the next Google, and the fear of making a stupid decision that becomes obvious in hindsight. (For the best companies, they worry about both.)

Trying to raise funds when your company isn't in a position to attract capital is a bad idea. You'll waste time and damage your reputation.

If the fundraising process is difficult, don't get discouraged. Many of the best companies faced this problem, because the best companies often look bad at first (and they're almost always not trendy). When investors reject you, believe their rejection, but don't believe their reasons. Remember, besides a "yes" answer, all other answers are "no"—investors have a knack for making a "no" sound like "maybe."

Parallel fundraising is very important—don't list your favorite investors in order. Let investors act in a way that makes them worry about other investors taking their opportunity.

View fundraising as a necessary evil and get it done as quickly as possible. Some founders get obsessed with fundraising; that's definitely not good. It's better to have just one founder handle it so the company doesn't stall.

**Remember, most VC firms don't understand most industries. Data is the most persuasive.**

Although things are changing, most investors (Y Combinator is a notable exception) still need to be introduced through someone you both know to take your project seriously.

Stick to clean terms (complex terms get worse with each round), but don't over-optimize, especially on valuation. Valuation is something you can compare with others, so founders like to push for the highest valuation. But a little less valuation is fine.

The first round of funding is the hardest to get, so focusing your energy on getting the first round usually means focusing on the people who love you the most. Always have multiple plans, one of which is not to raise any money, and be flexible based on interest—consider accepting the money if you can use it effectively and it's available on reasonable terms.

One important key to being a good salesperson is making your story as clear and easy to understand as possible. Of course, the most important key is truly having a great company. There are many ideas about what to include in a pitch, but at least you need to have: mission, problem, product/service, business model, team, market, market growth, and financials.

Remember, the bar for each round of funding will rise significantly. If you were just an impressive speaker in the seed round, you'll find this strategy doesn't work when you do the A round.

Good investors can bring a lot of value to a company, while bad investors can bring a lot of negative impact. Most investors are in the middle, neither adding nor subtracting value. Investors who put in only a small amount of money usually don't help you (e.g., those who participated in party-style funding rounds).

Great board members are the best external constraint mechanism for a company, aside from users. And the value of external constraints is far beyond what most founders imagine. To get a very committed great board member, you can accept a lower valuation.

I think PG's article is the best on fundraising*.

*Article URL: http://paulgraham.com/fr.html

## **Conclusion**

Remember, at least a thousand people have great ideas, but only one will truly succeed. It all comes down to execution. It's a difficult process, and everyone wants to skip the execution of turning "ideas" into "success," but so far, no one has found a way around it.

Therefore, you need only a great idea, a great team, a great product, and excellent execution.

That's really simple! ;)
