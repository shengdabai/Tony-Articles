Today, I want to talk about a concept: "First Principles."

First Principles refer to starting from the most fundamental facts or truths and building our understanding and simulation of the real world through logical reasoning and mathematical models. It is the foundation of scientific research, engineering design, and innovation.

What is First Principles?

First Principles refer to a principle or basic fact that does not rely on other assumptions or premises in reasoning. In simpler terms, it means starting from the most basic elements and laws to derive more complex results.

Why use First Principles?

Using the method of First Principles allows us to avoid starting from existing knowledge and following established paths, which can easily lead us into the constraints of existing thinking. First Principles thinking does not depend on existing ideas or assumptions, enabling independent analysis of problems and more open and free thinking, thus leading to more accurate and comprehensive conclusions. Ma Kankan, in his research on battery innovation, used the method of First Principles calculation to bypass the inherent limitations of traditional approaches and ultimately succeeded in creating a more efficient lithium-ion battery, which is considered one of the most significant innovations in the battery field.

How to use First Principles?

Using First Principles for analysis requires strong logical thinking and advanced mathematical knowledge. We need to abstract the problem into a mathematical model, and when building the model, we should identify the basic elements and simplify the model as much as possible to ensure the conclusions are more accurate and reliable. Elon Musk, the founder of Tesla, is very skilled at using First Principles thinking. For example, in the field of solid rocket propellant, he established a complete budget and timeline to clearly define the costs of manufacturing and avoid expensive mistakes. He also proposed the theory that the cost of solar power generation is lower than that of fossil fuels based on First Principles analysis, and eventually transformed this theory into practice, making Tesla one of the world's most complex battery technology suppliers.

In the scientific field, quantum mechanics uses the method of First Principles to understand the fundamental laws of the microscopic world. The basic assumption of quantum mechanics is that microscopic entities are composed of particles, which follow probability wave equations with wave-like properties. Their states can be described as a set of possible values. By deriving from these fundamentals, quantum mechanics can explain atomic structure, molecular chemistry, and many other physical phenomena.

In the design field, Jonathan Ive, the former chief designer of Apple, is also regarded as a representative of First Principles thinking. When designing Apple products, he started from the basic elements of the product, such as battery life, reliability, and usability, and through continuous optimization and simplification of the design, he created products that are deeply loved by users and revolutionary, such as the iPhone and iPad.

In the financial field, Benjamin Graham, the pioneer of value investing, is also a representative of First Principles thinking. He proposed the concept of "margin of safety," which emphasizes the importance of a safety cushion in investment, and evaluates the value and risk of investments through mathematical formulas and financial analysis. This method does not rely on the intuition and experience of professional investors but instead makes investment decisions through rigorous analysis and logical reasoning, becoming a successful investment strategy.

First Principles thinking can be applied in various fields such as science, design, and finance. By continuously breaking through traditional thinking frameworks and methods, we can analyze problems more accurately and objectively, solve difficult problems, and unleash the potential of human creativity.

Li Xiaolai once gave a brilliant analogy: "Education is like a pair of glasses."

If I were to say it, I would prefer the simplest version. This version comes from Dewey, with only three words: "Education is growth." In simpler terms, education is about helping people grow. Many Chinese scholars have mistranslated the word "growth" into "education is growth" or "education is shaping," but I believe the most appropriate translation is "education is growth."

Growth is not something that happens overnight. Growth means you cannot be impulsive; you need patience and foresight to wait for the flowers to bloom. Therefore, education should not be short-sighted; it should look to the long term. You need to calm down, rid yourself of impatience, and carefully nurture and cultivate things bit by bit to see results.

It is the wisdom to solve problems comprehensively, the ability to use critical and creative thinking to achieve innovation, the ability to communicate effectively, and the ability to identify and manage emotions. Most importantly, it is love and emotion.

Shen Zuyun proposed the seven lamps of education: from large-scale schools to learning organizations, from classroom-style classrooms to personalized communities, from knowledge transfer to emotional labor, from standard answers to solutions, from professional orientation to ability-based approaches, and through the cultivation of core competencies, enabling each person to have the ability for self-assessment and the motivation for self-directed learning. Do these seven lamps not continuously amplify the essence of being human? Ultimately, they enable us not only to face challenges but also to create a more beautiful new normal of human society in a future where intelligence is everywhere and the boundary between humans and machines is gradually blurred through harmonious coexistence between humans and machines.

I am an educator. No matter what scale of school I work in today, I can start by improving relationships to amplify the value of people, allowing children to explore and experience through self-directed choices and enabling teachers to support and grow through guidance and design.

Different stages bring different thoughts. The following three stories are from my experiences and reflections at different times in the past few years. Some of them seem naive, and some are overly idealistic, but they are all real experiences in my life.

The first story: Exploring the Beauty of a University Project

I recall my undergraduate thesis. I want to re-examine my state at that time. I was majoring in civil engineering, but in the spring of my senior year, I chose to enter the education industry and become an English teacher. Through the flow model, I analyzed my situation during the two months of completing my thesis.

1. Setting Goals

Complete the thesis, create a BIM information model for architecture, and participate in a national design competition to give a perfect ending to my civil engineering major.

2. Learning New Skills

For two months, I was fully focused on the thesis, working from 7 a.m. to 11 p.m., with a 30-minute lunch break. I was immersed in a state of flow, tackling one design problem after another, testing and making mistakes. First, I learned the importance of communication. In my daily work, I tend to be assertive and take charge myself, but in design, it's a team task. Among the five partners, we needed to strengthen communication. Even though I had already completed the model, I had to clearly assign tasks to others, sometimes even helping team members solve problems.

Second, I learned to be patient and endure solitude. When I was immersed in a state of flow, I paid little attention to the outside world. After completing the task, I felt a great sense of satisfaction. Sometimes, when I looked up, the room might be empty, with everyone leaving on time. But for me, that was the only thing I was doing, and everything else was secondary. I could handle the lunch break, and sometimes I felt uncomfortable because I was the only one working on the model. But gradually, I accepted it as my choice, and my standards were my own, not related to others. I was present in the moment, enjoying the beauty of the work, and letting the flow of creativity flow through me.

Third, setting clear goals. Often, I set many goals for myself because I was not satisfied with the status quo, but given the limited time and energy, setting detailed small goals is especially important. I also experienced anxiety and worries, and I was confused about why I wasn't more efficient. Over time, I gradually understood my own limits and realized that a bit of effort and going with the flow might be more helpful for me.

3. Establishing Internal and External Feedback

Internal feedback: Often, I ignored internal feedback and focused more on external feedback. However, in this design project, I was constantly reflecting on myself and adjusting. For example, I designed a single floor. I could use my professional knowledge to verify whether it was reasonable. I needed to check if the internal layout was optimal by comparing and validating it with my experience, and continuously adjusting. During the design process, internal observation was important. From an architect's perspective, I should pay attention to the exterior appearance, spatial layout, etc. From a civil engineer's perspective, I needed to consider structural design, seismic resistance, wind resistance, and coordination with the building. I also needed to analyze from the perspective of electrical and water design. But that was not enough. I also needed to consider other perspectives, such as user experience, the needs of real estate developers, and the regulations of government supervisors. I needed to analyze and think from more angles, using internal feedback to help me better focus on the state of flow.

External feedback: In addition to internal feedback, we also need to refer to external information. For example, the first thing is the feedback from team members. Sometimes I was limited by my own cognition, and the help from my partners gave me new thoughts, allowing me to understand from other angles. During the design process, based on limited internal knowledge, the help from teachers, online resources, and guidance from professional software engineers were all essential external feedback. Some problems that I thought about for a long time could be resolved with a single piece of advice from the outside. This is also a process of continuous trial and improvement. We shouldn't blindly believe in authority, nor should we doubt everything. Beyond design, we also need to consider human factors, such as the grief from the death of a family member, the anxiety from entering a new field after graduation, and the pain of a breakup. These external feedbacks can have different impacts.

Keep going and cherish the moment. I am grateful for that period of focus and immersion. Flow is very precious to me. In work, I often feel lost and tired, and flow seems out of reach. This analysis has made me realize that I can do better. I can enter a state of flow for each task, and through more analysis and thinking, I can help myself work better. Leaving the engineering industry, I am grateful for the thinking and valuable knowledge from the sciences. In the future, in the field of education, I will combine what I have and what is happening now to strive toward my goals. Let's go!
