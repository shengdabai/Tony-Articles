# National Treasure Without Peer

The character 士, I don’t know how to explain. I quote Wang Weigang’s understanding of 士 and combine it with my own thoughts to gain a deeper sense of this character!

士 is part of our Chinese tradition of honor. In ancient times, 士 were the top layer of the common people and the bottom layer of the aristocracy. 士 lived by their own merits, but were not subject to servitude. Internally, 士 believed they were equal to everyone, yet carried a sense of responsibility. 士 were independent and confident, yet always striving for self-improvement.

Today, 士 have three levels:

1. The highest level is the stakeholder. You are an interested party in a certain cause, a shareholder, with control. Chinese people often say, “When the country prospers or declines, every common person has a responsibility.” But in reality, you may know that even if something is related to you, your opinion may not count. However, you can still play a good role in the things you have control over. The bigger the matter, the greater your responsibility.

2. The middle level is the player. When we discussed game theory in Season Three, we mentioned player many times. A player may not be able to manage others’ affairs, but at least they feel in control of their own fate. A player refuses to accept others’ arrangements and always wants to think about the rules, trying to find a better decision.

3. The most basic level is called informed citizen in English, a term not commonly used in China. We can call it “a person who understands.” If I have to accept this situation, at least let me understand what is happening. When I was a graduate student taking a quantum mechanics course, some American elders came to listen in. I asked them why they studied quantum mechanics, and they said we are informed citizens, and we want to understand how the world works.

Through Wang’s analysis of 士, you may have already matched each level with your own examples. What I want to talk about now is “national 士,” or 国士. You might be thinking, here comes the topic I’m about to discuss—two national 士!

Let me share two stories from my life:

The first story is about a picky eater. Friends who know me might know I’m now quite fat, but most people don’t know I was very thin before middle school, due to picky eating and not eating well. Everyone knows the poem “Mǐn Nóng” (Mǐn Nóng), and parents often emphasize that food is the hard work of farmers. The logic is clear, but it doesn’t feel the same. It wasn’t until one summer when I returned to my hometown and saw my grandfather harvesting rice. It took several steps to finally get a few bags, and then they had to send it to factories for processing to become the rice we eat. The income from this hard work was minimal, and I once thought that amount of money wasn’t worth it. Yet the person working hard in the fields never complained, writing his research and papers on the vast Chinese land.

China is an agricultural country. For many years, we have restricted commerce. Our farmland is limited, and if too many people go into business, who would provide the basic food? Due to our national conditions, we have to consider the stability of the country, with food as our foundation. Agriculture has always been a priority in every dynasty. I wonder how many 90s-born people have ever gone hungry. Our parents experienced it, but we haven’t. Is this just the way it is?

Yuan Longping dedicated his life to the country, bringing warmth to the world. Our country is the world’s largest population, and food is our foundation. Yuan Longping planted the seed his mother once gave him on the land of his homeland, and we no longer worry about hunger. High yield, higher yield, ultra-high yield—Yuan Longping kept breaking records! Devoted to one thing, others may find it hard, but Yuan found joy in it. He spent his life doing this groundbreaking work. Young people, why are we still being lazy and hesitant? Shouldn’t we contribute something ourselves? For the country, for the people, for ourselves, let’s work hard and practice it!

President Xi Jinping highly praised Yuan Longping’s major contributions to China’s food security, agricultural technological innovation, and global food development. He called on all Party members, officials, and science and technology workers to learn from Yuan Longping, emphasizing that the best way to honor Yuan Longping is to learn his love for the Party, the country, and the people, his firm belief and unwavering commitment, his courage for innovation and modesty, and his dedication to the needs of the country and the people. He urged us to learn his noble qualities of working tirelessly in the fields, writing scientific papers on the land of our motherland.

The second story is about my grandfather, who was diagnosed with stomach cancer during my senior year of university. I didn’t know how to decide at first, but the trust of my family made me take charge of taking him to the hospital for check-ups. I visited major hospitals in Beijing, Shanghai, Nanjing, and Hefei, but I didn’t know what to do. I searched online, looked up information everywhere, and learned about Professor Wu Mengchao. He was an expert in liver and biliary surgery. In our initial tests, we couldn’t determine the exact location of the lesion, so I wanted to find Wu Mengchao to get his opinion. As the tests progressed, the diagnosis came back as stomach cancer that had spread to various parts of the body, especially near the liver and biliary system. After over a year of chemotherapy, my grandfather passed away.

I’ve read many reports about Wu Mengchao, and one scene stayed with me. At the age of 90, he was still working in the operating room, giving his life to medicine and achieving excellence in his field!

Professor Wu Mengchao has been a doctor for 78 years, treating over 16,000 patients. His right hand has held many awards, but it fits the shape of a hemostatic clamp best. He said, “I am just Wu Mengchao, and treating patients is my duty.”

“What is more important to me, Wu Mengchao’s reputation or a person’s life?” This direct question to the soul reveals Wu Mengchao’s values and life pursuit. If he had considered personal fame and fortune, he could have retired early, enjoying a peaceful old age with all the honors. Instead, at the age of 90, he still fought on the surgical frontline, driven by a doctor’s sense of responsibility and compassion for his patients. Most people work hard their whole lives, often trapped by fame and wealth, but among them are some outstanding individuals whose lifelong pursuit has already transcended fame and fortune. They don’t care about personal honor or loss, taking it as their duty to serve the country and the people. In the process of realizing their life’s value, they benefit the nation and its people. We call these outstanding individuals the backbone of the nation, and Wu Mengchao is undoubtedly one of them.
