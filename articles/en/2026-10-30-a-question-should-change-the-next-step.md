# A Question Should Change the Next Step

> 发布日期:2026-10-30 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-30-问完以后，下一步会变吗.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-30-a-question-should-change-the-next-step.md)

---

Two tools can ask you a question and appear equally attentive. One puts your answer into a form and carries on with the original plan. The other discovers that the plan is wrong and changes what happens next. Now that AI can produce a convincing piece of work quickly, I pay more attention to the question it asks before it starts: could your answer actually change its work? If it could not, the question merely makes waiting feel like participation. I want to work out what it takes for a question to have consequences.

A clipping about an AI presentation tool describes confirming the audience before generation, choosing the direction of the story, and inspecting the result afterward. The number of models behind the tool matters less to this argument than the choice it leaves with the person while the direction can still change. This is easy to get wrong. A product may collect preferences and then generate what it was always going to generate. Or it may ask so many questions that you complete a lengthy questionnaire before seeing anything useful. When should it ask, what should it ask, and where should the answer go? I propose one test: after the answer, a different next step must be possible. This is my design suggestion, not a result those studies have tested.

## A question must be able to overturn a decision

Suppose a user asks for a feature in a tool you are building. Recording the request is sensible. But you also need to learn when the user gets stuck, and whether the feature would make them work differently now. Otherwise the answer is only an item on a wish list. AI can turn a wish list into an attractive requirements document in seconds. It cannot make the choice of which wish matters simply by expanding the list.

[Steve Blank recalled a mistake he made on the MIPS chip project](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/). He asked prospective customers which order the chip should use to store data. Customers with systems using one order insisted on that order; customers using the other insisted on the opposite. The team supported both. The hardware engineers saw a modest cost, while the software engineers warned that the choice would create a great deal of work for subsequent development. Looking back in 2009, Blank argued that he had gathered requirements without testing the customer problem and the buying decision behind them. His account shows one way a decision can go wrong; it does not establish the right design for every chip.

I am not trying to decide which storage order they should have chosen. I want to know what they should have asked. Which customers were they trying to serve first? If they supported only one order, would those customers refuse to buy, or could they adapt their systems? Would the additional customers gained by supporting both orders justify the long-term work imposed on the software team? Answers to those questions might change the product route, or reveal that the team chose the wrong initial customer. “I want both options” cannot settle that tradeoff.

An AI product faces the same distinction. Asking whether you prefer a formal or relaxed tone may be a useful appearance setting if both versions can be generated at once. Asking who will read this document and what decision they must make after reading it can change which material belongs in it, how much evidence it needs, and where it should end. The first question has a place when you adjust the surface. Ask the second too late and the whole document may need rebuilding. A question's value lies in what it can change in work that has yet to be done.

There is another test I find easy to miss: are you willing to accept an answer you dislike? If every possible answer leads back to your preferred solution, you are not testing a decision. Before you ask, write the branches down. If the answer is A, what will you do next? If it is B, what will you drop? If the person does not know, what small test will you run first? When you cannot name any different branch, hold the question back. At least do not describe it as giving the user a part in the decision.

[Paul Graham's 2013 account of early startups](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html) gives concrete examples. When someone agreed to try Stripe, its founders would help set it up there and then. Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York to recruit hosts and helped existing hosts improve their listings. These are a practitioner's observations about particular companies, not an experiment guaranteeing the tactic in every industry. Their common point is that close contact exposes the step a user actually has to cross. By setting something up with a person, you may discover an obstacle absent from your instructions. A reply of “I'll try it” leaves that obstacle unseen.

Think of someone trying on a shirt. A customer says they like blue, so the shop assistant brings a blue shirt. When it catches at the shoulders, colour is no longer the first thing to solve. The same mechanism applies here: an initial answer points you in a direction, and use corrects the direction. You can see how a shirt fits on the spot. Whether someone has learned an idea, or will keep using a tool, takes longer to observe. The answer is a clue; what changes when the person acts gives it weight.

## In learning, the question should keep you in the work

There is another side to asking. AI asks you questions, and it can also answer yours. A clear explanation helps. But if it completes the very step you need to practise, the learning outcome may differ. I am not arguing against getting work done. When you need to finish a familiar, routine task, delegation often makes sense. When you are acquiring a new ability, you need an additional question after the work is delivered: could I make the judgment myself with different material next time?

[Shen and Tamkin ran a randomized experiment](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v2) with developers who knew Python but had not used the Trio programming library. A programming library is a collection of ready-made code developers can use; these tasks required them to learn Trio. In the main study, 52 participants attempted tasks with the new library; one group had access to AI, the other did not. Everyone then took an unaided test of conceptual understanding, code reading and debugging. On a 27-point test, the AI group's average score was 4.15 points lower. Completion times differed, but the evidence could not establish whether AI made people faster or slower on this task. This was a short test of learning one unfamiliar library. It cannot establish that every use of AI damages learning. It does show why finishing the assignment and acquiring the ability should be checked separately.

The researchers also watched participants' screen recordings. Some people asked AI to write the code. Some asked conceptual questions. Others took generated code and kept asking why it worked. The higher-scoring patterns generally involved explanation and independent thought. Full delegation could produce a quick result without the same understanding on the later test. Participants chose their own ways of using AI, so differences between these patterns are not a randomized causal comparison. Still, they point to a practical question: does AI's response send you back into the task?

Here is a question I propose, rather than one those studies directly tested. When you are learning a new concept, AI could first ask, “Which rule do you expect caused this error?” and then give a hint. If your prediction is wrong, the system can choose a rule to explain; if it is right, it can offer a different example for you to judge. The answers take the work in different directions, so the question has a chance to help. If the system supplies a complete solution and only then asks “Do you understand?”, “I think so” may be all you can say. That is a weakness in the question. It has not asked you to make a judgment that can be checked.

Another study supplies a necessary counterexample. [In a 2026 randomized field experiment, Contractor and Reyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849v2) had 211 undergraduates learn an unfamiliar topic and write an essay under a time limit in the first session. One group could use ordinary generative AI; the other could not. Both groups then took the first test without AI. Of those students, 205 returned roughly a week later for another test and essay without AI. Students allowed to use AI did better on average on the first unaided test. A week later, the estimated difference in correct answers was still 3.9 percentage points in their favor, but the evidence was too uncertain to establish that the advantage remained. The researchers also examined how students used the tool. Gains held up better among those who asked AI to explain the material; students who had it draft their work submitted strong essays at first but did not retain the same advantage in unaided writing. Access was randomly assigned. Students chose how to use AI, so the comparison between those uses is observational.

Placed together, the two studies are not a vote in which one paper wins. The AI group learned less in one experiment; access to AI improved average performance in the other. The participants, tasks, duration and ways of using the tools differed. Together they make me replace “Does AI help learning?” with a question I can inspect: which part of the work did you hand over? With an unfamiliar skill, explanations, hints and follow-up questions may preserve your own reasoning. Having the tool write the whole response may remove the judgment you needed to practise. What happens still depends on the task; no clever prompt settles it in advance.

Learning also needs a test with new material. [Across four experiments reported in 2017, Butler and colleagues](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c8baca1e5f7d136349ea789/t/5e739a3d46338e7dec37aaf6/1584634429337/Butler%2Bet%2Bal%2B2017.pdf) compared repeatedly answering the same application question about geology with answering different application questions. Two days later, participants who had practised with different examples did better on new examples. “Transfer” here simply means that you can use the same idea when the question changes. The evidence concerns the geology concepts and the two-day interval in those experiments. It suggests a plain test for AI questions: can the tool invite you to judge a new example before it shows you feedback?

That is why “Do you understand?” does not convince me. It asks for a feeling; if the next screen appears whatever you say, the answer has not changed the explanation. A changed example requires you to choose and explain first; your error can then guide what the tool explains next. A correct answer may be a lucky guess, so asking for the reason matters too. If you get it wrong, the useful response is to find the condition you misread, not automatically to add ten paragraphs of theory. Here the question has a diagnostic job. A polished explanation based on a wrong diagnosis can waste everyone's time.

## Let the answer into the next step

You might object that a tool need not turn every task into a test. Fair point. Someone may open an AI tool simply to organize material quickly or complete a repetitive task they have no need to learn again. Requiring a prediction, an explanation and a reflection would take back the time the tool saved. If the result is easy to check, the cost of an error is low, and the user has no reason to build a skill, asking less and acting faster can be the right choice. A question with consequences may lead to the conclusion that no question is needed here.

Other moments deserve one. If you are explaining something to readers you do not yet understand, choosing the wrong audience can change the whole article. If you are learning a skill for which you will later be responsible, skipping the reasoning can affect your next decision. If you are developing a product, a customer's stated desire and their actual adoption are separated by real work. A worthwhile question in those settings should change at least one of three things: the material, the practice, or the product route. Otherwise even a friendly question is only decoration.

“Consequences” does not mean the machine should immediately obey every answer. People can be mistaken, especially before they have seen a result. Blank's story is a reminder that a customer request can be sincere and the team's response can still be costly. Treat an answer as a direction to test. Generate a small section, show a sample, or provide one limited service by hand. Then watch what the person does. The answer changes the next step, and the next step remains open to correction by reality.

Learning works similarly. A student first says what they think went wrong. The tool uses that answer to give a hint, then asks the student to tackle a changed example. The first answer need not be perfect. Its value is that it exposes a hidden judgment so feedback has something to address. In a product, the user states what they want; the team offers the smallest version that can be tried and observes whether it helps them cross the obstacle. If it does not, revise the assumption rather than add more items from the questionnaire.

I suggest a small check you can try today. This is an exercise I propose, not an outcome established by the studies above. Open an AI workflow you already use and find the last question it asks you. Before answering, write down two possible replies and which step the system ought to change for each. Try both and see whether the output, explanation or action actually differs. If only the pleasantries change, remove the question. If the answer could change the direction but arrives after the product has been generated, move it earlier. Leave yourself one record of what you expected to matter and what changed when you tested it.

This check does not require a dozen new AI rules or a long essay from every user. It gives a question one small acceptance test. Ask which decision the answer changed, and how you will know whether that change was right. If you can answer both parts, the question may deserve the person's attention. If you cannot, let the tool do its work quietly.

The two kinds of questions at the start may look separated by nothing more than a form. I would rather look at the paths behind it. Where does answer A send the work? What does answer B stop? If the path can truly change, you have asked a real question.
