# Fluent Words Can Hide Unfinished Thoughts

> Published: 2026-10-10 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-10-说顺了不等于想清了.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-10-fluent-words-can-hide-unfinished-thoughts.md)

---

In Stripe's early days, when someone agreed to try the product, its founders would take the person's laptop and set it up on the spot. They did not simply promise to send a signup link. Paul Graham recounts this in [“Do Things that Don't Scale”](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html). The useful parallel is the problem hidden in a first attempt: people do not always state what they need in complete sentences, but the act of trying reveals where they get stuck. AI can now turn a messy spoken thought into tidy prose almost instantly. That is useful. Yet when an idea is still taking shape, I want the original words alongside the polished ones. Fluency is not the same as clarity. Erase a hesitation too early, and you may erase the question you needed to ask next.

## Mistake one: polished text must mean accurate meaning

A saved summary of a comparison of voice input tools draws a useful distinction. Some tools try to record what you said faithfully; others reorganize and rewrite it. That distinction matters more to me than the product rankings. We tend to group recognition accuracy, speed, and formatting under the single heading of “better input.” We ask less often whether the tool changed a word or changed our judgment.

Correcting a misheard word or putting a period in the right place usually repairs a loss in transmission. Removing uncertainty, or merging several unsettled options into one conclusion, makes a choice on the speaker's behalf. Both operations produce neater text on the screen. They carry different responsibilities. One resembles proofreading; the other resembles editing or even ghostwriting. Either can help, provided we know which authority we handed over.

Rough speech is not automatically more truthful. We misspeak, circle around a point, and repeat filler that adds nothing. Saving that effort is valuable. In a [2023 writing experiment](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf), Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang assigned short, specified writing tasks to 444 college-educated professionals. On the second task, the group given access to ChatGPT took about 37% less time on average than the control group, while its work received ratings about 0.45 standard deviations higher. A standard deviation expresses the difference relative to the spread of scores in that group; it does not mean an increase of 0.45 points. This version was a working paper that had not yet been peer reviewed. It measured immediate output on assigned tasks, not whether spoken thoughts survived a rewrite or whether writing skill improved over years.

That boundary matters. The paper also reports that 68% of treated participants said they submitted ChatGPT's initial output without editing it. For those assignments, this could be perfectly sensible. The prompt was defined, the work was judged as a finished piece, and delivering an acceptable piece quickly was the point. But if I am still looking for a problem I have not named, the hesitations in my first draft may be part of the material. A smooth answer can persuade me that the problem already has an accurate name. The part most likely to disappear is the part that has no name yet.

The same paper found no evidence that participants' edits raised average ratings above the raw AI output. That pushes me to choose a method according to the task. For an email with a clear purpose and a need for appropriate wording, I can let a machine draft it and check the facts. For a discussion with myself that will shape a decision, a high-scoring piece of prose does not prove I have thought it through. A rating tells me how well a draft performed in that assignment. It cannot tell me which hesitation deserves another question. Confusing those measures is the danger.

So I would draw a plain line for voice input: did the tool quietly change the degree of certainty? If a tentative thought becomes a claim, or something that needs checking becomes something presented as confirmed, I count that as an error even when the grammar is perfect. This matters in personal writing, and even more in a work record. Attractive prose cannot certify an accurate meaning.

## Mistake two: the unedited version is too messy to keep

Graham also describes founders using their own software on merchants' behalf to build online stores. His point extends beyond customer service. Working beside a user exposes obstacles that a product description never mentions; sometimes a missing feature becomes obvious halfway through the job. His essay is an account of startup experience, not a controlled test showing that every company should copy the tactic. What I take from it is a way to observe: do not rush to translate another person's difficulty into a feature name you already know.

Natural speech works similarly. It may repeat itself, wander, or stop without a tidy ending. Yet a pause, or two concerns tangled together, can signal that the speaker has not separated the questions. Edited text is easier to send onward; the original may be better for asking a follow-up. The versions answer different questions. Throwing the original away leaves the processed result without a way to inspect what the processing changed.

The original should not become sacred, either. A repeated word may signal importance, or it may be a speech habit. A pause may signal doubt, or someone may simply be searching for a word. I would not diagnose my own thinking, let alone someone else's, from one pause. A sounder move is to compare the two versions and ask a narrower question: did this edit add a fact, or did it change a conclusion? The original offers a clue. Confirmation still requires a question, evidence, and the speaker's own choice.

It is easy to understand why a photographer keeps the original image. The adjusted photo is ready to share; the original lets you check whether the colors drifted. Spoken words and edited prose have a similar relationship: one version is convenient to use, the other helps you inspect the changes. But a photographed scene is fixed at the moment of capture. A person's thinking continues to move while they speak and revise. The first words are therefore not an eternally correct answer. They mark a point in time from which I can see what I changed and why.

In the revised version of [“1,000 True Fans”](https://kk.org/thetechnium/1000-true-fans/), Kevin Kelly emphasizes direct relationships with supporters. His example of a thousand supporters each yielding a hundred dollars in annual profit illustrates one possible livelihood; he explicitly says the number must be adjusted to the creator's circumstances. The original 2008 essay, included below the revision on the same page, also says that direct contact brings feedback as well as payment. The relationship interests me more than the target. If feedback passes through platform metrics, form choices, and automatic summaries before it reaches a creator, the creator may see the outcome without hearing why a person stayed or left. That is my extension of Kelly's argument, not a study he conducted on voice tools.

Keeping another person's voice or words requires their knowledge and a clear limit on what is retained. Anything made public needs permission and verification. The practice I suggest here can begin with my own spoken drafts. It does not require turning somebody else's private conversation into material. The more useful the raw information, the more restraint it deserves. Keeping an original is a means of checking meaning, not a reason to collect every record.

## Mistake three: a smoother process means faster learning

Rewriting tools offer a quieter temptation: they spare me the places where my own explanation breaks down. The typos are gone, the paragraphs are in place, and the connecting phrases appear. The text looks as though I have finished thinking, while someone else may have done the organizing I had not yet done. The output can be good without a matching gain in my ability. That is a risk to test, not a verdict one can draw from how easy or difficult the process feels.

Research on learning gives me a useful mirror. In [a 2016 paper on deliberate practice](https://ggsc.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Eskreis-Winkler_2016_Using_Wise_Interventions_to_Motivate_Deliberate_Practice.pdf), Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and colleagues conducted four longitudinal randomized field experiments with students. They randomly assigned students to different lessons in real school settings, then compared outcomes later. In this work, deliberate practice meant working on a weakness just beyond one's current comfort, receiving feedback, and trying again with attention to the error. Brief interventions about the value of such practice helped some lower-achieving students. One study still found an improvement in practice behavior after a month; the paper's abstract says another did not find the same behavioral improvement after four months. These were studies of students. They did not establish that retaining a voice transcript improves an adult's writing.

A [2021 study in undergraduate physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-021-00110-x) offers another contrast. In a course held in 2020, 350 students encountered two homework schedules over eight weeks: practicing the same type of problem in a block, or alternating among types. The alternating work felt harder and led students to judge their learning more poorly. Yet they performed better on later unannounced tests with new problems. Two subsequent high-stakes midterm exams showed no significant advantage. Those were physics problems, not voice drafts; the result cannot simply be transferred to writing. It does remind me that present fluency and later ability to handle change are different measures.

Together, these studies prompt a practice I propose trying: let AI remove mechanical barriers while reserving one point where I must make the judgment myself. This is my proposal, not a voice-writing method tested by either study. Speech recognition can do the typing, and a tool can divide the text into paragraphs. When a cause is unclear, a fact is unverified, or a conclusion is still moving, I want to review that passage. This division may be slower. It may also show me what I have not mastered. If I cannot tell which sentence the tool supplied, it will be hard to practice that choice deliberately the next time it appears.

That changes how I view revision history. We tend to admire the final draft and treat earlier versions as debris. But a writing skill often lives in the reason for a change. Why did “might” become “will”? Why was a condition removed? Why were two questions made into one? Picking the nicer-sounding version improves the surface. Explaining the choice gives me something I might carry into the next piece. No study here guarantees that my method works, so I need to try it on a different spoken draft and see whether I can recognize the same distinctions without help.

There is a serious objection. Some people rely on voice tools to express themselves at all. Others are trying to finish a job and do not need every short message turned into a writing lesson. Fair enough. Trapping people in cumbersome raw transcripts can waste attention and block expression. My proposal is for material that forms a judgment, explains a complex issue, or must preserve who is responsible for a claim. When the facts are settled and the only aim is communication, use the edited version. Tools should remove a burden. Practice should be reserved for a point worth practicing.

## Mistake four: keeping an original means giving up AI editing

The two versions can fit into a short workflow. First, speak and keep the initial recognition transcript. Then ask the tool for a readable version. Before using it, compare only three kinds of change: did it add a fact, turn uncertainty into certainty, or collapse several choices into one conclusion? If it did, return to the original and ask whether the edit has grounds. Restore or mark as unconfirmed what has no support. The rest—typos, punctuation, empty repetition—can go to the machine.

This does not require permanent storage of every recording. For my own exploratory writing, I can retain the first transcript at least until the piece is finished and its important judgments are checked, then clear it according to need. When another person's views are involved, I must first establish whether I may record and use them, then retain them only as agreed. Convenience and restraint can coexist. What really consumes time is finding, a week later, that a lovely conclusion has no traceable basis and having to think through the whole issue again.

There is a quieter failure, too: I might carefully compare versions each time, fix the current sentence, and miss a recurring pattern. If a tool repeatedly turns reservations into statements of certainty, I can reduce its rewriting strength or ask it to preserve uncertainty and qualifying phrases. If it cannot do that, I can switch to a more faithful mode. Revision history then improves how I use the tool as well as how I write. The tool gets the words onto the page. I decide which meanings it may alter and which ones require a question first.

This is something to try today without changing input tools. Choose a learning plan or work idea you were going to dictate anyway. Keep both the recognition transcript and the edited version. Do not begin by asking which looks better. Mark the three kinds of change above, then write one sentence: did the edited version make a judgment I had not made? If it did not, use it with confidence. If it did, turn that spot back into a question to investigate. On the next pass, you can check what effort the tool saved you and which judgments still belong to you.

I also want a standard for my own experiment. If I do this a few times and end up merely saving two files, with no change in my judgment, I should stop. Collecting a trace is not growth by itself. The trace earns its place only when I can point to a change in certainty that I caught and use it to revise a piece or ask a better question next time. That standard is stricter than keeping a streak, and more honest.

When I think again of the laptop Stripe's founders borrowed on the spot, the useful detail is not that they were willing to press a few extra keys. They did not let a convenient signup link replace the problem right in front of them. I want to give my own words the same chance: clean them up when that helps, and leave the part I have not understood on the table a little longer.
