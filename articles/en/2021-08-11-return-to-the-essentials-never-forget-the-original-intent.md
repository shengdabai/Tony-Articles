125th Original Piece

The mindset of returning to the essentials is the hardest to learn, especially similar to the concept of "first principles." In work, if things become more complex as you do them, you shouldn't get lost in the complexity and forget to think. In life, the most common mistake we make is forgetting why we started in the first place, eventually getting lost in "greed, anger, and ignorance."

Returning to the essentials sounds easy, but it's hard to do. The difficulty lies in two aspects: first, the limitations of one's own ability—knowledge, expertise, and insight may prevent you from finding the essence even if you want to. Second, the limitations of one's own mindset—emotions and broad-mindedness can all block a person from stepping out of the surface and returning to the essence.

The professor played a clip from *Thirteen Reasons Why*, and Mr. Xu Zhuoyun's words opened my mind. Let me share my thoughts.

Mr. Xu Zhuoyun is a historian. Born in 1930 into a prominent family in Jiangnan, he suffered from congenital muscular atrophy. He earned a doctorate from the University of Chicago and became a professor at the University of Pittsburgh in 1970, becoming one of the most renowned Chinese scholars of his time. He was elected as a member of the Academia Sinica in Taiwan in 1980. His representative works include *A Study of the Social History of Ancient China*, *The History of the Western Zhou Dynasty*, and *Ten Thousand Rivers and Rivers*.

Mr. Xu said that today's education produces ordinary people, people who live their daily lives. Our current intellectuals are online intellectuals, search machines, not thinkers. Today's books, information, and search tools are abundant. As long as one is willing to put in effort, a person can independently find their way through basic reading skills and minimal thinking training. "To establish the mind of heaven and earth, to secure the destiny of the people, to carry forward the lost learning of the ancients, and to open peace for all generations." This is the duty of intellectuals or scholars.

Looking back at the great Axial Age, philosophers in ancient Greece were thinking by the seaside, philosophers in India were meditating by the Ganges, and philosophers in China were walking by the Yellow River. The questions they raised still echo in our minds today. Generations have followed up, making annotations. But now, fewer and fewer people are tackling big questions, because the answers from the past are right in front of us, like McDonald's—fast food, a tool for satisfying hunger. Therefore, today's university education is disappointing, especially American university education, which has the biggest flaw: it's fragmented.

So, when we're searching for the meaning of work and life today, we should dare to think about big questions. I've always believed that we should have a dream, whether it can be realized or how long it might take, but it must exist. For me, the meaning of my work is to help more people who love language and want to communicate with many people around the world. Although I've only been engaged in English education for two years, the fire in my heart is rising steadily.

Sometimes, we teachers joke around, looking around, seeing skyscrapers, cross-sea bridges, domestically produced large aircraft, 5G communication, big data, artificial intelligence, and so on. The changes over the past few decades of reform and opening up have been earth-shattering, truly astonishing. Yet, English education has remained largely unchanged. The current model is still the standardized classroom education pioneered by Prussia hundreds of years ago. Reforming education fundamentally depends on reforming exams and assessments. The current college entrance exam is, in fact, a desperate measure. With technological progress, I believe there will eventually be an opportunity. The transformation of English education is ongoing. I hope to shift from a teacher-centered approach to a student-centered one, from traditional lecture-based classrooms to personalized education, and from passive listening to immersive experiential learning. I've seen many institutions and schools trying these approaches. I look forward to the future of schools and feel confident about it. My work and mission are to contribute a little to the cause of education, hoping that everyone can become a language expert.

Now, talking about the meaning of life, my life has been pretty smooth so far. I'm 25 years old this year, and I've had many opportunities to make mistakes. I'm grateful for the chaotic learning and the deep connection I've formed with it. I've already booked five years of chaotic learning ahead, and will I continue? Definitely. Also, with platforms like *Dedao*, *Fandeng Reading*, and *Kanxiang*, their company and support have accompanied me. The knowledge I've gained is effective, but the scope of my understanding has been continuously broadened. I dare to think, I'm willing to think, and I'm striving to build my own thinking framework, drawing out my own knowledge trees, and constantly adding branches and leaves. Following the professor, I'm transitioning from emotional thinking to idealistic thinking, and I can slightly glimpse the philosophical scientific thinking on the third floor. Looking up at the world of ideas, thinking about being present in the moment, searching for the origin of "one"—can such a life be without meaning? Let's resonate together, collide our thoughts, how beautiful it is (✪▽✪)
