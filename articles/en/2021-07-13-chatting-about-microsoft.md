Microsoft Case Study

For a long time, Microsoft was seen as a company suffering from "The Innovator's Dilemma." This is a common topic in chaos learning, where Professor Christensen's in-depth research on disruptive innovation explains why large companies are almost destined to be overturned by smaller innovative companies. Microsoft was the king of the personal computer era, but later missed a series of innovations, such as its slow response to the internet, falling behind competitors in search, social networking, and later mobile internet. It seemed to have fallen behind the times. However, to everyone's surprise, Microsoft performed a "big elephant dance." Today, Microsoft's market value is close to $600 billion, ranking second among tech companies after Apple and Google, surpassing Amazon and Facebook. In addition to its traditional stronghold in office software, Microsoft also has strong competitiveness in hardware, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence. Microsoft also entered the social networking field through the acquisition of LinkedIn. Since Satya Nadella took over as CEO in February 2014, Microsoft's stock price has more than doubled, and its market value has increased by $250 billion, surpassing its peak during the dot-com bubble in March 2000.

Why was Microsoft able to rise again? The October issue of *Fast Company* magazine's cover story, "Microsoft Reboot," introduced some of the things Satya Nadella did after becoming CEO. The magazine quoted Brad Smith, Microsoft's president and chief legal officer, saying that Nadella not only changed Microsoft's business strategy but also reformed its corporate culture, "Nadella has always been committed to changing Microsoft's way of operation by changing its way of thinking." Three points are particularly important in the mindset Nadella spread throughout Microsoft.

First is empathy. One of the first things Nadella did after becoming CEO in February 2014 was to require the company's senior executives to read the book *Nonviolent Communication*. When Bill Gates and Steve Ballmer were CEOs, Microsoft's corporate culture was competitive and "showing off IQ." Everyone would spend a lot of time preparing before meetings, which were not for discussing problems but for being tested. Nadella said, "Bill isn't the kind of person who would walk into the office to praise you for doing a good job. He would say: first, I'm going to point out the 20 things you got wrong today." Nadella was more gentle. He believed that humans are born with empathy, and empathy can help create a harmonious work environment and is more conducive to creating products that resonate with people. He preferred to understand, "Where does this person come from? What motivates their work? Why do they get excited or discouraged about something?"

Second is an insatiable curiosity to learn everything. Nadella inspired Microsoft's 124,000 employees to "learn everything," whereas the company's previous mindset was famously "know everything." Nadella once quoted the poet T. S. Eliot to describe Microsoft's goal: "You should never stop exploring, and at the end of all exploration, you will arrive at the starting point with a completely new understanding." Under this mindset, Microsoft also began to change its relationship with the outside world. For example, Microsoft started to build partnerships with companies it had previously viewed as competitors. Microsoft developed Office for Apple's iPad. Nadella himself also regularly visited Silicon Valley. Steve Ballmer once called the Linux open-source system "cancer," but after Nadella became CEO, Microsoft joined the Linux Foundation.

Third is a growth mindset. Nadella has mentioned this many times, influenced by the two thinking models proposed by Carol Dweck, a professor at Stanford University: fixed mindset and growth mindset. People with a fixed mindset are unwilling to take the risk of failure and prefer to use the skills they already have. People with a growth mindset understand that they can't be successful at everything, but they are happy to learn new things and accept failure. After becoming CEO, Nadella once faced a PR crisis. At an event, when asked what advice he had for women who were afraid to ask for a raise, Nadella replied, "The focus isn't on asking for a raise, but on clearly believing that as you grow and progress, the company will give you the right incentives. This might be another superpower that women who don't ask for a raise have, because it's about building good relationships. It will give you returns, and in the long run, it can help you solve problems." His words quickly spread on social media and attracted a lot of criticism. Nadella quickly admitted his mistake and publicly stated that his remarks were "nonsense from a man who enjoys privileges." His attitude also showed that we can make mistakes, but we can learn from them and do better.
