# The Demand That Never Got a Quote

> 发布日期：2026-10-26 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-26-那些没拿到报价的需求.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-26-the-demand-that-never-got-a-quote.md)

---

Some needs may disappear even though someone could meet them. The obstacle may be the work of finding out whether the job is worth taking. For a small custom order, the buyer must explain its purpose, and the supplier must check materials, labor, and delivery dates. Both sides must spend time before any transaction begins. I want to examine a proposal that has yet to be tested: use AI to organize an inquiry so a request otherwise dismissed as too much trouble gets a responsible first assessment. If that assessment becomes cheaper, some business that was never worth asking about might become possible.

## Why does nobody take the small order?

Working out whether to take it may already cost too much. An AI-generated interview summary raises a possibility: quoting a small batch of custom metal parts requires reading a drawing, choosing materials, and estimating machine time and a delivery date. The order may be too small to justify all that preliminary judgment. That raises a question beyond how fast a factory can make the part: who is willing to inspect the request in the first place? The summary proposes a scenario and a possible response, not a research finding; the material does not document time saved at any particular factory.

Suppose a small workshop receives a drawing for a custom bracket. The buyer wants only a few pieces but has yet to settle on the material, tolerance (how far the part's dimensions may vary from the drawing), or delivery date. If the workshop follows its full process for a large order, it may spend substantial staff time before it knows whether the buyer will proceed. If it gives a rough price instead, it may promise a date it cannot meet. The easiest response is to decline. But that single response hides two very different situations: the workshop cannot do the job, or it cannot afford to find out whether it can.

Think of a restaurant asked to serve a small party with special requirements. The cook has to ask how many people are coming, what they cannot eat, and when they will arrive before settling on a menu. Asking and checking take time too. If every small party requires a lengthy staff meeting, small parties stop getting attention. Industrial quotes are much more complex than meals: a misread drawing can mean rework and liability. The comparison explains the cost of assessment; it does not replace a technical review.

AI's first useful job is to turn scattered emails, drawing notes, and past orders into a request a person can inspect. Which conditions are known? Which are missing? Is there a comparable past part? What must be asked of the buyer? It can turn “I don't understand this order” into “these two conditions remain open.” If that works reliably, the person responsible for the quote can spend more time on the choices that require experience. The time saved is in collecting and organizing information. Responsibility for the promise stays with a person.

There is a second change here. When assessing a small order is expensive, a workshop naturally looks at larger orders first. When a preliminary assessment gets cheaper, the small order does not automatically become profitable, but it can finally be compared on its merits. What looks like “no demand” may sometimes mean that neither side can afford to articulate the demand clearly enough. That is my inference, and it holds only if production, delivery, and support still have capacity. Writing quotes faster cannot create those capacities.

## Is a fast quote enough?

A quote must know what it cannot guess. A complete-looking price may omit a material grade, confuse machining time with delivery time, or assume the buyer accepts a substitute. Then it merely delays the discovery of a disagreement. AI is good at filling blanks. A business commitment often requires leaving a blank visible until someone with the right authority confirms it. A fast wrong quote can cost more than an honest request for missing information.

[Steve Blank wrote in 2009 about an experience at MIPS](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/). Customers asked for two different ways of ordering data, so he recommended supporting both on the chip. The hardware change looked modest, but the software team warned of a lasting burden for developers. His lesson was to test a requested solution against the actual customer problem and the total cost of implementing it. This is his firsthand chip-design account, not an experiment on AI quoting today.

Back at the workshop, a buyer's “Can you do it faster?” might mean an assembly date is fixed. Or the buyer may simply want to know the available options. A system that automatically converts the question into a shorter deadline, perhaps even with a lower price, makes a decision the buyer may not have requested. A better first move is to confirm the critical date and explain what changes under expedited work, a different specification, or the original plan. Understanding a request and accepting every proposed solution are different things.

Someone could reasonably argue that once AI makes quoting cheap, a small workshop should send more quotes and correct the mistakes later. I see the appeal. Requests that used to be ignored finally get through the door, and excessive caution loses orders. But if a quote enters a purchasing system and shapes the buyer's production schedule, an error wastes more than an email. Someone else starts planning around a price and date that do not exist. “Quote more” therefore needs a boundary: an automatically prepared estimate must state its conditions, while a formal quote still needs a person to check the constraints that matter.

[In 2025, Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers who used generative AI, collecting 936 examples of its use at work](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf). Within these self-reported tasks, greater confidence in AI's ability to do a task was associated with less reported critical thinking. Here, “critical thinking” includes setting a goal, asking AI a suitable question, checking its answer, and deciding how to use the result. This survey measures reported practices and effort; it is not a long-term causal test of whether AI makes people worse thinkers. It suggests that a quoting process should name who checks materials, who checks delivery, and who may make a commitment.

The more useful thing to automate may be a judgment that can be questioned rather than a number alone. What conditions support the estimate? Which changed condition would require recalculation? Where is the system uncertain? The buyer gets options that can actually be compared, and the workshop gets a boundary that does not quietly expand its obligations. That speed may look less impressive in a demo, but it is closer to a speed a business can keep using.

## How do we know the need is real?

Look at what the buyer does next. Saying “I need this,” supplying the missing specifications, confirming a scope, and accepting a test piece are signals of different strength. Cheaper quoting may raise the number of inquiries without raising the number of orders. Once the entrance is wider, we still need to observe what happens between the inquiry and the next step. Otherwise, the workshop may simply be processing more conversations with no result, only faster.

[In a 2013 essay, Paul Graham described what Stripe's founders did early on](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html): when somebody agreed to try the product, they would help install it on the spot instead of merely sending a link. He also described Airbnb's founders going door to door in New York and helping hosts improve their listings. Both cases show how labor-intensive contact can reveal exactly where a user gets stuck. These are selected startup examples, not proof that the same tactic will grow every workshop. Still, watching the next action gets us closer to a real need than collecting polite praise.

For the bracket drawing, an inexpensive first reply could say, “We need to confirm the material and tolerance before giving a formal price.” If the buyer supplies them, the workshop learns more than the drawing's parameters. It learns which conditions matter most to this buyer. If the buyer does not reply, that does not necessarily mean the need is fictional: internal approval may be pending, or the buyer may be comparing processes. Labeling every silence “no demand” makes the market look smaller than it is.

I would therefore record an inquiry as a sequence of actions: what arrived, what was missing, what a person asked, how the buyer responded, and whether a quote the workshop could honor resulted. Seen this way, AI is more than a secretary that writes prices. It can help show which kinds of request repeatedly stop at the same point. If many small orders stall at material choice, the workshop might first explain the available materials. If they stall at delivery dates, it should examine its capacity estimates. Find the recurring obstacle before building a tool to remove it.

There is a temptation here to keep only the customers who eventually buy and then conclude that successful customers all look alike. Rejected drawings, inquiries that never became quotes, and quotes that got no response are observations too. Buyers who stay may already know the industry, describe their needs well, and have larger budgets. If we only look at them, AI may learn who can write an impressive inquiry email rather than who has a worthwhile problem.

That is another reason to keep people involved early. A person can ask the one question the system has not yet learned to ask, then add that question to the next version of the process. Automate it once the same judgment recurs. If the whole intake process must be unattended from day one, the team may move quickly without knowing whom it turned away.

## Does repetition make the team better?

Repetition alone does not. When a quote is wrong, the useful question is whether the error came from reading the drawing, estimating cost, understanding the buyer, or promising a delivery date. If a system saves only the final price, it can make the same guess next time. If it keeps the reason for the estimate and what actually happened afterward, quoting may become a series of opportunities to learn as well as a series of deliveries.

[A 2023 working paper by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31161/revisions/w31161.rev0.pdf) studied 5,179 customer-support agents at one company. After a real-time AI assistant was introduced, the authors estimated an average 13.8% increase in productivity, measured as issues successfully resolved per hour. Less experienced agents benefited more. The paper is careful about a crucial distinction: newer agents reached higher output faster, but the researchers say they cannot tell whether those agents learned the job or were simply following recommendations. Support chats have extensively recorded outcomes; custom manufacturing quotes do not have identical conditions, so that figure cannot be transferred to the workshop.

That distinction is exactly what interests me. If AI helps produce a plausible quote today, will the person in charge better understand tomorrow which jobs to accept and which conditions to ask about, even with AI switched off? If the answer remains no, the tool may have improved delivery speed without improving the team's judgment. For a small team, that matters. Models and prices change; recorded reasons let the next person avoid a mistake already made.

[Ericsson and Harwell revisited the conditions for purposeful practice in 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6824411/): improvement needs a specific goal, feedback that reveals the gap, and another attempt after adjustment. Merely putting in more hours is not the same as practicing what needs improvement. “Deliberate practice” in its strict sense also involves training individually designed by a qualified teacher; a workshop should not casually apply that label to a quote review. I am drawing a simpler action from their account: locate a specific error, inspect the evidence, change the method, and see whether the next similar order goes better.

If a material cost was underestimated, record which price was used, when it was updated, and who checked it. If a buyer declined the quote, try to distinguish a price objection from a delivery problem or a canceled need. AI can help classify what is known. It cannot infer a buyer's private motives from silence. Where there is no outcome, write “unknown.” The record is unglamorous, but those unknowns and specific errors are what a team needs before it expands automation.

Some owners may choose to optimize only for quoting speed. Sometimes that is sensible: the product is highly standardized, parameters are clear, and a wrong estimate is detected quickly at low cost. More automation may then be worthwhile. When specifications vary, customers plan other work around a quote, and mistakes emerge late, a person needs to stand before the commitment. These businesses should not follow the same rule of “automate as much as possible.” The boundary depends on the cost of an error and how long it takes to discover one.

## What can we try today?

Pick one kind of small request whose outcome you can check. Do not start with a system for every inquiry. Find a kind of request people have actually made recently, whose conditions are fairly clear, and whose result can later be reviewed. Write down the human steps from receiving that inquiry to sending a reply, especially the step that repeatedly makes somebody search for information. Without this baseline, “AI saved time” has nothing to compare against.

Then remove private and sensitive information from old examples of that kind of request, and ask AI only to organize the inputs: list known conditions, missing conditions, and risks that need a person's decision. Do not let it send a price or deadline. Have the responsible person check each item and decide whether the request merits a formal quote. In the first trial, record what AI missed, whether the follow-up question helped, and which task the person actually did less of. Moving the same manual checking to another screen does not necessarily make the process cheaper.

Apply the same rule to new inquiries. Compare two things: human time from arrival to a trustworthy answer, and whether needs that might once have been dismissed as too much trouble now reach specification confirmation or a formal quote. Both matter. If time falls but false commitments increase, the trial has failed. If more inquiries receive replies but nobody supplies the missing conditions, we have not shown that a new market has opened.

Finally, ask the harder question: did you decline this small order because making it would not pay, or because finding out whether you could make it cost too much? The answers call for different next steps. The first calls for a firm boundary; the second justifies more work on quoting. Giving a small need a fair assessment is already a change. It does not guarantee that every order can be accepted, but it can turn “no” into a decision with reasons behind it.
