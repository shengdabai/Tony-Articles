# Progress Needs a Spoilsport

> 发布日期:2026-07-28 · [中文](../zh/2026-07-28-进步需要一个扫兴者.md) | [English](../en/2026-07-28-progress-needs-a-spoilsport.md)

---

Every age has a person everyone finds annoying.

When the room is full of excitement, this person asks who pays the cost. When everyone is admiring the new machine, this person asks what kind of human being the machine will produce. When the clever people say the future is inevitable, this person says: maybe the future is being sold to us as inevitable because someone does not want us to discuss its shape.

We usually give that person an ugly name.

Backward. Negative. Anti-progress. Too sensitive. A spoilsport.

But after reading today's notes on two hundred years of technological storytelling, I think that word deserves a better job. A mature civilization does not only need inventors, founders, engineers, investors, and users. It also needs trained spoilsports. Not people who hate new tools. People who refuse to let the story of progress delete its bill.

The important question is not whether technology is good or bad. That question is too small, and usually too lazy.

The better question is: when a new technology arrives, does society still have a working correction loop?

## Progress Always Arrives as a Story

Technology never arrives as only a device.

The steam engine did not arrive as only a machine. Railways did not arrive as only tracks. Electricity did not arrive as only wires. The internet did not arrive as only protocols. AI is not arriving as only models.

Each wave arrives wrapped in a story.

The story usually has the same grammar: liberation, abundance, connection, democratization, a wider future, a stronger human being. It says old limits are being broken. It says ordinary people will gain access to power that used to belong to elites. It says we will become more productive, more connected, more creative, more free.

Much of that is true.

That is why the story works.

The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on September 15, 1830. On the same day, William Huskisson, a Member of Parliament, was struck by Stephenson's Rocket and later died.[^1] The fact has become a dark historical footnote: the first age of railway modernity opened with both celebration and blood.

But the celebration continued.

This is not because people were stupid. It is because progress creates real value, and real value produces real momentum. Once a technology starts increasing speed, reach, output, or profit, the story around it becomes harder to interrupt. The train is moving. The factory is running. The network is growing. The model is improving. The future has already been announced.

And once the future has been announced, objection starts sounding like bad manners.

That is the first structural advantage of progress narratives: they speak in visible benefits. Faster travel. Brighter rooms. Cheaper communication. More capable software. The gains are concrete and immediate.

The costs often arrive as atmosphere.

A worker loses bargaining power. A household standard rises until the machine saves no time. A public square becomes an attention market. A learner starts outsourcing the very judgment they hoped AI would strengthen. These costs are real, but they are slower, more distributed, and harder to photograph.

So the person who notices them looks unreasonable at first.

That is the burden of the spoilsport: to see a system cost before the system has learned to name it.

## The Point Is Not to Break the Machine

The Luddites are the simplest example of how badly history treats early critics.

Today, "Luddite" often means someone who irrationally hates technology. But the British National Archives describes a more specific conflict: skilled textile workers protested machinery that threatened their work and wages; in 1812, machine-breaking became punishable by death, and seventeen men were executed the following year.[^2]

That does not make every tactic wise. It does not make every fear correct.

But it does change the moral shape of the story.

They were not simply angry at machines. They were angry at a new economic arrangement being smuggled in under the name of machinery. The machine was visible. The power shift behind the machine was the real issue.

This is still where many arguments about technology go wrong.

We talk as if the choice is "for the tool" or "against the tool." But most serious critics are asking a different set of questions:

- Who owns the upside?
- Who absorbs the downside?
- Which skills are strengthened, and which are quietly removed?
- What kind of dependence is being normalized?
- What cannot be questioned once the word "inevitable" appears?

Those questions do not stop progress. They make progress accountable.

A brake is not the enemy of a car. A test is not the enemy of software. A proofreader is not the enemy of writing. A critic is not the enemy of technology.

The critic is part of the control system.

## The Internet Taught Us This Again

The early internet had one of the most beautiful progress stories ever written.

In 1996, John Perry Barlow published "A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace" through the Electronic Frontier Foundation.[^3] It carried the confidence of that moment: the network as a new space, beyond the old powers, naturally resistant to centralized control.

It was not foolish to believe some of that.

The internet really did lower barriers. It gave ordinary people publishing tools, search, communities, software distribution, global markets, and a strange new ability to learn in public. Many of us built our lives on that opening.

But the same network also became a machine for surveillance, addiction, manipulation, and concentration. The dream of decentralization did not prevent a few platforms from becoming the practical gateways to speech, attention, commerce, and identity. The story of connection did not automatically protect deep relationship. The story of free information did not automatically create free minds.

Then the spoilsports came back with better vocabulary.

Privacy law, platform regulation, antitrust arguments, humane technology design, data rights, algorithmic accountability: these were not born from people who hated the internet. They were born from people who loved reality enough to notice what the first story had hidden. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation entered into force in 2016 and has applied since May 25, 2018.[^4] Whether one likes every detail of GDPR or not, its existence proves a larger point: yesterday's annoying warnings can become tomorrow's basic guardrails.

Criticism is often late.

But without criticism, the guardrails never arrive.

## AI Has Made the Story Stranger

AI has introduced a strange twist.

In earlier technological waves, the promoters usually sold only the bright side. The machine would liberate labor. Electricity would illuminate life. The internet would free knowledge.

With AI, some of the strongest promoters also speak the language of danger. In May 2023, the Center for AI Safety published a one-sentence statement saying that mitigating extinction risk from AI should be a global priority alongside risks like pandemics and nuclear war.[^5] It was signed by major figures in the field.

This is historically unusual.

The person building the machine is also warning that the machine may be dangerous. On the surface, this looks mature. Maybe it is, partly. Powerful technology deserves serious safety work.

But there is another possibility we should keep in view: risk language can also become part of the sales language.

If a company says, "Our technology may be powerful enough to end civilization," it is not only confessing danger. It is also describing importance. The warning can become a halo. The fear can become a moat. The moral seriousness can make the technology feel more inevitable, not less.

That is why AI needs a different kind of spoilsport.

Not someone who merely says "AI is dangerous." That sentence is now easy to absorb. The industry already knows how to print it on a website.

The better spoilsport asks more grounded questions.

What work is being deskilled right now?

Whose data, labor, and attention are being treated as raw material?

Which decisions are being delegated before the human has learned to judge them?

Which AI products make people more capable after six months, and which merely make them more compliant?

Where is the evaluation loop independent from the person or system being evaluated?

That last question matters a lot. In my own work with AI systems, the most useful pattern is rarely "trust the machine more." It is usually: build a loop where the machine acts, evidence is captured, another standard checks the result, and the human becomes better at judging the next run.

That is how AI makes people stronger instead of lazier.

Not by removing the need to think.

By making thinking visible enough to improve.

## Keep One Seat for the Person Who Slows the Room Down

There is a cheap way to be a critic: reject everything new and call it wisdom.

That is not what I mean.

Some technologies really do make life better. Some resistance is fear wearing old clothes. Some criticism becomes nostalgia for a world that was never as humane as remembered. We should not romanticize slowness, poverty, gatekeeping, or manual difficulty just because they came before the new tool.

The spoilsport also needs discipline.

A useful spoilsport does not only say no. They ask for the missing cost. They separate the tool from the business model. They distinguish temporary discomfort from structural harm. They admit when the new thing has created real freedom. They can say, "Use it, but do not surrender that part of yourself." They can say, "Build it, but test who becomes smaller when it scales."

That is the kind of person I want in the room.

And honestly, that is the kind of person I want to become when I use AI.

Not anti-technology. Not intoxicated by technology. A builder with a correction loop.

Every person using AI now has to install a small spoilsport inside their own practice. Before automating, ask what skill should be preserved. Before delegating, ask what judgment must stay yours. Before celebrating speed, ask whether the loop produces learning or only output. Before believing the future is inevitable, ask who benefits from making it sound that way.

Progress does not become weaker when it is questioned.

Only fragile propaganda needs silence.

Real progress can survive a hard question. Better than that, real progress uses the question to grow a spine.

So when the next tool arrives with a beautiful demo and a sentence about changing everything, I do not want to be the person who claps first.

I want to be the person who stays awake long enough to ask:

What does this make possible?

What does this make invisible?

And what kind of human will I become if I use it every day?
