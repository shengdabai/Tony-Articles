# High Standards Need a Tradeoff Clause

> 发布日期:2026-11-17 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-17-高标准的让步条款.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-17-standards-need-tradeoffs.md)

---

When working with AI, adding requirements can sometimes move the finished piece further from its purpose. Each request sounds reasonable on its own: make it more complete, more attractive, and quicker to produce. Together, however, they may compete for the same time or call for actions that work against each other.

I prefer to think of high standards as commitments with an order of priority: what must come first, and what may temporarily give way when requirements conflict. If I specify everything that must improve without saying what I am willing to forgo, the list can keep growing until the most important thing pays for the rest.

This deserves some thought. AI can make revisions easier. It cannot decide for me whom the work should serve first.

## Take Every Improvement That Fits

An AI-generated summary of a recorded discussion about using AI encouraged people to spend more attention on what to make, what they need to understand, and what standards to establish. I agree with that direction. Delegating operations gives us an opportunity to ask more of the result.

There is a strong case for continually raising standards. Having to choose between clear writing and attractive presentation may once have been a matter of insufficient time. AI can now help organize material, adjust layouts, and produce alternatives. What we could manage before may no longer be a suitable limit for what we can do today.

Nor does all the time saved have to become an earlier finish. It can go toward checking a source, considering a reader we might otherwise overlook, or trying an approach that previously seemed too troublesome. Some improvements reinforce each other: better structure can make reading faster, and removing repetition can make facts easier to check. Good. There is no need to make something worse just to demonstrate an appreciation of tradeoffs.

Asking AI whether it can satisfy several requirements together is therefore entirely reasonable. Treating the limits of old tools as permanent wisdom can cost us opportunities. For ordinary creators, being able to attempt work that was previously beyond our resources is an important reason to use AI.

Still, the ability to do more does not mean every requirement should keep increasing. Does a new requirement bring the work closer to its purpose, or merely make it look like a more elaborate production? Execution ability cannot settle that question.

Here is the process I worry about: accuracy, appearance, and timeliness are all declared mandatory, but no priority is given for a conflict. Easily visible requirements receive attention first, while losses that are harder to notice are discovered later. A creator sees a more polished page without immediately noticing that essential information has become harder to find. This is my inference about how work might unfold; it needs checking in actual use.

## Who Are the Shirt Photos For?

In a [2016 interview](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/paul-graham-startup-school-radio-interview/), Paul Graham recalled running Viaweb. The company made software for building online stores, but some merchants did not want to use it themselves, so the founders built stores for them. Graham processed a batch of shirt images and made the site, only to learn from the shirt merchant that what mattered was pictures of the collars. He had to redo the work.

This is a founder's retrospective account, not evidence from which to calculate a method's success rate. But the detail is concrete: he had worked carefully without first understanding what the images needed to show. An attractive image and an image that lets a customer see what they need to see can satisfy different requirements.

Another detail matters. Goals can change after contact with users. The importance of the collars became clear through a conversation. If every initial requirement were fixed in place, that later understanding would have nowhere to go. High standards need priorities, but they also need room for new information to change those priorities. We cannot allow revisions to the work while forbidding revisions to the requirements.

I am concerned with more than starting in the wrong direction. Even after something helps people make a decision, additional requirements can gradually weaken that usefulness. For the shirt photos, the first discussion should concern whether the collar is clearly visible; the next can concern whether decoration distracts from it. Change the order of requirements and the direction of revision changes too.

In his [2013 essay, “Do Things that Don't Scale,”](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html?viewfullsite=1) Graham also argued that attention to users can improve their overall experience while an early product remains incomplete. This is practical advice drawn from startup cases, not a finding validated for every business. Its subject is the user's whole experience, rather than whether the software itself is mature in every respect.

These two works share an author. They do not provide two independent bodies of corroborating evidence. Read together, however, they suggest a useful distinction: care sometimes means completing more of the product, and sometimes means acknowledging what the product cannot yet do and having a person handle a crucial step. Requiring every part to look equally mature may not be the most responsible arrangement.

That matters especially for a small team. Accepting a temporarily plain interface in exchange for prompt help can be an explicit service choice. If customers need a clear interface to complete the task at all, though, the interface cannot give way. The point of compromise should follow how the product is used, not which job its creator finds tiresome.

With several kinds of users, there is another complication. Some need to browse quickly; others need to compare carefully. Accepting every suggestion may not produce something everyone likes. I need to state whom this version primarily serves and leave some requests for other versions. Otherwise, every piece of feedback becomes an additional task, while the people originally promised a useful product find it increasingly difficult to use.

## Doing Well Can Require Opposite Actions

Learning tasks reveal the conflict more directly. Producing a correct answer is one outcome; mastering the method yourself is another. Both are worth pursuing, but submitted answers alone cannot tell us whether both happened.

[A mathematics experiment by Bastani and colleagues](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf) took place in a single Turkish high school and measured short-term practice performance followed by an exam taken without the tool. Students with a standard chat assistant scored 48% higher on practice than the control group, which had no AI assistance, but 17% lower on the subsequent exam after access was removed. A version with teaching safeguards largely eliminated that negative effect, but did not produce an observed exam advantage over the control group.

The safeguards involved more than asking AI to withhold answers. The researchers supplied teacher-designed correct solutions, common mistakes, and corresponding hints, while instructing the system to help students incrementally. The result belongs to that combined design; it cannot be attributed to one instruction alone. The study also used the models available at the time in a particular mathematics setting and did not measure long-term professional ability.

Reading this as a reason to do everything yourself would also be a mistake. [A separate randomized experiment by Contractor and Reyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849v2) studied undergraduates learning unfamiliar topics and writing essays. Students allowed to use AI had a higher estimated score on an immediate test whose rules barred AI and other resources. But the AI-allowed group had more test-rule violations, both observed by proctors and reported by students. The estimated advantage shrank when rule violators were excluded, so the immediate gap cannot all be treated as evidence of greater learning. The estimate remained positive a week later, although the statistical evidence was weaker. This is a preprint, a research paper shared before formal journal publication, and its short-term findings need further testing.

The tasks, students, and tool arrangements differed between these studies, so they cannot be used to declare one right and the other wrong. What they jointly prompt me to consider is that different kinds of effort serve different purposes. Saving time looking up an unfamiliar word may help someone keep learning. Skipping the reasoning they were meant to practice may produce a completed assignment without the intended understanding.

Which part to delegate depends on the goal. If the immediate need is simply a usable result, having AI do more can be appropriate. If the work also aims to develop your understanding, your own thinking needs a place in it, even if completion becomes slower. A brief that demands both the fastest finish and substantial practice, without saying which takes precedence in a conflict, makes consistent choices difficult for whoever carries it out.

Return to the shirt photos. A merchant's need for customers to see the collars does not require the merchant to master every image-processing operation. Cropping can be delegated while the merchant retains the ability to judge whether the information is sufficient. The actions worth keeping depend on the purpose. Both doing as much as possible yourself and delegating as much as possible leave out that judgment.

## Every Change Creates Follow-up Work

Another conflict is easier to miss: implementing a new requirement can be cheap, while establishing that it has not affected anything else can be expensive. AI finishes the revision quickly, and looking at it can make the job feel complete. But accepting a modification and accepting all its consequences are different actions.

In his [appendix on Shuttle reliability](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v2appf.htm), Richard Feynman discussed software verification specifically. He described checks that expanded from individual parts to the whole system, followed by testing by an independent team. His proposed way to save money was to reduce the number of requested changes while preserving the quality of verification for each. These were observations about a particular engineering system; its level of testing should not simply be transferred to ordinary writing.

I want to follow that question back to my own work: am I willing to pay for the follow-up checks a revision requires? Rewriting a factual passage calls for checking that its meaning has survived. Revising instructions calls for checking that later steps still connect. The cost of a requirement may come after its implementation.

Temporary compromise should also be distinguished from permanently lowering standards. Delivering a clear version today and adding decoration later leaves room for improvement. Presenting uncertain information as a definite conclusion has already changed the facts the reader receives. A narrower scope is negotiable; factual distortion is not something to trade for speed. That is a boundary I want to keep in public writing.

Suppose you are packing a suitcase. The requirements are easy to understand: bring what you need, keep the case light, make things accessible, and still close the lid. Adding another garment is easy, but it may make something used frequently harder to reach. The person packing first needs to know what the trip cannot do without, then decide what stays home.

AI resembles a more capable organizing tool: it can help fit in things that would not fit before. While there is space, nothing necessarily needs removing. But clothes occupy space, whereas requirements at work can also change behavior. Having AI supply an answer directly changes the learning process. Such conflicts are more complicated than the capacity of a suitcase; tighter packing alone will not resolve them.

That also means a tradeoff clause must not become an excuse for avoiding work. Additional time, a narrower delivery scope, or a different approach may satisfy all the crucial requirements and deserve serious consideration. What needs specifying is how to choose when requirements conflict under current conditions, rather than assuming that every improvement must cost us something else.

Sometimes the delivery arrangement is what most needs changing. Delivering in stages instead of all at once can make the current version more focused and leave time for later requirements. What must remain clear is which parts are commitments and which are possibilities to explore. Presenting a future possibility as a capability already delivered turns a scheduling choice into another ambiguity.

## Put the Tradeoff in the Next Brief

I want to try a small change. The next time I ask AI to revise something, I will hold off on a long string of adjectives and first state the main purpose, the conflict, and what may give way. This is a personal method proposed from the discussion above, not an experimentally validated universal process.

1. State what the user needs to do next. In the shirt-photo example, the customer needs to see collar details clearly. Naming that action makes it possible to judge whether a proposed revision serves it.
2. Identify what currently obstructs that action. Ask AI to explain which obstacle it intends to address before making the change. If no obstacle can be identified, leave the additional requirement as a candidate for now.
3. When requirements conflict, specify which shortcoming is temporarily acceptable. Collar details must remain clear; decorative effects can wait. If both can improve together, improve both. There is no need to manufacture a tradeoff.
4. Review the new version against its main purpose, and record whether the compromise actually helped. If it did not, restore the earlier arrangement. If an important new problem emerges, revise the priorities rather than treating the old list as permanent.

When I try this, I also intend to put the versions side by side. Looking only at the new one makes it easy to treat change itself as progress. Keeping the earlier version lets me identify what became more useful and what became worse. If all I can say is that the new version looks more sophisticated, without explaining how it helps its user, at least the reason for the revision remains unclear.

This does not require scoring every preference or turning a single image into a management system. It is meant to make explicit a decision often left vague: when several desirable things cannot all be achieved, which part am I willing to leave less good for now?

The check must address the conflict that actually occurred. If a plain layout was accepted to get essential information to readers sooner, check whether they can find and understand it. If completion slowed down to make room for learning, check whether you can explain the key judgments. A compromise that does not serve its intended purpose is not worth keeping. Even if it took effort.

The harder step, I think, is admitting that a requirement I added can be withdrawn. It is normal to recognize only after something has been made that a request was poorly considered or unimportant. Removing it does not make the work less conscientious. It gives the remaining care somewhere useful to go.

This method also needs a limit on its own cost. Changing an occasional word does not warrant reopening the entire discussion. Writing down the choice becomes worthwhile when important requirements conflict or the same rework keeps recurring. If the record costs more effort than the revision, shorten it. A process that consumes all our attention can also crowd out the work it was meant to support.

Before my next request to make something better, I intend to add a sentence: if the requirements conflict, which part am I willing to let give way temporarily for the thing that matters most? Once that is clear, I can press send.
