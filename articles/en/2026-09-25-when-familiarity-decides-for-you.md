# When Familiarity Decides for You

> Draft date: 2026-09-25 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-25-别让顺手替你决定.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-25-when-familiarity-decides-for-you.md)

---

Imagine you are about to reply to someone who disagrees with you. Your fingers are already over the keyboard, and your argument has formed: point out what they missed, then explain your position. You know how to do this. It may even have kept you from making hasty decisions before. But this time the other person may need something different from a debate. They may be trying to tell you a fact you have not yet noticed.

A note about “default scripts” offers a useful possibility: some reactions may persist because they once helped us. By a “default script,” I mean a response that starts almost on its own, such as explaining yourself as soon as someone disagrees. The note offers a proposal, not a verified research finding. I want to take the question one step further. The more familiar a method becomes, the easier it may be to confuse “this is what I know how to do” with “this is what this situation calls for.” Learning to notice that gap matters more than collecting another supposedly perfect technique.

Here is my view: a good habit does not deserve a permanent pass. It has to earn its place in the situation at hand. We do not need to reconsider every part of daily life. We do need to inspect our most comfortable method when the result keeps going wrong.

## How an old answer takes over a new problem

Suppose someone responds to every difficult task by gathering more information. That can serve them well when preparing for an exam or checking the details of a contract. Later they try to make a small creative work. They research, compare tools, and refine the plan. Every step looks responsible. Yet the rough version that another person could try never quite arrives.

The trouble is not that research has no value. The job has changed. An exam has a reasonably defined scope, so filling gaps in knowledge can directly prevent mistakes. Whether a new work solves someone else's problem is often something you cannot find out by continuing to research alone. The same action reduces risk in one setting and delays the feedback you most need in another. I call that pattern letting familiarity make the decision: automatically carrying an old answer into a new question. It is a name for a pattern in this essay, not a diagnosis of someone's character.

Why might even a capable person fall into it? My inference is that familiar actions are easy to start. You already know where to find material, how to outline, and how to explain your reasons clearly. Showing someone an unfinished piece and inviting criticism creates uncertainty. Of the two routes, the first may feel quicker and more dignified. But ease of starting shows only that you have taken that route often. It does not show that it still leads to the present goal.

Consider a more ordinary response: explaining yourself the moment you hear criticism. Perhaps quick explanations used to clear up frequent misunderstandings; they may have protected you. If the other person is pointing to a checkable error, an explanation may still be needed. If they are describing how something felt to them, a long explanation can prevent them from finishing. We cannot infer selfishness from “explains first,” and we need not demand permanent silence instead. The useful question is whether this conversation is about disputed facts or feelings I have not yet understood.

A familiar route saves effort because we do not consult a map at every corner. It can also hide a road closure or a changed destination. That analogy explains why a practiced response starts automatically. Conversations, however, are much more complex than roads; no navigation app can calculate the effect of a sentence for us. I care less about declaring habits good or bad than about noticing signs that the route has changed.

## Why familiarity is incomplete evidence

We usually judge a method by what happened before. But that history can be short and lopsided. We remember when the method got us out of trouble, not necessarily when it cost us a chance to connect. We see a loss that happened, but not always an opportunity postponed. This is my inference about everyday decisions. Someone's preference for analysis alone is not evidence that they are avoiding action.

A useful piece of evidence comes from [Hertwig and colleagues' 2004 experiments on risky choice](https://drupalgsb-dev.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1329/Weber_RareEvents.pdf). Some participants received descriptions of options and their probabilities; others learned about equivalent choices by sampling outcomes. In the experience group, the median number of draws per problem was 15: sort the participants' draw counts, and 15 was the middle value. In those tasks, rare outcomes often had less influence on choices made from experience. The authors examined small samples and the weight of recent outcomes as possible explanations. These were designed risk problems, not arguments with friends or decisions about a business. They cannot prove that a particular habit will mislead you.

What the study suggests to me is modest but useful. Personal experience matters, yet “I have always done this and nothing bad happened” may miss an outcome that is uncommon but costly. The reverse is also true: reading one alarming warning does not show that the danger is common. When I decide whether to reuse an old response, I should look at what I have encountered while admitting that my sample may never have included the important case.

There may be a quieter trap too. A method can leave signs that look like proof of its own success. You explain a disagreement and the other person stops arguing. Perhaps they agree; perhaps they have simply given up on the conversation. You polish a plan and the plan becomes impressively detailed. That may feel like progress, while the original problem remains untouched. “They fell silent, so I persuaded them” and “I was busy, so I advanced” are interpretations that have not been checked. Such interpretations can be tempting, which is why I want to decide in advance what would count as help and what would merely provide reassurance.

And we only see what followed the action we actually took. We do not see what might have happened if we had opened differently. Silence after an explanation could mean the explanation worked; it could also mean nobody wants to keep spending effort. We need not turn a relationship into a laboratory. In a low cost situation, we can try a different opening and ask which one made the issue clearer. That offers a local clue, not a law about everybody.

## The strongest objection: we cannot recalculate everything

There is a serious objection here. If we examine every reply, every plan, and every practice session, we will never get through the day. Speed and steadiness are hard won rewards of learning. Constantly second guessing ourselves can slow down work we already know how to do and consume attention needed elsewhere.

I agree. Imagine stopping at every intersection on a familiar trip to buy groceries just to inspect a map. That would be exhausting. A default response often deserves to stay when the environment is stable, errors are easy to notice, and the consequences are manageable. I am arguing for a trigger to review it: the same problem keeps returning; an important result never arrives; or the person, goal, or constraint has plainly changed. Take the familiar route on ordinary days. Stop when one of those signals appears.

The direction of a possible mistake matters too. If you try a new way to write a private draft and it fails, you can delete it. Publishing an unverified claim may hurt someone; you cannot justify doing that merely to get feedback. Conversely, withholding a harmless draft from a trusted reader indefinitely also has a cost. The right moment to pause is often where the cost of a mistake, the cost of waiting, and the ability to undo the action have changed. Those costs depend on the situation. A slogan about taking risks cannot replace that judgment.

This boundary also keeps improvement from turning into self blame. An old method probably survived because it did something useful at some point. Updating it does not erase that service. It is more honest than declaring, “I will never do that again.” First identify the problem it once solved. Then ask whether this is still that problem.

[Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer's 1993 paper](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf) separates ordinary work from deliberate practice. Deliberate practice means working on a specific weakness to improve performance, then using feedback to correct it; ordinary work often rewards reliable delivery. The paper concerns skill and expert performance; it does not show that every habit in everyday life can be changed through a training routine. Still, it is a good reminder that traveling an old route repeatedly will not automatically tell you when to turn.

## Give one familiar response a situation test

Here is a small exercise I propose for a low risk problem. It is an idea to try, not a training method verified by the studies above. Pick a situation that has recently repeated without a satisfying result. Perhaps every time you mean to start writing, you reorganize your research instead. Write three short sentences: what triggered you, what you did immediately, and what result you wanted. Resist writing, “I am just a procrastinator.” That turns an adjustable action into a claim about your identity that is hard to test.

Now mark where the old action does help. If you lack essential material and are likely to get facts wrong, checking sources first makes sense. If you already have enough material but do not know whether a reader understands it, writing a small section and asking someone to read it may teach you more. Treat that as an alternative to try, not a new universal rule. When a similar situation comes up, test it while keeping the old approach available for comparison. Record the concrete outcome of each. If the new action creates a bigger problem, do not insist on it just to prove you are “growing.”

Keep the alternative small. If you want to understand criticism, you do not need to become a person who never defends themselves. Before your first explanation, repeat the specific concern you think you heard and ask whether you got it right. If it turns out that a fact was misunderstood, you can still explain it. This changes the order of actions while preserving your ability to clarify. Changing the order is often easier to evaluate than announcing a grand new personality.

Write the standard of success beforehand. If your goal is to make an explanation clear, see whether the reader can restate its point and identify where they got lost. If your goal is to reduce misunderstanding, ask whether you accurately repeated the other person's meaning. Comfort, busyness, and praise may be worth recording, but none should stand in for the actual goal. For decisions involving money, health, safety, or another person's rights, this informal experiment is no substitute for appropriate expertise and checks.

This small exercise is my proposal to try. The two startup sources below offer ways to think about it; neither verifies its effect. [Paul Graham's 2013 advice to founders](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) urges them to spend time directly with early users because their initial model of those users is usually inaccurate. It is advice from startup cases, not a controlled test for every person. In [Amazon's published 1997 shareholder letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazons-original-1997-letter-to-shareholders), the company says it would measure the effectiveness of its investments, drop programs with inadequate returns, and invest more in those that worked. That is a company's account of its own approach; it cannot establish that any single change caused its growth. What I take from both is a willingness to replace “I think this works” with “I am willing to look at the response,” not an instruction to copy a particular company.

## AI can expose the pattern, but it cannot choose your goal

My inference is that AI might make an old script harder to notice, or help someone inspect it. Suppose a person's default is to gather more information. AI could arrange that information neatly, and the person might take longer to realize they need an actual reader. If the default is to explain immediately, AI could compose a smoother explanation without knowing whether this moment calls for fact checking or listening. This is a judgment about using the tool, not an AI effect established by the studies above.

A more useful request may be: separate the action I am about to take, the result I want, and the situation I might have missed; then suggest an alternative I could test safely. AI can help arrange the possibilities. The result still has to be checked in the world.

I want to give the tool another boundary. Instead of asking only for “a better way to say it,” ask what real world information would make that sentence justified. If it suggests “listen first,” I can ask how I would know I understood: could I restate the concern accurately, or did I merely avoid interrupting? If it says “act first,” I can ask which step can be undone and which might impose on someone else. These questions do not make AI a judge. They keep fluent advice from masquerading as a verified decision.

If the input describes only the virtues of the old method, AI may give reasons centered on those virtues. That is a concern to test, so the input should also include when the method failed to help.

[Butler's 2010 experiments on retrieval practice](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf) offer another reminder. After reading passages, participants who repeatedly tried to answer questions from memory and received feedback later did better on new inference questions than participants assigned to repeated study. In those experiments with prose and designed questions, learning involved more than rereading. The finding does not guarantee that a conversational or creative method will transfer to a different setting. We still have to try it there and notice when to keep the familiar response and when to choose another.

Return to the disagreement at the start. With my fingers over the keyboard, I may not need an entirely new philosophy of communication. I can first ask one concrete question: is this person presenting a checkable fact, or feelings I have not yet understood? If it is a fact, we can check it. If they are describing feelings, I can restate what I heard before I explain. The old method is still there. It simply no longer gets to decide for every situation. To me, recognizing when it deserves the job looks more like growth than promising never to use it again.
