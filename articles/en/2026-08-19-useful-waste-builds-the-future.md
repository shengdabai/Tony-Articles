# Useful Waste Builds The Future

> 发布日期:2026-08-19 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-19-未来常常建在有用的浪费之上.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-19-useful-waste-builds-the-future.md)

---

One of the hardest things to judge in a technological wave is whether something is waste or preparation.

At the surface, the two can look almost identical.

A company lays too much fiber before enough people are online. A factory buys electric motors but keeps the old layout. A team adds AI tools before it knows how work should change. A person builds a note system, a writing habit, a few awkward automations, and a private workflow before any obvious return appears.

From the outside, all of this can look inefficient.

Sometimes it is.

But sometimes the thing that looks wasteful is not waste at all. It is unused capacity waiting for the world around it to catch up. It is an option. It is a road that seems empty because the city has not moved there yet.

The difference matters now, because AI is full of apparently excessive investment: data centers, chips, power, fiber, internal tools, agents, workflows, training, documentation, evaluation systems. Some of it will be burned capital. Some of it will become the ground on which the next economy quietly stands.

The question is not whether there is waste.

There is always waste.

The better question is: what kind of waste are we creating?

## There Are Two Kinds Of Waste

Bad waste disappears when the excitement ends.

It cannot be reused. It cannot be connected to anything else. It requires constant subsidies to stay alive. It has no learning loop. It creates no reusable surface area. When the story cools, all that remains is cost.

Useful waste is different.

It may be early. It may be underused. It may even be financially painful for the first owner. But it leaves behind capacity that other people can later use at lower cost. It creates standards, paths, habits, skills, tools, interfaces, and infrastructure. It changes what future builders can assume.

That is why technological history is hard to read in real time. The market punishes overinvestment quickly, but society may need years to discover what the overinvestment made possible.

The first owner of the road may lose money.

The next generation may build a city beside it.

## The Pattern Is Older Than AI

In the railway boom of the nineteenth century, capital rushed ahead of reliable demand. The result was brutal. A famous banking failure tied to railroad finance triggered the Panic of 1873 in the United States. PBS summarizes the background plainly: between 1866 and 1873, 35,000 miles of new track were laid, railroads became the nation's largest non-agricultural employer, and the collapse of a major railroad financier set off a wider panic.[^1]

That crash was not a romantic story. Businesses failed. Railroads went bankrupt. Workers were hurt. Real people paid the price.

And yet the tracks did not vanish.

Once built, infrastructure keeps asking a second question: who can use me next?

The same pattern appeared with electricity. The first generation of electrification did not instantly transform productivity. A classic paper on the "dynamo and the computer" argues that the productivity impact of a general-purpose technology can be delayed because organizations need time to redesign themselves around the new capability.[^2]

Early factories often treated electric power as a replacement for steam power inside the same old arrangement. They changed the energy source, but not the system. The real gains came later, when factories were reorganized around distributed motors, new layouts, better flow, and eventually moving assembly lines. The Library of Congress notes that one carmaker's moving assembly line reduced assembly time from 12.5 hours to 93 minutes.[^3]

The motor alone was not the revolution.

The redesigned factory was.

That is a crucial distinction for AI. A model inserted into the old workflow may feel like progress, but it is often only a faster engine attached to the same old transmission. The deeper change arrives when the work itself is rearranged.

## Dark Fiber Is A Better Metaphor Than Gold

The dot-com era gives us a more recent image: dark fiber.

During the late 1990s, telecom companies built aggressively for an internet future that arrived more slowly than their financing models required. After the crash, much of the newly laid fiber sat unused. Forbes cites estimates that 85% to 95% of fiber laid in the 1990s remained unused after the bubble burst.[^4] Whether any single estimate is perfect matters less than the pattern: demand did not meet the timetable, companies failed, but the physical capacity remained.

Then, years later, the world found uses for that capacity.

Search, video, cloud computing, mobile internet, and streaming did not have to wait for every trench to be dug from scratch. The empty glass in the ground lowered the cost of the next wave.

Now the metaphor has become almost literal again. In July 2026, Verizon's official investor page listed its second-quarter earnings call materials, and Reuters reported that Verizon had secured a deal with Google worth more than $1 billion to provide dark fiber connectivity for Google's data centers.[^5] A piece of infrastructure once associated with overbuilding is now part of the AI data-center conversation.

That does not prove that today's AI spending is automatically wise.

It proves something narrower and more useful: infrastructure can be mispriced by one era and essential to another.

Timing can destroy investors while still helping the future.

That sentence is uncomfortable, but it is often true.

## Do Not Worship The Bubble

There is a lazy version of this argument, and we should reject it.

The lazy version says: "Bubbles build the future, so bubbles are good."

No.

Bubbles also destroy savings, distort incentives, reward storytelling over substance, and pull talent toward games that may not deserve it. Many assets produced in a frenzy do not become useful infrastructure. They become abandoned dashboards, obsolete hardware, empty communities, half-built products, and tools nobody maintains.

Useful waste has conditions.

It must be transferable. Other people must be able to use it after the original story changes.

It must be maintainable. If it requires heroic effort just to keep it alive, it is not infrastructure; it is a liability with a logo.

It must sit near real demand. An empty road in the wrong place is still empty.

It must teach. Even if the first business fails, the attempt should leave better methods, clearer standards, trained people, reusable code, or sharper judgment.

Most important, it must lower the cost of future action.

If today's excess does not make tomorrow's work cheaper, faster, safer, or more imaginable, it is probably just excess.

## The Personal Version Of This Problem

This is not only a story about railways, electricity, telecom, and AI companies.

It is also a personal problem.

A person who wants to grow will also create unused capacity. You read books before you know exactly how they will pay off. You learn a tool before a client asks for it. You build a writing archive before the audience is obvious. You make templates, prompts, checklists, tests, and small automations before the workflow fully proves itself.

From a short-term accounting view, much of that looks wasteful.

But growth has its own infrastructure.

A note you can find again is not just a note. It is a future input.

A public essay is not just an essay. It is a test of whether your thinking can survive outside your head.

A repeatable workflow is not just convenience. It is a way to stop spending attention on the same problem twice.

A small automation is not just saved time. It is a claim that this piece of work deserves to be turned into a system.

The danger is that AI makes the bad version easier. It can generate more notes, more drafts, more summaries, more plans, more images, more prototypes, and more unfinished things. That looks like abundance, but it may only be a larger pile.

The stronger use of AI is different.

Use AI to build roads, not piles.

Make it help you preserve context, compare versions, test assumptions, find contradictions, turn repeated work into procedures, and make your judgment easier to inspect. Let it increase your ability to act, not your ability to avoid acting.

This is where the idea of becoming stronger matters. AI should not be a pillow for the mind. It should be a lever, a mirror, a workshop, and sometimes a strict reviewer.

## Build Capacity That Can Meet The Future

Long-termism is often misunderstood as patience.

Patience is part of it, but not the core.

The core is building capacity before the result is obvious, while being honest about whether that capacity can compound.

You do not need to predict the future perfectly. Almost nobody can. But you can ask better questions about the capacity you are building:

Can this be reused?

Can this be connected to other work?

Can someone else understand it later?

Can it survive a change of tools?

Does it leave a trace of decisions?

Does it make the next attempt cheaper?

Does it make me more capable, or merely more comfortable?

These questions are small, but they separate useful waste from vanity waste.

The future is not built by efficiency alone. If everyone only builds what is immediately justified, no one builds the empty road, the spare bandwidth, the practice room, the testing ground, or the archive that becomes valuable later.

But the future is also not built by chaos. Excess becomes useful only when it can be organized, maintained, connected, and handed forward.

So maybe the right posture is neither hype nor cynicism.

When a new wave arrives, do not ask only: "Is this a bubble?"

Ask: "What will remain after the bubble?"

Ask: "Will that remainder be usable?"

Ask: "Who becomes stronger because this capacity exists?"

That is the real test.

The best kind of preparation often looks premature. The best infrastructure often looks underused. The best learning often looks inefficient before it compounds.

But if it creates capacity that can meet a future demand, it was never merely waste.

It was the future arriving early, before we had learned how to use it.
