# Care Should Survive an Absence

> 发布日期:2026-10-05 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-05-真正的照顾，应该经得起一次缺席.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-05-care-should-survive-an-absence.md)

---

Imagine booking an appointment at the barbershop you usually visit. The barber who knows you is taking the day off. The person at the desk cannot find what was agreed when you booked, or why you changed your haircut last time. You have to explain everything again. The problem is not that your barber took a day off. The problem is that the shop's understanding of you vanished when he left.

Today I read a saved summary of someone else's account of a small service business. It praised an owner for remembering customers and looking out for them, while also describing the owner's worry that taking a break would leave regulars stranded. This was a secondary summary, not my experience or a verified study of that shop. But it raised a question I had not followed far enough: if one person must always be present for customers to feel cared for, how sturdy is that care? My view is that a business should preserve more than one person's warmth. It should also be able to respond properly when that person is away.

## Do customers need constant availability, or a clear next step?

In the imagined appointment, a customer might choose to wait for the barber they trust. Some services really do depend on a particular person's craft and relationship with the customer. A replacement is not always the right answer. But waiting by choice is different from being left in the dark. If the shop tells you when that person will return, whether your earlier agreement still stands, and whom to contact in the meantime, you can decide what to do. Without that information, the more devoted the owner is, the harder it may feel to let them rest. Every answer seems to live in one person's head.

I am not asking a small shop to turn human relationships into scripted lines. I mean something simpler. When the familiar person is away, whoever takes over should know what has already been promised, which questions require the customer's input, and which decisions they have no authority to make. The customer should not have to repeat known background or suddenly receive an arrangement that contradicts the earlier one. A day off can then remain a day off rather than becoming a disappearance. Trust no longer depends entirely on one person's stamina.

A handoff is a little like a relay race. The fastest runner does not have to pack all their skill into the baton. The next runner needs to know where things stand, where to go, and when to stop and check. The analogy explains how information can move between people. It stops there: customers are not fixed tracks, their needs change, and holding a record does not mean understanding a person. A useful handoff must let the customer correct it. An old note cannot be the final verdict on a new situation.

There is a distinction I find easy to miss. Remembering someone and making sure they are cared for are connected, but they are not identical. The first can live in an owner's memory. The second also requires essential context, existing commitments, and known uncertainties to be visible to whoever acts next. That does not mean collecting every detail about a customer. Knowing what should not be retained is part of caring too.

## Do the work yourself before deciding what to hand over

None of this means a new business should begin by designing an elaborate process. In [Paul Graham's account of early startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), founders recruit users personally, operate their products on users' behalf, and sometimes perform by hand the work they hope to automate later. These are cases he observed, not a controlled experiment showing that every small business should follow the same path. What I take from them is the order of operations: touch the problem yourself before deciding which part can be handed over.

If you do not yet know why a customer came, where they got stuck, or what a good outcome would be, a handoff form can freeze your guesses in place. It looks orderly while helping everyone make the same mistake faster. The value of serving someone yourself is that you encounter things the form does not yet capture. A request for a feature might really mean the customer wants to avoid explaining something again. A customer saying there is no rush might still be waiting for a definite reply. Those are possibilities, not conclusions we can draw from a sentence. Questions and later behavior have to test them.

[Superhuman founder Rahul Vohra's account of his own company](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/) describes a checkable process. His team asked users how they would feel if they lost the product, what benefit they actually received, and what should improve. The team then distinguished feedback connected to the product's central benefit from feedback that was not. This is one founder's account of one company. Its survey threshold is no universal law. The useful idea for me is a sequence: hear how different people describe value, then decide which observations should inform the team's shared judgment.

That is why handing work over too early and too late can both hurt. Too early, and you pass along untested assumptions. Too late, and every customer relationship, exception, and repair depends on one person. A reasonable time to start might be after you have personally handled a recurring kind of request. You can then explain what the customer is trying to accomplish and where misunderstandings tend to occur, while remaining open to a new case that overturns the old rule. “Might” matters here. This is my practical judgment, not a universal timetable established by either account.

## Pass along clues for judgment, not a personality profile

Suppose someone wants to change an appointment. The next person may need to know what conflict the customer is trying to avoid, what was already confirmed, which alternatives are available, and which choice the customer must approve. That person does not need the customer's entire private life. Nor should a single past choice become a permanent label. If a record presents a guess as a fact, careful execution can make the resulting harm more consistent.

I would keep a useful handoff to a few kinds of information: the goal the customer stated, the commitment already made, what remains uncertain, and who must confirm the next step. Each entry should be open to correction and tied to the circumstances in which it was recorded. “This customer hates rescheduling” claims to define a person. “Confirm the available times before changing this appointment” describes an action required in this case. The first invites overconfidence. The second gives the next person a workable clue.

AI can help, but it cannot take responsibility away from the person using it. [A study by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond](https://danielle.li/assets/docs/GenerativeAIatWork.pdf) examined 5,172 customer-support agents at one company. Under the deployment conditions studied, AI suggestions raised issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. Less experienced workers gained more, while the highest-skilled group showed small declines on some quality measures. This was text-based customer support in a particular firm and period. Its numbers cannot simply be moved to a barbershop, a consulting service, or today's models.

The lesson I draw is not that uploading notes to AI will automatically improve service. We need to ask where the suggestion comes from, who checks it, and what happens when the request falls outside the available examples. In the study, suggestions appeared to the agents, who could choose whether to use them; the system could also give no suggestion when it lacked enough training data for a topic. If we compress an old exchange into “the customer likes this” and omit the circumstances, both a colleague and an AI assistant may mistake an exception for a rule. The faster the automation, the more clearly the handoff must say “we do not know.”

That leaves three useful states for a record to express: confirmed and ready to act on; supported but still requiring confirmation; and unknown, requiring a fresh question. It need not be long or full of warm adjectives. If the customer changes their mind today, the record should help someone notice the change rather than prevent it. Its purpose is to support the next sound decision, not to display how attentive its author once was.

## The strongest objection: can a relationship fit in a form?

The best objection I can see is that some care consists precisely of judgment that cannot be written down. A familiar barber may sense that today is a day to speak less. Someone may return to a shop because they do not want to be identified, grouped, and predicted by a system. If every conversation becomes data collection, service can start to feel like an interrogation. Maintaining records also takes time a small team may not have. If “easy to hand over” means customers must leave a trail everywhere they go, I would oppose it too.

So the handoff needs limits. Keep only what is necessary to honor a commitment or spare the customer from repeating relevant information. Ask about a preference directly when you can, instead of inferring it in secret. Do not save a preference the customer does not want recorded merely because it makes operations easier. A shop's ability to remember the next step does not grant it permission to know everything about a person. I want commitments to survive an absence, not people to be turned into dossiers. For work that depends heavily on one person's craft, scheduling the next appointment with that same person may be the best handoff.

There is another serious objection. Even an accurate record does not teach someone to handle a new case. In [Ericsson and Harwell's analysis of research on practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), improvement-oriented practice has a clear target, feedback on a person's specific errors, and another chance to try. Simply doing the job repeatedly is not the same thing. Their article analyzes definitions and evidence about practice; it is not an experiment on service handoffs. Applied here, it gives me a testable question: after the handoff, can the next person spot when the old approach no longer fits, and does specific feedback change what they do next time?

[Learning experiments by Butler and colleagues](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c8baca1e5f7d136349ea789/t/5e739a3d46338e7dec37aaf6/1584634429337/Butler%2Bet%2Bal%2B2017.pdf) add another piece. Participants learned geology concepts and answered either repeated or varied application questions. Across the experiments, those who practiced on varied examples performed better on new questions two days later. These were short-term learning experiments, not field tests in service businesses. They do not show that a few rehearsed handoffs guarantee good service. They do support a design choice: do not rehearse only the smooth, identical case. Try a changed request, an ambiguous note, or an absent owner. Such variations expose judgments the next person has not yet learned to make.

## Give your service one absence it can afford

If you build a product, provide a service, or maintain a long-running piece of work for a team, I suggest a small experiment. Pick a recurring request with low stakes. Avoid health, money, and sensitive identity matters. Remove identifying details from the real case. Keep only the customer's goal, the commitment already made, the open questions, and the points where the next person must stop and seek approval. Then ask someone who does not normally handle the request—or an AI assistant whose answer you will check—to explain what they would do next using only that material.

Do not start by asking whether they followed the note. Ask whether they can distinguish actionable facts from guesses. Ask how they would update the record if the customer changed their mind, and what clear reply the customer would get while the original owner is away. Then try a varied, hypothetical version of the request. Perhaps the customer accepts a new time but declines a substitute service. If the second answer simply repeats the first, you may have passed along an answer without the conditions under which it was sound.

Afterward, change just the part most likely to cause misunderstanding. Perhaps a promise needs a time attached. Perhaps a preference needs to say it applied only to the previous visit. Perhaps no one knows when to stop and ask the customer. Put the revised note back into ordinary work and watch whether the next customer still has to start from the beginning. One simulation cannot prove the service is dependable. It can expose a point of failure that used to be hidden in one person's head while the cost of finding it is still small.

Speed is not the only measure. I care more about three things: whether the original commitment was honored, whether the next person admitted uncertainty, and whether the customer could still change their choice. Skipping confirmation to reply faster is not progress. A record that makes it harder for the customer to correct us has not carried care forward. A successful test may look ordinary: the customer knows the familiar person is away and knows who is now handling the matter.

Return to the imagined barbershop. Of course the barber can take a day off. What would reassure me is a shop that can say, honestly: we remember what was agreed; we will ask before making a choice that belongs to you; and we will give you a clear plan for anything we cannot do today. I see that capacity as a lasting asset for a small team. It lets people show care without proving it by being online forever. An absence becomes a useful test of whether the service can stand on its own.
