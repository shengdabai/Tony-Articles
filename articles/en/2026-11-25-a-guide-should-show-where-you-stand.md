# A Guide Should Show You Which Step You’ve Reached

> 发布日期:2026-11-25 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-25-%E4%B8%80%E4%BB%BD%E8%AF%B4%E6%98%8E%E8%A6%81%E5%86%99%E6%B8%85%E5%8A%9E%E5%88%B0%E5%93%AA%E4%B8%80%E6%AD%A5.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-25-a-guide-should-show-where-you-stand.md)

---

You’ve shortened a set of instructions, maybe with some help from AI. Your reader follows them, calls to request an extension, and is told they can have a few more days. They hang up and still have to come back to you. Does the original deadline still apply? The way I see it, if you keep whom to ask but cut the condition that determines whether the deadline can change, the reader may have to ask again and you may have to explain it anyway. [Scenario adapted from a contract-reading question](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

I want a more concrete test for a draft. Put the reply or receipt the reader already has next to the instructions. Ask them to tell you what it confirms, who still needs to do what, and which sentence supports their judgment. A receipt is a message or slip from the recipient showing that something arrived. In this example it only confirms that the materials came in. If I have to say aloud that approval is still pending before the reader realizes it, that sentence belongs in the guide. [The contract question whose format I borrowed](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

## Did the phone call change the deadline?

Joseph Kimble included a contract-reading question in an article in The Scribes Journal of Legal Writing. The question puts you in the contractor’s shoes. Circumstances beyond your control mean you’ll miss the original delivery date. You speak with a department official by phone, and the official tells you that you can have ten extra days. If you rely on that answer and deliver ten days late, can the department still treat the work as overdue? It’s a hypothetical, and the contract supplies the answer. [The original clause, rewrite, and sample question](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

Under this contract, if the contractor hasn’t received the information they need from the department or faces other circumstances that could justify a delay, they may ask for an extension in writing. The department may grant it in writing. The phone call leaves that written approval outstanding. If you deliver late on the strength of the call alone, the department can still choose either option in the contract. It can end your services and withhold payment for work delivered late, or it can deduct an amount from your payment as the contract specifies. The question asks what the department can choose. You can answer it without guessing what it would actually do. [The sample contract’s extension clause and late-delivery options](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

Think of an expense claim. Suppose a company’s finance team pays only after someone with approval authority signs the form. A colleague hears you out and says sure, and the signature box on your form is still empty. The phone call in the sample contract leaves you in much the same position. However certain the official sounds, the contractor still needs the department to give its approval in the form the contract requires before planning around the new date. The signature belongs to the expense example I made up. The contract specifies written approval from the department. [Who can approve an extension under the sample contract and in what form](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

Kimble had readers consult either the original or the rewritten contract as they answered. These figures come from 27 state-agency employees, most of whom had never used the contract. The groups with the original and the rewrite got 53.6% and 78% correct overall, respectively. They took an average of 14.8 and 12.4 minutes to answer all 14 questions. The rewrite changed wording and organization together, and the report gives no separate score for the phone question. These figures let us compare performance on the full test. To tell which edit helped, we’d need other evidence. [Contract test methods and results for state-agency staff](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

I want to borrow the way the question is asked. Ask whether the instructions are clear, and when the reader nods, I’m tempted to nod along. Give them a reply over the phone, ask which day they’d deliver, and have them point to their reason. That gives me a chance to see which line in the draft they took as settling the matter. A question like that is what I propose using to check a draft. [The contract-reading question and test instructions](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

## They found the section. Can they act on it?

When someone tries the draft, I suggest keeping separate notes on where they get stuck. If the extension clause takes a while to find, look at the heading and where the clause sits. If the reader reaches the page and takes a verbal reply as formal approval, check whether the text spells out who can approve the request and what kind of reply counts. Kimble’s rewrite gives the due date and the consequences of missing it separate headings, with the extension conditions under the due date. If a reader reaches the consequences and misses the extension conditions, you have a specific place to work on. [The order of the clauses in the rewritten contract](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

Time saved in the contents can be spent again once you reach the text. Hewlett-Packard funded the research in the late 1980s. Janice C. Redish and Joseph S. Dumas reported that the study tested five printed manuals for the same debugging program in a lab. A debugger is a tool programmers use to track down what’s going wrong in a program. Ten programmers with relevant experience used each version, and they all attempted the same tasks. Versions four and five differed by one set of features designed to help people find instructions. The fourth version’s contents were organized around what readers wanted to do. [The manual study’s timing, materials, and test design](https://redish.net/wp-content/uploads/Redish_Dumas_umetric_paper.pdf)

Readers with version four spent an average of 4 minutes 14 seconds in the contents. Those with version five spent 6 minutes 6 seconds. For the whole test, though, the averages were 1 hour 37 minutes 3 seconds for the fourth version and 1 hour 27 minutes 6 seconds for the fifth. In the logs and recordings, the researchers saw readers find a heading quickly, get to the text, wonder whether they’d found the right section, and return to the contents. The researchers offered this back-and-forth as one possible explanation. The comparison was between printed manuals for a single product, and the fourth version bundled several changes to help people find things. [Times and observations for versions four and five](https://redish.net/wp-content/uploads/Redish_Dumas_umetric_paper.pdf)

In 2013 Robert F. Lorch Jr., Julie Lemarié, and Hung-Tao Chen reported two experiments. In the first, 134 college students read explanatory texts about energy with either headings or opening sentences that conveyed the same topics and how they fit together. An opening sentence like this appears at the start of a section and might tell readers that air pollution is one type of environmental damage the text will discuss. Students with headings remembered more subtopics. The researchers found no reliable difference between the groups in how well they remembered specific facts. The data couldn’t establish a difference, and whether the groups performed equally well remains open. [The energy texts and the recall experiment](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/archivos/ed2013v19n2a2.pdf)

The second experiment asked 120 college students to outline a text. They included more topics from the text with headings or with opening sentences than they did with neither cue. When the researchers compared the two cues, the test couldn’t establish a difference in the number of topics included. Whether the two cues would yield the same result remains open. The researchers suggested that which cue helps more might depend on what the reader is trying to do. The headings also stood out more visually in these experiments, and the researchers said the role of the reading task still needed a direct test. [The outlining experiment, scoring, and researchers’ discussion](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/archivos/ed2013v19n2a2.pdf)

When a reader lands on the right page, I’m tempted to put a check in my notes. In the manual study, readers found the heading and still went back to the contents. The energy-text experiments measured recall and outlining. Deciding what you can do with a reply you already have is a further step. My suggestion is to give that step its own line in the test notes. Once the reader has found the right paragraph, record whether they can use it to tell if the matter is settled or if someone still needs to act. [Observations from the manual study](https://redish.net/wp-content/uploads/Redish_Dumas_umetric_paper.pdf) · [The recall and outlining tasks](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/archivos/ed2013v19n2a2.pdf)

## Which sentence should go?

Put every exception at the top and the delivery date can get buried. Put the date alone on the first line and there’s another catch. A reader can spot the date, move on, and assume it applies to everyone. My preference is to give the usual date first, put any condition that affects the immediate decision or a pointer to it right beside the date, and follow with the details for an extension. Someone checking the date can see straight away that there are exceptions. Someone applying for more time or looking at a reply then has a place to check how far they’ve got. [An example of how the due date and extension conditions are organized](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

In 1997, John Morkes and Jakob Nielsen had 51 experienced web users try five versions of the same small travel site built for the study. One task asked users to choose the best attraction to visit and explain why. The other two asked them to look up specific facts. The easy-to-scan version used features such as headings and lists to help readers spot what they needed quickly. The concise version cut secondary material and kept the information needed for the tasks in the original order. Total time for the three tasks averaged 209 seconds with the concise version, 229 seconds with the easy-to-scan version, and 359 seconds with the promotional version. [Web writing study, experiment three and Table 1](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)

My suggestion is to try cutting repeated setup first and move background the reader has no use for at that step. When you get to a condition that governs what the reader can do, put the before and after sentences side by side. Shorten *the department may approve an extension in writing* to *the department may approve an extension*, and the department is still there. The words specifying the form of approval have vanished. In the sample contract those words determine which approvals count, so keep them in the same sentence as the act of approving. [The sample contract’s requirement for the form of approval](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

There’s a further concern. Could making a precise clause easier to read make it less precise? In a study published in 2023, Eric Martínez, Francis Mollica, and Edward Gibson compared pairs of excerpts reflecting common US contract clauses, with each pair designed to express the same meaning. The 105 lawyers in the first analysis understood and recalled the plainer versions better than the more convoluted versions. The second experiment reported ratings from 102 lawyers and found no reliable difference in enforceability ratings. Here, enforceability means the lawyers’ judgment about whether the clause could be enforced under the law, for example whether one party could legally require the other to honor a promise in a dispute. The data couldn’t establish a difference in the ratings, and whether the two versions would be rated equally remains open. The researchers recorded how lawyers rated the excerpts. Actual disputes were outside the scope of the experiment. [Contract excerpt comprehension, recall, and rating experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064/)

What I want to borrow is the requirement that both versions mean the same thing. Change *the department may approve an extension in writing* to *the department will approve it in writing as soon as it receives your request*, and you’ve turned the department’s power to decide into a promise to approve. Change *request received* to *extension approved*, and the reader may think they can already plan around a new date. After editing, put the same receipt next to both versions and check who can make the decision, what the receipt actually confirms, and which step changes the original deadline. Even if both versions mention the new date, check what each says the reader must receive before using it. [How the researchers designed the excerpts to mean the same thing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064/) · [The sample contract’s request and approval provisions](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

Suppose you’re writing instructions for submitting materials to an event. The official document only says that if you’re having trouble, you may contact the organizers to discuss your options. Someone has a reply that says their request was received and wants to know whether they still have to submit by Friday. The reply establishes that the organizers got the request. The event document leaves open what kind of reply changes the deadline. The sample contract calls for written approval from the department. If the event has such a step, its own rules need to establish it. I suggest turning the gap into a question the person who sets the rule can answer. Does Friday’s deadline still apply after a reply like this? If it can change, who tells the applicant, and how? Get the answer before adding it to the guide. [The sample contract’s written approval clause, for comparison](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)

## Try it on one instruction

Pick one instruction about a request, a confirmation, or a deadline and find the original material it comes from. If you can, ask someone like the intended reader who hasn’t heard your explanation. These four steps are my proposal for checking a draft. Start with one instruction and keep the same material on hand so you can ask the same question after the edit.

1. Put the original material next to the draft. Write down what the reader already has, who needs to act next, and what kind of reply lets them go ahead. If they have a submission receipt, find what the original says it confirms, then check for wording that says it also counts as approval. Mark the words that support each judgment. If the original only says the reader may apply, write down the questions of who can confirm the outcome and how.
2. Give the reader just the draft and the material they’d have at this stage. Using the contract question, you could ask what date they’d plan around if all they had was the phone reply. Have them say what they’d do next in their own words, then point to the sentence that supports their answer. Hear them out and note where they looked and what they relied on. If you supply a condition aloud, write down the sentence you said. That gives you a place to look when you go back to the draft. [The contract-reading question](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol5_06_Answering_the_Critics_of_Plain_Language.pdf)
3. If you use AI, give it the shareable original, the draft, and the point where the reader got stuck. Copy this prompt if it fits.

   > 1. Shorten the sentences that caused trouble and keep who approves, what reply counts, and when the new deadline takes effect.
   > 2. Preserve permissions in the original, keeping “may” as “may.”
   > 3. Remove steps the original leaves out and add no new ones. Turn the removed steps and gaps in the original into questions, and ask the person who sets the rule to confirm them.
   > 4. For each revised sentence, include the wording in the original that supports it.

   Check each match yourself. Look for a submitted request that has turned into an approval or a new date the original never gave.

4. Give the revision to another person like the intended reader and ask the same question. See whether they can find the relevant paragraph on their own, explain which step they’ve reached, and point to the sentence that determines what comes next. If they got the answer from experience, record that too. If you only have the original reader, use the second try to find what’s still unclear. They may remember the first answer, so look at what they can point to in the draft this time.

Give yourself two minutes. Open a guide you’ve just written or asked AI to shorten and pick the step you know so well you tend to skip over it. If you catch yourself thinking someone usually checks one more thing, write this question beside that line. With just this receipt in hand, who does the reader still need to hear from, and in what form? If the original answers it, add the answer. If it leaves the question open, show the shareable part of the receipt to the person who sets the rule and ask them to spell out that step.
