Does the economic cycle affect the corporate profit cycle?

Yes, the economic cycle affects the corporate profit cycle; the corporate profit cycle, in turn, affects the profit data announced by listed companies; the profit data announced by listed companies affects investors' attitudes; investors' mindset cycles affect market volatility; market volatility cycles affect the tightness of credit availability, and the credit cycle, in turn, affects the economic cycle. The economic cycle affects corporate sales revenue, thereby changing corporate profits and influencing the corporate profit cycle. However, due to differences in operating leverage and financial leverage among different companies, the impact of changes in sales revenue on corporate profits varies, leading to different variations in corporate profit cycles. Nevertheless, all corporate profit cycles share a common phenomenon: the upward and downward swings in corporate profit cycles are significantly greater than the fluctuations in the national economic cycle. The economic cycle is more stable than the corporate profit cycle.

What are the main factors that influence corporate profits?

Economic upturns and downturns affect sales revenue. Economic growth determines the process of expansion or contraction in corporate sales. When GDP grows strongly, corporate sales revenue generally grows strongly as well; when GDP growth is weak, corporate sales revenue also tends to be weak; and when GDP declines, corporate sales revenue generally declines as well.

Operating Leverage: When the economic situation is good and corporate sales revenue increases, operating leverage causes the growth in corporate profits to exceed the growth in sales revenue. Conversely, when the economic situation is poor and sales revenue declines, operating leverage causes the decline in profits to be greater than the decline in sales revenue. If sales revenue drops too much, it can even push a company from profitability into loss.

Most companies' costs can be divided into three categories: fixed costs, semi-fixed costs, and variable costs. The sum of these three types of costs is the company's total operating costs. These three types of costs vary in sensitivity to sales revenue. When we say that the gross margin increases, it means that the growth in operating profit is significantly greater than the growth in sales revenue. This is operating leverage: a one percentage point increase in operating revenue can drive a greater percentage point increase in operating profit. Generally speaking, the higher the proportion of fixed costs in a company's total costs, the lower the proportion of variable costs, which leads to a higher level of operating leverage.

Financial Leverage: A company's capital is also a cost, composed of equity capital and debt capital. If the debt capital is fixed-income securities and the equity capital is non-fixed-income securities, then a decline in sales revenue will affect the proportion of equity capital and debt capital in the company's capital structure, thereby affecting the company's profits. This is the impact of financial leverage on corporate profits. Changes in external factors outside the economic system, such as management decisions on inventory, production scale, and capital investment; technological progress; changes in laws, regulations, and tax policies; and changes in external factors beyond the industry, even beyond the business world, such as weather, war, and fashion trends. These special external factors are the main reasons why fluctuations in corporate sales revenue and profits can be much greater than those in the economic cycle.
