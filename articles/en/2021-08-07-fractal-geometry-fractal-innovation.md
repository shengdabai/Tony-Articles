The fundamental idea of fractal geometry is that objective things have self-similar hierarchical structures. The local and the whole are statistically similar in terms of shape, function, information, time, and space, which is known as self-similarity. For example, every part of a magnet is like the whole magnet in having a north and south pole. If you keep dividing it, each part still has the same magnetic field as the whole magnet. This self-similar hierarchical structure remains unchanged when the geometric size is appropriately magnified or reduced. The "fractal fever" that began in the early 1980s has never faded. As a new concept and method, fractals are being explored in many fields. The American physicist John Wheeler once said: "In the future, anyone who doesn't understand fractals cannot be considered a cultured person in science." This shows how important fractals are. Professor Zhou Haizhong, a renowned scholar in China, believes that fractal geometry not only showcases the beauty of mathematics but also reveals the essence of the world and changes the way people understand the mysteries of nature. One could say that fractal geometry is truly the geometry that describes nature, and the study of it has greatly expanded the boundaries of human cognition. As a very popular and active new theory and discipline in the world today, the emergence of fractal geometry has made people re-examine the world: the world is nonlinear, and fractals are everywhere. Fractal geometry not only allows people to feel the fusion of science and art, the unity of mathematics and aesthetic appreciation, but also has profound methodological significance in science.

From the perspective of "evolution," the logic we often use can be divided into two types: 1. The mechanistic logic applicable to the industrial era, for example, how the world was described back then as a huge "clock," with various gears of different sizes working together to push each other. 2. Darwin's "theory of evolution." At this point, mechanistic logic becomes ineffective in the internet age. With the information explosion, variables are constantly emerging. For example, cells, microbes, and the brain—if you try to explain this logic using "mechanistic" reasoning, you'll fail.

An organization is a fractal of a group of "founders": many management models and innovative thinking in enterprises are reflected in history and "biology." For instance, in high school biology, there are several key terms: "external mutagenic factors," "chemical factors," "biological factors," and "base mutations." Base mutations are like when a company sends a group of outstanding personnel to other companies or business schools to learn excellent ideas, and then they bring these ideas back and implement them across various departments, thus improving results or certain processes. Chemical factors are like sudden public opinion or risks a company or brand faces in the market. External mutagenic factors are like the environment, risks, and resource allocation the company currently faces. Biological factors are like a group of people with different ideas and perspectives quietly influencing the company's organizational atmosphere. Sometimes, the underlying disciplines can better reflect commercial behavior. It's clear that theprototype of all organizations is decided by "a few founders." In the early stages of a company, resources and survival space are extremely limited, so everything might be disrupted. For example, to survive, you might choose "staged talent." To improve efficiency, managers might choose more "executors," and all of this gradually dilutes the "original vision." When the business scales up and stability is achieved, the most urgent thing to accelerate is the "company culture." This entire process is like mutation and evolution. Companies and businesses are different; businesses are the "subsystems" of the company. Businesses can die, but the organization cannot. A company is built by its "core people." As long as you find the right people, establish themanagement philosophy and rules, and identify the cognitive gaps between upstream and downstream, you can form your own market and build a value network. However, if you innovate within an old business without splitting the old organization to find breakthroughs, the past experience and advantages might "actually bind the future's certainty" when new opportunities, trends, or technology arrive.
