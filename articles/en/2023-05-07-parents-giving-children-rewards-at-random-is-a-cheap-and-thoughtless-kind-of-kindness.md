### **01**

### **Why "Reward-Based Education" Should Not Be Promoted?**

As psychology becomes more popular, some theories have started to be known to the public and applied to child-rearing without a full understanding.

> The theory of associative learning suggests that all learning is a process of establishing a direct connection between a stimulus (S) and a response (R).

There are two famous experiments that may be familiar to many. 1. Pavlov's dogs: After the bell and food are connected, the dogs salivate at the sound of the bell. 2. Skinner's rats: When the rat presses a lever and gets food, after repeated attempts, the rat learns to use the lever. Some people might think, if someone repeatedly practices with rewards, can they form a habit? For example, through rewards, can a child be trained to go to bed on time and complete homework? So there are parenting articles suggesting that external rewards can be used to cultivate habits and develop a love for learning. Therefore, many kindergartens may have such a point system, like giving a child a small red flower or a sticker. When they accumulate enough, they can exchange for bigger rewards, like an extra hour of watching cartoons on weekends or an extra ice cream.

Does this work? The answer tells you, **at first, it really works, and the effect is immediate. You'll find that with rewards, the child seems to suddenly become well-behaved.** But the side effects come quickly. 1. The child will constantly demand more rewards. 2. The child will only be willing to complete tasks when pushed by rewards. 3. The child will avoid effort and be unwilling to tackle more difficult tasks. **The longer the time, the more obvious the side effects become.** In the community, there are parents who have mentioned this issue.

> I've read *The Game Changers*, which mentions that game design uses the "improve skill -> immediate feedback -> improve skill -> immediate feedback" model, which makes people addicted and unable to let go. I tried to borrow this model, but found that life is too complex to know where to start.

In fact, not only children, but adult family members also have similar problems. I'm more of a long-term believer, but there are often these voices at home: "Academic performance is important, I can see it. Your method, I don't know when it will bear fruit."

This parent borrowed the model of immediate feedback from games to reward the child to cultivate interest. But they found it didn't work well. It was hard to implement and had side effects. They originally wanted to raise a child with a long-term mindset, but ended up raising a child with a short-term mindset.

### **02**

### **Why Does the Reward System Have Side Effects?**

####

Why do rewards weaken a child's intrinsic motivation?

The answer lies in the existence of the "overjustification effect" (overjustification effect).

> If people have a sufficient intrinsic reason for a behavior, such as interest, then their understanding of the behavior and its reason is coordinated.
> But if a more attractive stimulus (such as monetary reward) is given to their behavior, adding an "excessive" reason, then people's understanding of their behavior will shift to these more attractive external reasons, reducing or abandoning the original intrinsic reasons.
> At this point, the behavior shifts from internal control to external control. If the external reason no longer exists, such as no longer providing monetary rewards, the behavior loses its reason and tends to stop.

In 1973, a paper by Mark R. and Richard E. from Stanford University and Michigan University proved the existence of the overjustification effect.

*Undermining Children's Intrinsic Interest With Extrinsic Reward*

Paper link: https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1974-10497-001

Psychologists designed an experiment. Three groups of children took a drawing test. Group A was given a predetermined reward, Group B was given a random reward, and Group C had no reward. Children in Group A, who received a predetermined reward, performed the worst because the reward destroyed their intrinsic motivation. Children in Group B, who received a random reward, performed the best, but only in the short term, and their interest would gradually be replaced by the reward. Children in Group C, who had no reward, performed moderately, but in the long run, no reward was beneficial for stimulating their initiative. In other words, both rewards and random rewards can damage a child's intrinsic motivation to some extent. The side effects of rewards have been confirmed, but you may not know that even worse, some parents unknowingly overuse rewards, and it can lead to bad habits in children. In daily life, have parents used the "random reward method"? Yes, many have, and many parents have used this effective method to shape children's bad behaviors. For example, when a child is noisy, many parents will pull out their phones, and the child will immediately quiet down, and it works every time. This approach seems to "control" the child, but in reality, it's rewarding the child's noisy behavior with the phone. After a while, the parent may feel that giving the child too much screen time is bad and refuse the child's request to play with the phone. This intermittent reward is precisely the most effective reward method—“random reward method.” The child will become more “determined” to be noisy until the parent gives up the phone. Thus, we are unknowingly constantly rewarding the child's noisy behavior, and the child becomes more addicted to the phone. In the community, there are also parents who made this mistake: the child repeatedly asks you to buy something, and you refuse, but you can't help giving in.

> The child is satisfied once, and the next time he will be even more demanding and beg or cry. I did this badly. ① I bought the child many new stationery items, mostly costing about ten yuan, sometimes when he looked pitiful, I couldn't help buying it. ② He has always wanted to keep a cat, but I refused for almost a year. But this summer, when he went to his grandfather's house, his grandfather fulfilled his wish, and now there are already seven or eight animals at home. ③ My child has high resilience, and even after being refused multiple times, he doesn't give up. I can't help giving in after a few refusals. After all, high resilience is a good thing, but he uses this resilience against us as parents, and it's really hard to resist. So I'm very troubled about this.

Sometimes the child is rewarded for crying, sometimes not. This is precisely using the most effective "random reward method." As a parent, you might think it's the child's high resilience, but in reality, this so-called "resilience" is cultivated by your inappropriate rewards. Your occasional giving in is reinforcing the child's crying as a "high point." Many parents say, "Oh, I've spoiled my child." Unthinking rewards are the key to spoiling children. Using rewards to praise a child's achievements is also a key to weakening their intrinsic motivation. Using the most effective method to reward bad behavior and an ineffective method to reward good behavior—when you look back, isn't it terrifying? Even more terrifying is that the reward mechanism is the easiest way to fall into inertia, because it's too effective and has immediate results. Many parents have gone to an extreme, relying too much on rewards to get the child to do things. Your reward was meant for the child's good, but it ended up going the opposite way. This is actually an unthinking, cheap kind of kindness. Don't give the child **unthinking** **kindness**, and **abuse rewards**, which is a form of **unthinking kindness**. In fact, the effort a child puts into anything can itself become an intrinsic reward. In other words, effort itself is a reward.

### **03**

### **Why Cognitive Effort Itself Has Intrinsic Value?**

What does that mean?
It means that a child who practices thousands of hours to play the piano will be happy, a child who draws a picture and carefully adds each stroke will be happy, and a child who solves a math problem and uses up a page of draft paper will also be happy. In fact, the child likes doing these things, and he doesn't need your external intervention.
A paper published on January 31, 2022, in one of the world's four most famous journals, *PANS*, confirmed this conclusion: **effort can become an intrinsic reward, and cognitive effort itself has intrinsic value.**

*Rewarding Cognitive Effort Increases the Intrinsic Value of Mental Labor*

Paper link: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2111785119

Are you surprised by this answer? Let's look at an experiment to see how psychologists verified the "value of effort." Researchers found 121 students at a German university and divided them into two groups, who did math problems of difficulty levels 1 to 5. Group A was a random reward group, and regardless of their results, they would get a random reward of 1 to 60 cents for completing a set of math problems, which was unrelated to their performance. Group B was a reward group based on effort, meaning the more effort the students put in, the more reward they received.
The researchers used objective indicators like blood pressure and heart rate to determine the students' effort levels. The first experiment was completed. Group A, regardless of how they performed, received a reward, which was unrelated to their performance or effort. Group B, the students who put in more effort, received more rewards. Both groups of students were told in advance that they would receive a reward, but they were not told what determined the reward.
Now the second experiment begins, with the same math problems of difficulty levels 1 to 5, and the same two groups of students. The difference is that the researchers told them at the beginning: this time, you can choose a difficulty level of math problems to do, you will not receive any reward, and your performance will not be indicated. This scenario is very similar to how you educate your child. I give my child rewards every day, but I can't afford it, and the child keeps bargaining with me. I have to make him learn on his own. Oh, they say self-driven motivation is good. From today on, I want to start cultivating my child's self-driven motivation and stop rewarding him. So you bring your child and seriously tell him: "You've grown up now, you need to work hard on your own. From today on, I won't reward you for doing your homework." What were the results? Group A students generally chose lower difficulty tasks. Group B students generally chose higher difficulty tasks. (The effort-reward group generally chose higher difficulty tasks in the second experiment.)
Moreover, from the results, Group B, which was rewarded based on effort in the first experiment, still chose higher difficulty math problems without any reward, and their accuracy did not decrease. You see, Group B students didn't receive any reward, yet they still chose higher difficulty tasks and performed well. This is not exactly intrinsic motivation, is it? Why did the two groups of students make different choices without any reward? The researchers conducted extensive data analysis and eliminated interference factors and discovered the existence of "the value of effort."

> with our assumption derived from learning theory that effort can gain the quality of a secondary reinforcer and become gradually intrinsically rewarding when it frequently leads to extrinsic reward repeatedly paired with reward

— With our assumption derived from learning theory, when effort frequently leads to extrinsic reward, it can become a secondary reinforcer and gradually become intrinsically rewarding. In other words, **the attitude of "effort" itself can be reinforced through rewards. Once "effort" is reinforced, the child doesn't need external rewards to actively put in effort to complete a task.** For example, a child who is bad at math occasionally tries hard to do math problems, and you notice it and give him a look of approval, saying, "Child, no matter how well you do, you're the best for trying." His effort is rewarded by your feedback.
Then, he finds that his effort has improved his math score by 50%, and he gets positive feedback again.
Finally, he gets a high score on the exam and feels a great sense of achievement. He thinks, "Wow, my effort can bring so many rewards."
In this way, the child's effort is reinforced, and the rewards he gets from his effort are internalized as his own motivation.
Throughout the process, you never said, "If you score X, I'll reward you," and you're very smart, you don't say, "If you make a mistake, you're a stupid child."
But in the next math problem, he enjoys the feeling of effort and thinking.
Over time, the child will develop "achievement motivation."

> in real life, effort rewarding socialization practices occur over years of learning to promote the development of a strong and generalized intrinsic motivation to seek demanding tasks. Such a generalized intrinsic motivation to seek effort has been investigated as a personality characteristic called achievement motive

— In real life, effort-reward socialization practices occur over years of learning to promote the development of a strong and generalized intrinsic motivation to seek demanding tasks. This generalized intrinsic motivation to seek effort has been investigated as a personality characteristic called achievement motive. Children who develop "achievement motivation" will voluntarily challenge higher difficulty tasks and not fear failure, because the effort they put in during the challenge will make them feel the greatest reward.

### 04

### How Can Parents Avoid Children Falling into the Trap of "Weakening Intrinsic Motivation"?

Now you understand that children naturally have intrinsic motivation, and parents shouldn't easily destroy it. Next, I'll talk about it from another angle: how can parents avoid letting children fall into the trap of "weakening intrinsic motivation"? Many parents mistakenly believe that children need immediate feedback. In fact, a large part of the reason is that you want to design the child's life, rather than let the child develop on their own.

As Li Xiaolai mentioned in his class:

> Parents always first set their own pre-set goals, and then all their behavior is aimed at pursuing this artificially designed goal... In the end, everyone hopes to be successful, but almost no one doesn't fail or feel disappointed.

If you are a design theorist, you might think: the child must develop an interest at a certain age and not interrupt it, the child must rank high in high school, the child must get into a certain university, study a certain major, and find a certain job after graduation. A design theorist would think that the child must meet certain requirements to be considered successful. What if the child has no interest? Then just reward them, it's simple and efficient. So, a design theorist would want to use rewards to motivate the child, step by step "guide" the child onto the path you think is good for him. If you are a design theorist, your eyes may only see the child's grades, not his effort, and even if you don't say it, the child can feel it. Over time, the child under your influence will lose his own initiative. That's why many children with good grades don't know what they want to do after university and have never thought about what they like. Life is not a game, and you can't design a precise reward system to make the child step by step follow your expected direction. Life is a developing process, not something designed. It is the result of many comprehensive reasons and events, accumulating step by step and developing through mutual influence. A line from *Zeng Guang Xian Wen* is the true reflection of a development theorist.

> But do good deeds, don't ask about the future. With time, the result is unpredictable.

Think about it carefully, and you'll find that it's actually the same as the scientific theory we mentioned earlier. **Do good deeds, don't ask about the future: let the child put in effort and enjoy the process of effort, and don't judge him by his grades.** **With time, the result is unpredictable: when the child learns to internalize effort as a reward, after years of accumulation, he will have "achievement motivation" and set high standards for himself.** As a parent, you must first become a development theorist, and your child can become a development theorist, right?

### 05

How should rewards be used? How can we cultivate children's intrinsic motivation?

1. **Don't overuse rewards. Don't use rewards for things the child should do on their own.**
For example, brushing teeth, sleeping, and studying are things the child should do on their own. Clearly tell the child that they must do it, and if they don't, there will be consequences.
In most cases, as long as you clearly explain to the child, they will listen. If the child has a hard-to-change bad habit, parents should prefer punishment over overusing rewards, and not turn the child's natural tasks into a transaction.

2. As a parent, believe that your child naturally has intrinsic motivation and let them find positive feedback from their own efforts. You might have a question: If I don't give rewards, how can the child see feedback? In fact, when the direction is correct, the effort a person puts in will almost always have feedback. The child can feel it themselves. If the child continues to work hard without seeing any feedback, the direction is probably wrong. If the direction is wrong and there's no feedback, let the child learn to adjust on their own. Think about it carefully. A simple example is when a child plays with sand. The sand castle falls down again and again, but the child is still happy. Because in his eyes, the castle he built is gradually taking shape according to his imagination, which is a form of positive feedback. You don't need to say, "I'll give you an ice cream if you build this sand castle," do you? The same logic applies to learning. When the child puts in effort and completes the problem, they can see whether they're right or wrong. They can also see their scores gradually rising and the admiration from their classmates. The child is capable of finding positive feedback on their own. Parents don't need to interfere.

3. If you do want to reward, encourage the child's effort, not the child's performance. Often, parents' words and actions also destroy the child's natural positive feedback. For example, if the child does well on a test, you say, "You're so smart," and reward them with money. If they do poorly, you criticize or even punish them. This kind of reward not only replaces the child's natural positive feedback but also makes the child associate high effort with failure and punishment. Because you're giving the child the idea that only success makes their effort effective. That's why children who are often praised for being smart tend to back down when faced with difficulties.

The conclusion in Part 03 also mentions this.

> If in everyday life, reward is based primarily on success, people will learn that high effort signals that a task involves a high likelihood of failure. Consequently, effort becomes a secondary punisher promoting effort avoidance.

— If in daily life, rewards are primarily based on success, people will learn that high effort signals that a task involves a high likelihood of failure. Therefore, effort becomes a secondary punisher that promotes effort avoidance. So, encouraging the child when they put in effort is far more effective than rewarding them based on their performance.
Let's summarize this article.

**Rewarding children to develop interest can lead to a series of side effects, and the longer the time, the more obvious the side effects become. Because of the existence of the "overjustification effect," the child's intrinsic motivation will gradually be replaced by external rewards.**
As a parent, be a development theorist, not a design theorist.

Finally, here are three suggestions.
1. Don't overuse rewards.
2. Let the child learn to find positive feedback from their own efforts.
3. Encourage effort, not reward performance.
Thank you for reading this article carefully. I hope it helps you.
