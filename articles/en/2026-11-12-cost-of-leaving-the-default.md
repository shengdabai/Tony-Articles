# The Cost of Leaving the Default

> Publication date:2026-11-12 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-12-退出默认选项的代价.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-12-cost-of-leaving-the-default.md)

---

Does an off switch mean you have a real choice about an AI feature?

I think we need to check one more thing: after switching it off, can you still do what you came to do? A switch certainly helps. But some options look available until you actually choose them and discover that you need another tool, have to move your materials, or must learn a new process. Declining a convenience can be a lot of work.

This is the standard I would rather apply to AI products: a default can save people from making unnecessary decisions while still leaving them able to change their minds. Otherwise, the time it saves may be collected again as the cost of leaving it. For people who want to learn and make things with AI, the practical question is whether they can still change how they work once the tool starts doing things for them.

## Isn't an off switch enough?

Look at what remains afterward.

A clipped article discussing the bundling of AI features with basic services raises this question. The tension it raised was simple: a product may let you decline one feature while taking other capabilities away with it. The judgment I draw from that is that choice needs to be assessed across the whole task, beyond the settings page.

Suppose you use an AI writing tool to find sources, organize materials, and generate a draft. You like its help collecting public originals, but this time you want to build the argument yourself, so you turn off automatic drafting. The source access disappears too, and moving your materials out is difficult. You can switch tools, of course. You just have to do the earlier work again.

Now there is a gap between the choice shown on the screen and the choice you can practically make. You declined automatic drafting, but the price includes finding and organizing your sources again. Keeping the thinking step you wanted to do yourself has become an extra expense.

I call this an exit cost: the additional time, effort, and loss of existing work involved in departing from the default while still trying to accomplish the original task. It arises in small, everyday changes of mind as well as when you close an account. Continued use might reflect a preference for the default, or it might reflect how difficult the other routes have become.

The cost can also change as you keep using a product. When you have no materials in it, moving is easy. Once it holds sources, annotations, and unfinished work, changing your mind becomes harder. So checking whether departure is easy requires more than trying an empty account. We also need to see whether someone can continue a task that is already half finished. The more work a user has invested, the more that distinction matters.

Usage counts and activation rates can tell us what happened. On their own, they cannot tell us whether users wanted it. When evaluating a product, I want to check whether it makes changing methods reasonably easy alongside making continued use easy.

## Why wouldn't someone want less work?

The same step can serve different purposes.

Take the same writing task. When you need to deliver an explanation quickly, automatic drafting may be a good fit. When you want to understand where your judgment comes from, selecting the material and deciding what to say first are part of the task itself. The finished pieces may look similar while their writers want different things from the process.

This does not require us to keep every step for ourselves. Formatting can be delegated, and AI can help find counterexamples. The question is who decides which steps are worth keeping. A tool that treats doing less yourself as the only direction of progress can remove the very activity its user wants to practice.

[A high school mathematics study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) helps clarify this distinction. Conducted at one school in Turkey in fall 2023, it examined practice and immediate unaided exam performance after the use of two GPT-4 tools. GPT-4 is an AI model that responds to written questions; this mathematics study does not directly describe adult writing or long-term ability. A standard chat version improved assisted practice performance but led to worse performance on the subsequent unaided exams. Another version included teacher-provided information about the problems and instructed the tool to work mainly through hints. It largely eliminated the negative effect, but the study did not provide enough evidence that it improved unaided exam scores.

What interests me here is the design objective. Help can be organized around getting an answer quickly, or around keeping the learner involved in solving the problem. The latter requires concrete arrangements, including problem information and the way hints are given. Calling a tool a learning assistant does not do that work by itself.

A single person can also change purposes. Wanting to finish a draft quickly in the morning and explore a question through the same material in the evening is perfectly reasonable. Dividing people permanently into those who want AI and those who do not misses this change. A more useful unit of choice is one step in the task at hand. Accepting help with research need not include accepting someone else's arrangement of the argument.

In writing, building an argument yourself and receiving a draft should therefore be choices you can separate. But having a choice does not guarantee choosing well. Students in the study were overly optimistic about their learning outcomes. We need both room to change methods and ways to check what actually happens. Saying that users chose for themselves cannot carry the whole responsibility.

## How strong should the default be?

Strong, but separable where it matters.

I support products that offer a smooth default. Facing a blank page and then having to understand a pile of settings is exhausting. For a beginner, a process that works from beginning to end can be more helpful than studying every option in advance. That is a product design tradeoff; it need not turn every user into a configuration expert.

Yet organizing information and making a judgment can sit next to each other in a workflow. Doing the first well does not automatically justify taking over the second. [An experiment by Dell’Acqua and colleagues](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/dell-acqua-et-al-2026-navigating-the-jagged-technological-frontier_5c589c8c-fbb5-458f-b285-c944746cd717.pdf) involved 758 consultants using GPT-4 from 2023, with the paper published in 2026. AI improved performance on a set of product innovation tasks but reduced correctness on another task that required combining interviews with numbers from a spreadsheet. The experiment measured particular task outcomes, not the effects of designing an exit option.

One detail stands out: on the judgment task, AI-assisted recommendations were more coherent and persuasive even when their conclusions were wrong. Accepting an entire workflow can sometimes include accepting its definition of finished. A polished page and a well-formed explanation can still rest on a judgment that needs another look.

My requirement for a default is therefore that users can change direction at meaningful steps. A writing tool can offer a draft by default while retaining a way to examine sources and organize materials yourself. Every internal operation need not become a button. But declining a particular step should not take other useful parts away with it.

We should also distinguish undoing an output from changing a process. Deleting a generated draft removes the result. If the original materials remain hidden behind summaries, the user still has to understand them in the sequence the tool has arranged. Rebuilding an argument requires access to the materials themselves. An undo button and a process that lets you organize the material independently address different needs.

Automation can also carry human care. [Writing in 2011 about CD Baby's shipping email, Derek Sivers](https://sive.rs/cdbe) described replacing an ordinary notification with an extravagantly playful message that was then sent automatically with orders. This is a founder's recollection. It does not compare customer acquisition with and without the email, so we cannot tell whether the email brought in the new customers he reported. My takeaway is modest: a person can think something through carefully once and have a system repeat it well.

Doing everything manually has never been my concern here. A carefully designed default deserves repeated use. Trouble begins when the user's purpose changes and the system can only keep repeating itself. A good default should also accommodate the next request being different.

## Who maintains the alternative?

The designer has work to do.

Someone will reasonably point out that another workflow means another maintenance burden. For a small team, that is substantial. Both routes need explanations, materials must carry across, and failures have to be traced to the right step. Demanding that every feature stay exactly as it was would make progress difficult.

That objection narrows what I ask for. I do not propose preserving every old interface forever or making a route without AI as fast as the route with it. I care about whether there is a workable alternative for the user's core purpose, and how much of their invested work survives the change.

An escalator makes reaching the next floor of a shopping center easier. The stairs may be farther away and slower, but someone who does not take the escalator can still reach the same floor. Software is more complicated: some services exist only because AI makes them possible, and removing their core capability cannot be expected to leave the result intact. The requirement applies to separable parts, such as retaining access to read and save collected materials after drafting has been disabled.

An alternative need not be a complete parallel system. It could be a materials page that preserves sources, an editable file, or clear instructions for continuing the task. Maintaining it lets people change their method while preserving useful accumulated work.

File export is only a beginning. In that writing task, exported text containing conclusions without their source relationships still makes further work difficult. A useful handoff lets someone distinguish original material, generated suggestions, and parts they have already edited. It need not preserve every internal process of the tool, but it should preserve the basis for the next judgment.

[In his 2013 essay on startups, Paul Graham](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html) emphasized that the early product is only part of the user experience and that serving users directly helps founders revise their understanding of what people need. These are observations from advising startups and selected cases. They cannot show that the same practices will produce the same results for a typical small team. My extension is that the alternative route belongs to the product experience too. Designers discover what their default overlooks when people actually try that route.

Still, declining the default should not automatically turn into an obligation for customer support to do everything. A product can state its support limits and explain that a particular route costs more. Clear conditions let users decide whether to accept them. Neither designers nor users need to pretend every method is equally cheap.

## What can you check today?

Try the route without the default.

There is no need to rush into changing tools or prove how much you can do without AI. I want to check whether the current tool lets me change just one step in a task. A small, low-risk task is enough for this check, and the result should be used only to assess that particular workflow.

You can use the writing tool you already have:

1. Write down what you want to finish and mark the step you want to do yourself. You might need a short article and want to decide how its materials support the conclusion, while still accepting help with research and formatting.
2. Find the setting or control for that step. Disable only automatic drafting, then check whether your materials, sources, and editing functions remain available. If you cannot find the control, record that too.
3. Finish the task through the alternative route and record the extra work. Did it take another click or a new search for sources? Did the interface change, or did your annotations disappear? These distinctions are more concrete than a statement that a feature can be disabled.
4. Decide whether to keep the default. If it fits your purpose, continue using it. If it prevents you from separating an important step, adjust the process or look for a tool that supports the way you want to work.

Before starting, you can also write down the difficulties you expect, then compare them with what actually happens. Something you expected to redo may already have an entry point. Something you expected to take a click may require reorganizing all your materials. This gives you a concrete difference to examine instead of rating a tool through your enthusiasm or dislike for AI.

This is not a scientifically validated product scale, and there is no universal threshold. The same additional work may be acceptable to one person and exhausting to someone who repeats it every day. The importance of the task, the frequency of changing methods, and the survival of existing materials all belong in the decision.

Afterward, I want to be able to answer a specific question: am I declining automatic drafting, or am I also giving up research and saving my materials? The first means choosing a method. The second calls for reassessing the price of the convenience.

For toolmakers, this offers a more concrete check than asking whether users like the product: find a step someone has a reason to skip, then follow the remaining process all the way through. If it works only because the designer stands beside the user fixing things, the alternative is not ready. If a step cannot be separated, explain why.

I propose a design direction that still needs testing: keeping an alternative may make people more comfortable trying the default. Being able to take their materials and adjust afterward means that trying it once need not amount to committing to the same method indefinitely. This is my proposal, not a finding from the studies above. Whether it actually makes people more willing to try a product still needs to be observed in that product.

I am willing to give AI more of the execution while keeping room to change how I work. The attention saved can go toward judging the goal. When that goal changes, the tool should be able to accommodate it. That is a convenience worth depending on over time.

Leave yourself a little room for your next change of mind.
