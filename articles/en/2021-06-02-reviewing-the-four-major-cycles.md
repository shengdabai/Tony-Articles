**Government Regulation of the Business Cycle**

The government regulates the business cycle to control inflation and employment rates. It aims to prevent economic growth from being too fast or too slow. The economy has cycles, and these cycles tend to reach extremes—economic weakness leads to unemployment, while excessive economic strength inevitably leads to future recessions, neither of which is desirable. Therefore, a key part of the central bank and the Ministry of Finance's work is managing the business cycle, which is known as government countercyclical regulation. The government mainly regulates the cycle through fiscal policy, including tax policy and government spending policy. When the government stimulates economic growth, it can cut taxes and increase government spending. When it believes the economy is growing too fast, it increases taxes and reduces spending. Central bank regulation mainly focuses on controlling inflation rates. A central bank governor who acts quickly to control inflation is considered hawkish, while one who acts quickly to stimulate economic growth and promote employment is considered dovish. The best approach is to balance between hawkish and dovish policies, controlling inflation while ensuring employment and maintaining steady, healthy economic growth. The Ministry of Finance's main focus is managing the cycle through tax policy and government spending policy, ensuring that economic growth is neither too fast nor too slow. Fiscal deficit: commonly used, when the economy is weak, government spending exceeds fiscal revenue, mainly from taxes, increasing the fiscal deficit to expand government spending and stimulate demand. Fiscal surplus: rare, when the economy is strong, government spending is less than fiscal revenue, increasing fiscal surplus.

Government regulation of the business cycle has limited effectiveness. The government's regulation is often in the opposite direction of the economic cycle; managing these intervention tools is far from easy. If it were easy, we wouldn't see so many extreme fluctuations in the economic cycle. The government can stimulate the economy through fiscal policy by increasing government spending, or it can slow down economic growth by increasing taxes. However, the government's money isn't just windfall—it comes from tax revenue. When the government urgently needs money to push the economy, tax revenue often starts to decline, so they want to spend but don't have the money. Eventually, they have to issue government bonds, increasing the fiscal deficit. But how much government debt is reasonable? There has always been debate. If the government overborrows, people will worry about how it will repay. It's said that the debt will be repaid through future taxes, but it seems that deficit spending has never stopped. That is, each year, the government's income is less than its spending, leading to increasingly large debts.

**Corporate Earnings Cycle**

The economic cycle affects the corporate earnings cycle; the corporate earnings cycle, in turn, affects the earnings data announced by listed companies; the earnings data of listed companies affects investors' attitudes; investors' attitudes affect market volatility; market volatility affects the tightness of credit availability, and the credit cycle affects the economic cycle. The economic cycle influences corporate sales revenue, thereby changing corporate profits and affecting the corporate earnings cycle. However, due to different operating leverage and financial leverage, the impact of changes in sales revenue on the profits of different companies varies, leading to different changes in the corporate earnings cycle. However, all corporate earnings cycles share a common phenomenon: the upward and downward swings in corporate earnings are much greater than the fluctuations in the national economic cycle. The economic cycle is more stable than the corporate earnings cycle.

The up and down movements in the economy affect sales revenue. Economic growth determines the process of expanding or contracting corporate sales. When GDP grows strongly, corporate sales revenue generally also grows strongly; when GDP growth is weak, corporate sales revenue generally also grows weakly; when GDP declines, corporate sales revenue generally also declines. Operating leverage: when the economic situation is good, corporate sales revenue increases, and operating leverage allows corporate profits to grow more than sales revenue. If the economic situation is poor, corporate sales revenue declines, and operating leverage causes corporate profits to decline more than sales revenue. If sales revenue declines too much, it can even turn a profitable company into a loss-making one.

Most companies' costs can be divided into three categories: fixed costs, semi-fixed costs, and variable costs. The sum of these three types of costs is the company's total operating costs. These three types of costs have different sensitivities to sales revenue. When we say that the gross margin rises, it means that the company's operating profit grows much more than its sales revenue. This is operating leverage: a one percent increase in operating revenue can leverage a greater percentage increase in operating profit. Generally, the higher the proportion of fixed costs in a company's total costs, the lower the proportion of variable costs, leading to higher operating leverage. Financial leverage: a company's capital is also a cost, composed of equity capital and debt capital. If the debt capital is fixed-income securities and equity capital is non-fixed-income securities, a decrease in sales revenue will affect the company's profitability due to the different proportions of equity and debt capital. This is the impact of financial leverage on corporate profits. Changes in external factors outside the economic system, such as management decisions on inventory, production scale, and capital investment; technological progress; changes in laws and tax policies; and changes in external factors outside the industry, even outside the business sector, such as weather, war, and fashion trends. These special external factors are the main reasons for the greater fluctuations in corporate sales revenue and profits compared to economic cycle fluctuations.

**Risk Attitude Cycle**

Risk is the most important variable in investing. At any specific point in time, the overall attitude of investors toward risk and the actions they take are crucial in shaping the entire investment environment, and we find ourselves in this environment. Understanding the current state of the investment environment is critical at this point, as it determines how we should view risk and how to act accordingly. Assessing the position of the risk attitude cycle is the most important content.

Risk aversion is an essential element of investing. Most investors are risk-averse. Risk-averse investors are cautious when handling investments. When considering investments, especially those with high risk, they carefully analyze and study. Risk-averse investors incorporate conservative assumptions and appropriate skepticism into their investment analysis. They require a larger margin of safety for risky investments to protect against potential analysis errors and unpleasant surprises. Risk-averse investors insist on a significant risk premium, meaning they expect higher incremental returns, which makes them willing to take on risky investments. They reject irrational, foolish investment transactions. When investors become too relaxed about risk aversion and become overly risk-tolerant, they tend to feel better about the environment and are more optimistic about future outcomes. As a result, their cautiousness in the investment process decreases. Since they no longer perceive investments as risky, they feel there's no need to carefully analyze them. Investors often make broader assumptions and substitute trust for skepticism. They are willing to accept lower margins of safety even if it means taking on more risk. Investors no longer worry about risk as much as before and don't require such a high risk premium to feel secure. They are no longer so rigid or particular, as they focus more on the attractive returns of risky investments and are less vigilant about the risks behind those returns.

Positive events lead to increased optimism; increased optimism leads to higher risk tolerance; higher risk tolerance leads to lower required risk premiums; lower required risk premiums mean lower required returns for risky assets; lower required returns for risky assets lead to higher asset prices; higher asset prices mean higher risk levels (but this also attracts "momentum traders" who chase higher prices and buy more stocks). Human psychology and emotions can easily become overly optimistic and overly risk-tolerant. When the situation turns downward, people tend to become overly concerned and risk-averse. This means that the time when people are most eager to buy is often the time when they should be most cautious, and the time when people are most reluctant to buy is often the time when they should be most aggressive. Outstanding investors recognize this and strive to act contrary to the crowd, engaging in contrarian investing.

**Emotional Cycle—Investment Psychology and the Emotional Pendulum**

Emotional fluctuations in the securities market are like the movement of a pendulum. The pendulum swings back and forth, forming an arc, with the center of the arc perfectly describing the pendulum's "average" position. Investor psychology and the emotional pendulum swing from excitement to depression, from celebrating good news to worrying about bad news, from overvaluation to undervaluation. This swinging from one extreme to another is the most certain feature of the investment world. Investor psychology swings like a pendulum, often moving to extremes, either one extreme or the other, rarely staying at the center point, rarely achieving the happy, balanced state we culturally associate with moderation. The pendulum swings continuously, moving to one extreme or the other; next, it swings to an extreme, and then the pend, beginning to reverse, turns back toward the center point. The force that pushes the pendulum back to the center point is the reversal of kinetic energy. It can also be said that the pendulum often swings back to the center point or the average value, but like most cycles, the pendulum often overshoots, passing the center point and continuing to swing to the opposite extreme from where it started.

People are unreliable, not objective, and not rational. Our emotions are easily influenced. Few people can consistently remain calm and not be trapped by emotions. Because of this, only a few investors can set benchmarks, define the center point, and remain in balance between greed and fear, no matter how the future develops, maintaining balance, neither too greedy nor too fearful. In contrast, most investors swing greatly between greed and fear. When they are emotionally optimistic, they become overly greedy; when they are emotionally pessimistic, they become overly fearful. Most investors' psychology and emotions swing to the wrong positions at the wrong times. Positive events lead to excessive market excitement, causing them to rush in and push prices to overlevels, at which point most investors become even greedier and want to chase higher prices. Conversely, negative events lead to excessive market pessimism, causing them to rush out and push prices to overlevels, at which point most investors become even more fearful and want to sell more.

Understand your own psychology and emotions, and objectively view yourself and the market. Strive to cultivate yourself to become mature, rational, skilled in analysis, objective, and not influenced by emotions. Strive to maintain an appropriate balance between fear and greed. Strive to resist external influences and maintain emotional balance, taking rational actions, seeing both favorable and unfavorable events, and remaining calm and objective when considering these events, without emotional bias in your analysis.

**Review and Summary**

Public sentiment also swings like a pendulum, moving between greed and fear, optimism and pessimism, belief and doubt, risk tolerance and risk aversion, forming cycles. When optimistic, investors are willing to take on more risk because they believe there will be more returns; when pessimistic, investors are unwilling to take on any risk. The author believes that the emotional cycle determines investment returns. Changes in the emotional cycle lead many investors to make two types of mistakes, which we can call "errors" and "misses." An "error" is buying the wrong assets at the wrong time, resulting in direct losses; a "miss" is not buying the right assets at the right time, meaning missing out on the returns you should have earned. We will discuss these separately. An "error" is reflected in ignoring risk and participating incorrectly. In financial markets, the higher your risk tolerance, the more business you can get. The 2008 subprime crisis is a case in point. "Subprime" refers to subprime mortgages, which are loans issued by financial institutions to borrowers with weaker repayment abilities, some of whom even have no jobs. Many people who couldn't afford to buy houses ended up buying them. However, once the market changed and interest rates rose slightly, these people couldn't afford their mortgage payments. Financial institutions ignored this risk, and long-established investment banks like Lehman Brothers, due to their disregard for risk, over-participated in the subprime market and collapsed overnight when the crisis broke out. A "miss" is reflected in extreme risk aversion and the abandonment of opportunities. Opportunities often appear when the market is extremely pessimistic. During the subprime crisis, some investment products not significantly affected also plummeted. Howard Marks calculated that investing now could yield an annualized return of over 20% with almost no risk of loss. He went to investors to raise funds, although he had already explained the calculation results in detail, the investors still doubted it, asking six times, "What if things get worse?" Howard Marks' answer was that the worst-case scenario would be a return to the 1929 Great Depression, but the investment would still not lose money. However, the investors still hesitated to commit. In the end, the author couldn't convince them, so he used his own money to invest in the fund, which turned out to be his most profitable investment. Influenced by the emotional cycle, most investors often panic and buy at the peak of a bull market, paying a high price; at the bottom of a bear market, they fear losses and panic-sell quality assets. The experts, however, can resist external influences, maintain emotional balance, and act against the crowd.
