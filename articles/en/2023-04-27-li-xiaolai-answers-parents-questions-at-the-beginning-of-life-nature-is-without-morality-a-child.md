Today, we begin with this topic: **"At the beginning of life, human nature is good."**
This line from *San Zi Jing* (The Three Character Classic) may be the most fundamental belief for many educators, and also a deeply rooted idea for many parents when educating their children. However, for a long time, there has been debate over whether **"At the beginning of life, human nature is good"** or **"At the beginning of life, human nature is evil."** It has often been a topic in university debate contests. But as time has passed, the field of psychology has made significant progress, no longer the same as it was 20 or 30 years ago, and neuroscience has also been developing continuously. What is the result of this? Our understanding of the human brain has changed dramatically from what it was 30 years ago. Therefore, today we have a reason to re-examine the direct answer to the question of "At the beginning of life, human nature is good/evil." **Every human infant comes into the world without a clear sense of good or evil; rather, it should be described as "nature is without morality."** This is based on two reasons. First, while people believe that human nature is good at birth, they also believe that children are like a blank slate when they come into the world. You paint what you want on it, and it becomes what you want. Isn't that contradictory? In reality, children do resemble a blank slate when they first arrive, but the human brain doesn't fully develop until around the age of three. Before that, only one function is fully developed from birth and remains throughout life. This function is called **"trial and error."** Through trial and error, they make mistakes and then correct them. For example, when we are passionately discussing whether children should be physically punished, nature has already done that for us. Think about it: when a child is learning to walk, they walk and suddenly "Duang" fall down. Does it hurt? Yes, it does. That's nature punishing them, right? Then what do they learn? They learn that this way of walking might lead to falling. So after they get up, they try to adjust, to avoid the action that caused the fall and to regain balance. They fall several times, and through that, they avoid many incorrect postures. Eventually, although their walking posture isn't perfect and they may wobble, they can walk normally and no longer fall. This is the trial and error mechanism at work. Therefore, when human infants come into the world, they don't have good or evil. For them, the only thing is trial and error, satisfaction and dissatisfaction, pain and no pain. They learn through constant trial and error, through a lot of trial and error, from morning to night, and through this process, they gradually acquire good and bad, and eventually, good and evil. So children initially don't understand good and evil. They are blank, but they are a self-evolving operating system because they have a very important and powerful mechanism called trial and error. Therefore, we cannot assume that children are inherently good, nor can we assume they are inherently evil. **Good and evil are very complex concepts; they are not only complex, but they may require us to learn and iterate repeatedly, with contradictions, for a lifetime.** Therefore, we cannot use our own ideas to understand children's behavior, nor can we mistakenly assume that what we consider good will never be proven to be evil, nor can we assume that what we consider evil might become good in the future. There are too many examples of this, so I'll take a recent incident to analyze it. Recently, a video has been widely shared online. A child is standing by the roadside, and another child walks up behind and pushes the child standing by the road, causing the child to fall down. The girl falls to the ground and cries in pain. Then the camera doesn't cut away, and another adult walks in from the camera's perspective. This adult is likely the mother of the girl who was pushed down. The adult walks up behind and pushes the child who pushed the girl, causing the child to fall down as well. The adult also cries loudly, and the video ends. This video, I believe, has been seen by many people. The comments below are all: "Well done! That's a good move! This bad kid should be punished!" After watching it, I felt a mix of emotions. First, I guess most of the commenters may not be parents, or they may be parents of only one child, so they didn't realize that their observations and judgments were wrong. In our family, if you have more than one child, you will definitely see two children fighting. I've seen this same behavior pattern in our children many times. For example, one child is standing in front, and another child suddenly runs up behind and pushes him. The child falls down, and the child in front cries out in pain. The child who pushed him laughs. Wait a minute. From an adult's perspective, we might think that the child who pushed is really bad. You push someone from behind, which is dangerous, and the person hurts, and you still laugh. That's really bad, isn't it? Actually, I want to tell you it's not like that. The child doesn't know he is doing something bad. He isn't trying to be bad. He just wants to play. He finds it fun. **This is a child who hasn't learned how to make friends, hasn't learned how to avoid danger, hasn't learned to think from others' perspectives, and has many other things to learn, resulting in a natural behavior pattern.** So my view is different from what most people think, and it's also different from what the commenters think. I don't see a bad kid; I see a pitiful child and two pitiful parents. The child in the video looks quite old, maybe four or five years old, and he still thinks that pushing is a way to play, and he hasn't realized that it's a dangerous action, and no adult has reminded him that he shouldn't do this. Theoretically, if there's an opportunity, such as when several children grow up together, or if they are twins, or if they have siblings, this behavior pattern could be observed before the age of three. Once this behavior pattern is observed, in the eyes of adults who understand psychology and the growth process of children, it's a behavior pattern that needs to be corrected. Then, they take measures to correct it, and the child will no longer do it. Earlier, we mentioned the boy who pushed the girl in the video. If he's already five or six years old, what does that mean? **His parents never realized he had this behavior pattern, and of course, they never corrected it, right?** We can also understand the mother who pushed the child in the video, right? But to be honest, if this mother understood the behavior patterns of human infants like I do, would she do that? Even though she was very worried about her child, I don't have any advice or judgment, but through this example, please understand that children are like this. In their world, their understanding of good and evil is very different from ours. **The good and evil we understand today are things we learn gradually, not something we are born with.** Some people argue that human nature is evil. Why? Because they believe children are born with a destructive impulse. Actually, it's not that they destroy for the sake of destruction. They just try, they want to know if it works, they want to know if it's good, they want to know if it's rewarding. Sometimes they make destructive actions, but if we can tell them that doing so is not worth it, that it's bad, they won't do it. Compared to the naive idea that human nature is good at birth, a more advanced understanding is what Thomas Sowell said:

> Every new generation is a barbarian invasion of civilization. We must educate them before it's too late to protect our civilization.

This statement is more advanced than the previous one because it is more realistic. The previous idea, that "human nature is good at birth," is, in a sense, just the wishful thinking of humans or adults. The latter is more realistic and accurate. Therefore, educating the next generation or human infants is extremely important. Of course, there are still many people who believe that love can educate everything. We also hope that's true. But more realistic people (like me) know clearly that love is necessary, it may be essential, but it is absolutely not a sufficient condition. What I want to say is that there is a more advanced understanding than Thomas Sowell's: **a child's character is acquired through trial and error, and he acquires important qualities through repeated trials.** We often say "human nature is good at birth," which is a very naive statement. Children may naturally have some things that seem bad to us. But we know it's acquired, and we know that initially, they are not intentional. Therefore, we can design many methods to help them gradually understand that dangerous and destructive actions are bad, and that safe and friendly actions are better. This requires a lot of patience. Of course, through this example, we can deeply understand that **the process of educating children can be understood as their own evolutionary process, and it has only one method, which is trial and error.** In the early stages, as he can speak normally and listen to others' reasoning, his understanding of good and evil will gradually improve. Through this trial and error, he will understand that if he wants this response next time, he just needs to cry again. Therefore, the mother's action is actually an unconscious mistake. There are many solutions to this, and you don't need to ask me because infant care books should already be very comprehensive in this area.
