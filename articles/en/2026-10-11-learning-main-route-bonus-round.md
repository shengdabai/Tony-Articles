# Give Learning a Main Route and a Bonus Round

> Publication date: 2026-10-11 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-11-学习要有主路，也要有奖励区.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-11-learning-main-route-bonus-round.md)

---

We tend to treat “hard” as a single category. When we cannot learn something, we call it too hard. When we get bored, we call it too easy. Yet within the same subject, some difficulties are part of the road everyone must travel; others are side paths worth taking by choice. I propose dividing a learning task into a main route and a bonus round. The main route practices a skill you can use on your own; the bonus round lets curiosity take you further. This is a proposed way to arrange learning, not an outcome established by the studies below. When AI can help produce an impressive piece of work, drawing that boundary becomes a human responsibility.

## What belongs on the main route?

A breakdown of a word game described a design choice: obscure words counted as bonuses. Finding one earned a reward; missing it did not prevent a player from completing the puzzle. The choice raises a question about where to draw the line. Require every obscure word, and a player who has learned the basic game can still get stuck on a handful of unfamiliar terms. Make every word optional, and the player might swipe a few times and mistake that for mastery. Someone has to decide what counts as completion and what counts as exploration.

Learning has the same boundary. By a “main route,” I do not mean an exam score or a certificate. I mean an observable test: if the next task is similar but new, and the hints are briefly put away, can you perform the essential actions? The bonus round holds finer distinctions, unusual cases, and challenges that appeal to particular interests. It may be demanding. It should not masquerade as the entry fee for everyone. Separating the two tells a beginner what they can already do without pushing a curious learner out at an artificially low finish line.

Suppose you are learning to write an email explaining that a piece of work will be late. This is a hypothetical example, and I will use it throughout. The main route is concrete: state what remains unfinished, what you can provide next, and what decision you need from the recipient. Check the dates and facts yourself. Make promises you can keep. Finding ten elegant subject lines, comparing subtle shades of tone, or studying email customs across industries can go in the bonus round. Those skills can matter, but they are not prerequisites for sending a first responsible message.

Here is a practical way to draw the line. If you omit this step, will the real task fail, or will someone else carry a risk you should have handled? If so, it belongs on the main route. If the task can still succeed and the step mainly adds finesse, speed, or personal style, it is a candidate for the bonus round. How rare a piece of knowledge is does not determine its place. The task, its risks, and its reader do. A change in use can move the same detail across the line, because the necessary checks change with the recipient and the consequences.

This distinction also lets someone complete a whole piece of work early. In our hypothetical example, writing an email you have checked and could send can reveal what to improve next. Reading a menu of “advanced writing techniques” still leaves you to apply them to the email. Finishing is not the end of learning. It gives the next improvement somewhere to stand. Without that step, the bonus round becomes an exquisite menu. You read it all; dinner never gets made.

## AI can deliver the work while hiding the route

[Noy and Zhang ran an experiment](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf) in which 444 college educated professionals completed short writing tasks related to their occupations. The researchers randomly assigned participants to two groups: one was offered access to ChatGPT, while the other was the control group, which was not assigned to use ChatGPT. The group offered access took 37% less time on average than the control group, and evaluators rated its writing more highly. These were bounded writing assignments. The study did not measure long term performance in a real job, much less whether participants later learned to write independently. That distinction matters in education: a better piece of work and a more capable writer are different report cards.

The same study found that 68% of participants assigned access to the tool reported submitting its initial output without editing it. For a person trying to finish a short assignment, that may be a sensible choice. The experiment did not label those people lazy. But if the goal is to practice judgment, you need to know which steps the tool crossed for you. Return to the delay email. AI might produce fluent prose without checking the date. Its tone might be considerate while the promise exceeds what you can deliver. The text looks complete; essential actions on the main route remain undone.

That is why I would not rank learners by whether they used AI. I would ask whether they can identify the decisions in the final message for which they are responsible. Which facts did you verify? Which promise did you remove? Which request does the recipient need in order to act? None of this requires typing every word again. It places a boundary around the tool's help. You can ask AI for a draft and still know what you are about to send.

[Ericsson and Harwell's review of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full) offers another point of reference. They explain that the original term referred to training tailored to a learner's present performance by a qualified teacher. The teacher sets a clear goal, provides feedback the learner can use, and helps the learner repeat and refine the attempt. Merely spending time on related activity is insufficient. The authors are participants in a scholarly dispute about how practice should be defined. Their account does not validate the proposed main route, but it suggests a useful question: what exact ability are you practicing, and what should you change next?

This also explains why loading every hard thing onto the main route need not make a course more rigorous. If a learner cannot yet check the date, the facts, and the request in an email, making them compare dozens of tones first scatters the feedback. They can work all evening without knowing which action improved. At the other extreme, asking AI for a usable email can make “I can send this” feel like “I can judge this.” A good main route neither piles up complexity until nobody can move nor quietly removes the learner's essential actions.

## The bonus round can be harder

“Bonus round” may sound like lowering standards. It can mean practicing more seriously. Once you can handle facts, promises, and requests, try writing a version of the email that would invite a misunderstanding, then find exactly where that misunderstanding begins. Or ask AI for drafts in different tones and explain which fits the recipient. The challenge lies in explaining your choice. It does not come from adding more buttons to a screen.

In [three experiments published in 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9902256/), Wong studied a total of 120 undergraduates reading scientific explanatory texts. In one condition, learners intentionally wrote plausible conceptual errors of their own and then corrected them. Comparison conditions included producing correct alternatives or finding and correcting somebody else's errors. On the low stakes tasks used in the studies, creating and correcting one's own errors led to better retention and better application of concepts in different domains. That latter ability is called transfer: you learn an idea in one place and can use it when the problem changes. The transfer questions in the study pointed learners toward the relevant concept, so the work did not show that they would spontaneously notice every opportunity to transfer it in life.

What catches my eye is that seeing an error and making and correcting your own error did not have the same effect. The result cannot prescribe how to write every work email. It does suggest a learning exercise worth trying: make a plausible mistake in a safe draft, then explain why it is mistaken. In our example, you might write an unconfirmed delivery time as a firm promise, then replace it with wording you can verify. You are practicing the boundary of a responsible decision. If AI supplies a complete list of mistakes and you merely nod along, you may gain familiarity with an answer without practicing the decision yourself.

Here is the relationship between the two paths. The main route preserves actions you must be able to perform. The bonus round lets you change the conditions on purpose and see whether those actions still hold. One offers a usable starting point; the other offers room to keep trying. Learners need not pick the same extra exercise. One may explore tone; another may explore the order of information. If each can say what they changed and why, those side paths deepen the skill.

Error exercises must stay inside practice. An unverified promise should not be sent to a real recipient. Nor should a high stakes task make a critical check optional for the sake of learning through mistakes. Wong's participants were undergraduates studying texts in low stakes settings. Some rare mistakes in actual work are costly enough that the relevant check belongs on the main route, however obscure it seems. The bonus round is for exploration that can be revised. It is not a way to hand the consequences to somebody else.

## Who decides that you have finished?

Someone might object that a shorter main route and optional hard problems will produce people who can pass a checkpoint but cannot do the work. That concern is fair, especially when the tool awards its own pass mark. AI can write for you and praise what it wrote. Put those actions together and smoothness starts to feel like reliability. My answer is to place the checkpoint closer to actual use, rather than keep lengthening the route. Can the recipient of the delay email understand the status, the risk, and the next action? Can you verify its facts and stand behind its promise?

In [a 2013 account of early startups](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes how Stripe's founders would help a willing new user install the product on the spot instead of merely sending a signup link. This is his firsthand observation about early companies, not an experiment proving that personal installation always produces growth. I use it here to explain the distance to feedback: putting something in the hands of a real user may show the exact step that stops them. This is an analogy, not a research finding about email writing. “It seems clear to me” still needs a test: can the recipient make a decision from the message?

An actual reader is not the sole judge, either. A person may be busy and fail to reply, or understand the message and choose not to answer. One silence does not prove the writing failed. When you have no recipient yet, ask someone who knows the task to check its key facts, promise, and request. At a minimum, reread it the next day and see whether you can state the next action without guessing. These checks differ in strength, but all are closer to the task than a flattering score generated by AI. The finish line needs some contact with facts outside the draft or it becomes a paper victory.

The harder problem is that beginners often do not know which errors are most expensive. An experienced person should often help draw the main route first. Then real feedback can redraw it. A detail you treated as a bonus may turn out to be the explanation readers most need. A supposedly mandatory technique may prove irrelevant to the task. The route can change, but keep the reason for moving each part. Otherwise every new feature and every new tip becomes another requirement, until the main route turns back into a maze nobody can finish.

## Draw a route you can walk today

Take something you want to learn and resist writing “master this subject” as your goal. Reduce it to a small deliverable. For the email, the target is a message that lets its recipient decide what to do next. In another field, ask who will use the work, what they will use it for, and which facts must be right. “Understand the tutorial” does not yet draw a main route.

Next, write down the minimum actions required to finish. For each one, ask whether removing it would cause failure or pass an avoidable risk to somebody else. Keep those actions on the main route. For the email, that means checking progress, making the request clear, confirming the date, and cutting any promise you cannot keep. Put refinements in appearance, speed, and personal style in the bonus round for now. You can move them if real feedback changes their importance.

Then make one version of the work. Let AI help draft and edit sentences, but identify the basis for every important decision yourself. If you cannot explain a decision, “it sounds natural” is a weak reason to approve it. After completing the main route, choose one extra challenge you actually want to try: rearrange the information, try another tone, or make and correct a deliberate error in a draft. Change one thing at a time so you know what the response addresses.

Finally, put the work before somebody who would use it, or somebody who can check the task's standard. Record one specific piece of feedback. Use it to adjust the main route or the bonus round. Which thing you treated as decoration affected understanding? Which laborious refinement changed nothing the reader could do? If you have no feedback, call it “I completed a practice attempt.” Do not rush to award yourself a certificate saying “I have learned it.”

This approach will not make everyone progress at the same speed or make every side path worthwhile. It can help avoid two kinds of waste: turning every interesting difficulty into an entrance barrier, and letting AI perform all the entrance skills on your behalf. Walk the main route far enough to use the skill. Leave a bonus round for the person who wants to keep exploring. In the thing you are learning today, which step must you do with your own hands? Circle that step and take it slowly. Do not let a polished result answer in your place.
