# Finding a Sense of Certainty

124th Original Piece

Xu Zhiyuan, once a financial media editor and columnist for the Chinese edition of *Financial Times*, is now one of the founders of the Beijing cultural landmark, *Single Direction Space*, and the main creator of the phenomenon-level interview video series *Thirteen Questions*. He has authored several books, including *Those Sad Young People*, *The Stranger in the Motherland*, and *Youth Reformer: Liang Qichao (1873–1898)*.

Is the restlessness and superficiality of this era really a disease? Is there a cure?

Everything is about speed, money above all—does this represent a fixed value orientation, or does it contain the potential for positive fractal growth?

Angry, depressed modern people, how can we escape the anxiety of the times and live out authentic creativity?

This era of rapid change gives us more questions than answers. Whether we are aware of it or not, everyone is answering these questions or adding their own commentary through observation, thinking, and action.

Xu Zhiyuan talked about his understanding of the times, maintaining skepticism and anger. Young Xu was also full of anger toward the era. He urged maintaining one’s passion. I’m currently in this period, which made me think of the content I was recently studying in Liuda’s logic course. We started by blindly believing, then moved to a binary independence, began criticizing everything around us, thinking in black-and-white terms—sort of like acontrarian troll (a person who insists on being right). But Xu’s view is different: we need independent thinking, which is the third stage. We should accept the conflict of different viewpoints and externalize our criticism. He believes the era has self-correcting properties, and we should trust it, waiting for its self-correction. Young people should follow the progress of the times, and we must remain angry, alert, and sensitive. Why? Because this is the source of creation. If you don’t seek growth and refuse to self-correct with the times, you’ll be left far behind. The business world is also constantly changing. Without constant vigilance and sensitivity to information and trends, what justification do we have for innovation and change?

Our inner world forms its own system, and there’s no need to tie it to something external. This sentence struck me deeply. Why do I feel anxious? Why do I have so many unrealistic thoughts? Because I’m always searching for a connection with the outside, believing that inner harmony is insufficient and that I need to link with the external world. But why should I force myself? Accept yourself, embrace yourself! Critical thinking should never target the outside or others—it should always be directed inward. Only self-directed criticism can lead to mutual understanding and new inspiration. We often judge others, easily creating friction. Essentially, we never reflect on ourselves but instead make hasty judgments about others. Such behavior is useless for personal growth and only leads to more complaints. Complaints are a disaster, or rather, they create one disaster after another. We have endless reasons to complain about others, yet we are overly confident about ourselves, lacking any real self-criticism.

This era has many of us trapped in anxiety, because various things cause anxiety—salary fluctuations, public transport conditions, personal learning, family relationships, and so on. There are countless things that can trigger anxiety at any moment. Upon reflection, it’s because we lack confidence. Xu said that from a historical perspective, history constantly self-corrects, and the overall trend is getting better. Think about stock market trends—short-term fluctuations are inevitable, but if you look further, the long-term trend is upward. The famous investment smile curve: many people can’t hold on during the downturn, even crashing at the bottom before the upswing begins. By the time the gains start to grow exponentially, two major cycles, even years or decades, have passed. The smiling people remain smiling. Facing all aspects of life, confidence is our medicine. Invest long-term, look to the future!

Bubble~ We might be living in a bubble era, where life appears colorful and rich. But if we’re not careful, it could all collapse with a *Bung* sound. How should we respond? Perhaps humanistic education can offer new insights. Many people don’t value humanistic education, like myself. In school, why did I choose science? I guess many of us are the same—no more need to study Chinese, memorizing too much is too troublesome, and I didn’t want to write anyway. In reality, we still did. Studying science is personal creation? Or is it still about memorizing a lot? Some might say logic is enough, and you can reason on your own. But think about it—where does logic come from? How do you think independently? Without a solid foundation in the humanities, your thinking might still fall into your own mental traps. Writing? That’s even more obvious. So how much have we lost? Now we’re just practicing, ha ha! Keep building your humanistic literacy, because only then can you gradually learn to live with yourself, which is so important!

How is our worldview formed? It probably comes from a cycle of building, destroying, rebuilding, and destroying again. When will we ever build a complete worldview? Perhaps we’re always in the process of building, and that’s fine. This is the process of cognitive upgrading and iteration. If it stops improving, maybe our lives will end! In this iterative process, we strive to discover the one thing we most desire and that fits us best. Only then can our creativity keep growing. With creativity, we can continuously build our worldview. Maybe we’ll never build that Tower of Babel, but how precious inner richness is to us!

Finally, Xu shared one thing: Passion! Entrepreneurs need passion, but it sounds easy, and it’s hard to achieve. What kind of things can ignite us? Those we believe we can accomplish, regardless of whether we actually do. At least, they give us the impulse to act. When did I find mine?

Recently, I took Li Xiaolai’s writing course, and I was deeply moved. Writing is something you can do if you want to—it has no barriers. More accurately, it’s just a physical labor. After listening, you might wonder, is it really that easy? Is it that simple? The answer is just one word: yes! And it must be. Is the difficulty of writing in rhetoric? In content? In word count? No. Writing is the expression of logic—clearing up your thoughts is all it takes. It’s two things: recording your perception and conveying it. Thinking from the author’s perspective about the reader, and from the reader’s perspective about the author, the goal is to clearly communicate perception. When you can express your perception clearly, that’s a good article, and it has value for others. The rest is just doing it. Listen to me finish—doesn’t writing seem less scary now? Good writers ignite readers’ passion for writing, not tell them how hard it is. Think about our middle school and college teachers—exams are like a mountain, so hard, and once you pass, you’re free. It’s terrifying! But exams are nothing compared to that. Once you pass, it’s the start, the real fight for lifelong learning!

Respect every fighter in this era, ignite your passion, and walk forward with determination!
