# Whose Job Is Your AI Doing?

> 发布日期:2026-11-10 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-10-AI%20替谁把事情做好.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-10-whose-job-is-your-ai-doing.md)

---

While building Viaweb, Paul Graham encountered merchants who did not want to use his store-building software but were happy to have the founders build their stores for them. [He later recalled](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html) that doing the work for merchants taught the team how using the software felt. I would judge an AI assistant’s loyalty to its user by a related test: when goals conflict, whose goal does it protect, and who bears the cost?

Being capable leaves a question open: capable on whose behalf? Software can build a store, while the merchant may simply want to get on with running a business. Learning to operate the software may be an additional expense for that person. The value of the same feature can look very different from different positions.

That distinction matters even more with AI. An assistant can explain its recommendations in a considerate voice and remove a great many options from our view. Fewer choices can feel like a relief. But did anything we wanted to preserve disappear with them?

## The payer and the user

A saved summary about AI shopping assistants prompted the question I want to pursue: a personal assistant might choose products for a buyer while receiving its funding from sellers. This creates a possible conflict of interest—a situation in which the action that earns the service money differs from the action that benefits its user. The user may never see the trade-off embedded in the recommendation.

Seller funding can, of course, be helpful. It may reduce what buyers pay for assistance and help suitable products get discovered. The problem arises when the goals diverge: continuing with an existing tool would suit the user better, while the service earns this particular revenue only by generating a new purchase. Helping someone choose well then becomes a different outcome from completing a transaction.

Suppose I want to learn to build a personal website and ask AI to help me choose a tool. My budget is limited, and I am willing to go slowly because I want to understand how pages are made. One tool can deliver a complete page quickly; another requires more hands-on adjustment but makes each step easier to observe. We do not need brand names, and we are making no assumptions about any actual referral agreements.

Now add one condition to this example: recommending the fast-delivery tool earns the recommending service more money. That does not automatically make it a bad recommendation. But the assistant should at least address an inconvenient question: are the operations it saves me precisely the things I wanted to learn?

Here is the difficulty. I may want speed, learning, and low cost at the same time. Those wishes may not all be compatible. An assistant can make the trade-offs visible and let me choose. If it quietly treats the outcome most likely to produce a sale as success, it has chosen the order of my priorities for me.

In the same essay, Graham discussed how payment can change a relationship: giving early customers extraordinary attention creates different expectations from selling that attention by the hour. This was startup advice drawn from experience in 2013, not a controlled comparison: such a comparison would put similar customers under different payment arrangements and compare the results, which the essay does not do. My takeaway is specific: we need to say what the money is buying.

Having the buyer pay does not settle everything either. Usage-based billing could give a service a reason to encourage more use; hourly billing could create different feelings about finishing sooner. These are possible incentives, not findings about a particular service. Payment provides clues. Behavior makes a conflict visible.

## A user’s goals need an order

Telling an assistant to help its user still leaves a lot unspecified. Which wish should it help with?

In [a 2016 interview](https://hbr.org/podcast/2016/12/the-jobs-to-be-done-theory-of-innovation), Clayton Christensen emphasized that the same customer may have different jobs to accomplish in different circumstances. Combining products around that person does not automatically create useful integration. This is a method for understanding needs; a particular need still has to be checked against the actual situation.

Return to the website example. If I need a page to display my work tomorrow, rapid delivery is valuable. If building the site is a learning project, keeping some operations in my own hands is valuable. I am still the user, and my budget might be unchanged, yet the recommendation should change.

This is one reservation I have about an all-purpose assistant. Knowing that I enjoy learning does not mean it should assign practice every time. Knowing that I value efficiency does not mean it should take over the entire process every time. Long-term preferences can help it understand me, while the purpose of this particular task still needs to be established.

A tape measure can tell you how wide a desk is. It cannot decide how much of the room the desk should occupy. More space for the desk leaves less space to move around. Measurement can expose the cost; the person living in the room still decides how to use it. The performance, price, and difficulty figures supplied by AI should serve that kind of choice.

A tape measure has no revenue target, while an AI service may have one behind it. Seeing the dimensions is therefore not enough. We also need to know which dimensions receive the most prominent placement and which costs are omitted. A ranking may look neutral, but its criteria already contain a choice.

For me, a useful brief should specify what I want to accomplish now, what I am willing to spend or do for it, and what must not be casually sacrificed. The order matters. A spending limit, a learning objective, and a preference for speed lead to different advice from a firm deadline with learning postponed until later.

That order can change. After seeing actual tools, I might discover that their maintenance demands exceed what I can manage and choose something simpler for now. When new information drives the change and I confirm it, that is ordinary decision-making. The assistant should make the change explicit so I know what I am giving up.

## A high score can conceal the conflict

Would adding a score solve the problem? It depends on what the score rewards.

In [a randomized experiment published in 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills), Anthropic recruited 52 software engineers, mostly junior developers, to learn Trio, a Python library unfamiliar to them. Here, randomized means participants were assigned by chance to use AI or work without it, so the researchers could compare the groups. After completing coding tasks, participants took a quiz covering areas including conceptual understanding and code reading.

The AI group averaged 50% on the quiz, compared with 67% for the hand-coding group—a difference of 17 percentage points. The difference in task completion speed was not statistically significant: the study did not obtain sufficient evidence to establish a speed improvement in this setting. It measured understanding shortly after particular tasks and did not determine what would happen to skills over the long term.

What makes me cautious here is the definition of success. An assistant rewarded only for producing a working page might perform beautifully while failing to meet the needs of someone who wanted to learn to build one. Displaying its delivery score more prominently will not make learning part of its objective.

I would keep the inference narrow: when a user wants to acquire an ability through the process, judging assistance only by the finished product may leave an important cost uncounted. The experiment did not test shopping recommendations or commission payments. Its evidence concerns the possible separation between completing immediate work and mastering the relevant knowledge.

For an ordinary user, that distinction is more useful than another leaderboard. When an assistant recommends a tool, I need it to say what the ranking preserves and what it gives up. Fast completion deserves credit. A compressed learning process also needs to be accounted for. A single attractive score cannot answer for both.

This does not require choosing the most laborious option. It would be exhausting if every AI request came with a lecture about the virtues of struggle. Preserving the user’s right to choose includes allowing convenience. An assistant should not introduce an unauthorized goal of educating its user just to demonstrate how responsible it is.

## Less effort can also support learning

One response would be to avoid the complexity: choose an assistant funded only by users and ask it to do a little less on your behalf. That sounds straightforward, but it binds together two different questions.

Who pays requires examining the service arrangement. Whether less assistance would help me depends on the task. A user-funded service can make unsuitable recommendations; a seller-funded service can provide good help when the two sides’ goals coincide. The payer’s identity is not the end of the assessment.

More fundamentally, learning more with less effort is possible. [Research on cover-letter writing](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02880v4) by Benjamin Lira and colleagues provides a counterexample. In one experiment with 2,003 participants, people first learned writing principles. Some then used an AI tool, some only viewed an AI-revised example, and others practiced on their own.

Those who only viewed the example could not edit it. When subsequently revising a new cover letter independently, they outperformed the practice-alone group. The researchers detected no significant difference between their performance and that of participants who had used the AI tool. The pattern persisted among the subset of participants invited back who completed a follow-up test the next day.

The conditions matter: participants received writing principles and feedback. The main writing measure used model ratings, supplemented with human comparisons of which letters seemed more likely to secure interviews. These were not actual hiring outcomes, and follow-up extended only to the next day. The results do not guarantee that all skills can be learned effortlessly.

They do, however, block an excessive conclusion: leaving the operations to the person is not always more helpful than providing a suitable example. In the website scenario, a complete example whose page structure is easy to inspect might be useful for learning too. The amount of automation alone cannot establish whose interests an assistant serves.

I would prefer to ask it to explain why the operations it removes are safe to remove for this purpose. Good advice should let me examine that reason and retain some practice if I choose. Whether the reason holds still requires trying the tool. An explanation that sounds right and an experience that turns out to fit are different things.

The funding arrangement deserves attention, and so does the help actually provided. Neither substitutes for the other. Our standard should allow a service to earn money by helping well and a user to grow more capable while exerting less effort. When those outcomes diverge, the user should be able to see that and choose.

## Keep a record of the trade-off

Here is a concrete question I want to keep for myself: which options has this assistant excluded, and might any of them fit my purpose better while making a transaction less likely?

Answering does not require investigating every commercial contract behind an entire platform. The trade-off record below is a check I propose; the research cited here has not tested whether it works. Particularly when spending, learning, and long-term use are bound together, I would write down the purpose first, then check whether the recommendation follows it.

1. Write down the purpose and its priorities. In the website example, specify whether the aim is to display work quickly or to acquire basic operating skills, and set a spending limit. Include the parts you want to do yourself so the assistant has concrete conditions to respect.
2. Ask it to retain different paths. Compare continuing with existing tools, making no purchase for now, and buying a tool on the same basis. Each path should identify the goal it serves and the burden it adds. When payment or partnership information cannot be established, record it as unknown.
3. Change one condition that actually affects the purpose and examine the recommendation again. For example, replace urgency with a willingness to learn more slowly. Ask which rankings change and why. Then try a small portion of the work to check whether the promised opportunities to learn actually exist.
4. Before deciding, record the cost you accept. Choosing rapid delivery means acknowledging that practice was not the priority this time. Choosing more hands-on work means accepting additional time. Confirm this record yourself, so that later you remember what you gave up along with how persuasive the recommendation sounded.

This is an initial check, not proof that a service has no undisclosed commercial interests. An assistant may know which explanations appeal to you and supply pleasant reasons accordingly. For a particular tool, its actual offering still matters. Leaving missing commercial information unknown is more reliable than supplying a satisfying story.

I would not turn every small purchase into an audit either. When a decision is cheap and easy to reverse, the record can be light. Choices involving a long commitment or a change in how I learn deserve more examination. Checking consumes attention, and that cost belongs to the user too.

For people building AI tools, the question goes a step further. Can we openly acknowledge that some outcomes suitable for users will bring us no revenue from this transaction? Can those outcomes remain in the recommendations? This is a requirement I would use to assess a product, rather than an already validated business model.

AI makes advice easier to obtain. It does not automatically make clear on whose behalf a trade-off is being made. I can delegate the task while still wanting to confirm the order of my own wishes.

I want the final say on that.
