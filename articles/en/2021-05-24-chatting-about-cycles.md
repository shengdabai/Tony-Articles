What makes Howard Marks' concept of cycles different from others? He standardizes the cycle, likening it to a pendulum that always swings toward balance. The closer it gets to the lowest point, the faster it moves. The highest and lowest points are fleeting moments, and the cycle repeats endlessly. One clear point from this book is that to invest successfully, you must study cycles. Winning, or investing successfully, is extremely difficult. What we see are only the highlights, but in reality, it's an extremely rare few. Studying cycles doesn't guarantee investment success, but it increases the chances of winning. With the same amount of capital, the allocation based on different cycle positions leads to different results.

The three major laws of cycles: First, cycles are curved—either naturally curved or bent, but never straight. Second, history does not repeat the same details, but it does repeat similar processes. Third, avoid the middle and go for the extremes. Events in a cycle should not be seen as one after another, but rather as one event triggering the next, with clear causal relationships between them.

The largest cycle is the economic cycle. Among these, government countercyclical regulation and corporate earnings cycles have significant impacts. Two particularly interesting ones are the investor sentiment pendulum. This is fascinating, and perhaps that's why women might be more suited for investing. The specific investment products we can profit from include the credit cycle, real estate cycle, and non-performing loan cycle. When we feel the economic cycle, it's usually too late. If you can truly understand how a 6% or 8% GDP growth affects your individual life, you've truly grasped the economic cycle. But I think 99% of people cannot grasp the essence of the economic cycle. The cycle that has the most impact on investing is the credit cycle, which is relatively perceptible and usable. Howard Marks' greatest success was his investment in junk bonds, which can be considered a classic example of countercyclical investing. Most people tend to follow the crowd, and the courage and skill to act against the trend are often lacking.

This requires solid analysis, confidence in your own thoughts and actions, and a strong inner resolve. Many people fail in investing not because of their knowledge, but due to their mindset. The description of investor psychology and emotions is also one of the book's highlights. Not all cycles can guide our investments. Valuable cycles include the real estate cycle, (stock) market cycle, credit cycle, and up to the non-performing debt cycle. Other cycles are not relevant to our investments. The market cycle, particularly the "three stages of a bull market," is worth paying close attention to. A few people call it insight, most call it fundamentals, and everyone else just calls it forever. It's rare and valuable that the book provides a standard for judging the market cycle, accompanied by a table, which is quite practical!

This section is worth savoring slowly. An investor with over 50 years of experience has a completely different mindset from someone who has invested for only five months. The most valuable part of this book is that Howard Marks is very humble, telling us that every super-investor isn't necessarily someone who is particularly outstanding, but rather someone who just happened to be at the best moment of the cycle, either the peak or the trough. This is purely random and absolutely accidental. The investment market fully reflects the psychology and emotions of investors. Avoid buying when the market is high and selling when it's low—this is extremely difficult. This is where investment experts show their skills. If you understand cycles, you should clearly realize that one extreme leads to the opposite extreme.

"Smart people act first, foolish people act last." At its core, investing is about two things: asset selection and positioning within the cycle. "Our emotions and feelings often lead us to buy assets when the market is high and sell them when it's low. Therefore, emotions are the enemy of investing, and we must control our emotions well." Many justifications for investing in real estate are simple and easy to accept: land is limited, emphasizing scarcity; houses can be used for living, emphasizing sentiment; buying a house can hedge against inflation, telling a story of wealth. However, no matter how you justify it, they cannot guarantee that your investment won't lose money. At least, losing money won't affect their lives. If the cost price of the investment is too high, then no justification matters.

Exceptional investing doesn't come from the quality of the assets you buy, but from the value-for-money of the assets—good quality at a low price. It sounds like the "Little Red Book" philosophy. Buying an Apple isn't the mark of an exceptional investor; exceptional investors buy undervalued stocks. "Catch the falling knife." "This time is different" is the most dangerous phrase in the investment world!

Building a good sense of cycles can help you think like a smart person and increase the chances of making good decisions. The three key points for building a sense of cycles are: First, understanding cycles. Natural laws and human behavior are the two main driving forces behind cycles, leading to the three most important characteristics of cycles: first, mean reversion, where the cycle swings around a central point like a pendulum; second, overcorrection, where it always moves from one extreme to another; and third, randomness, which makes it hard to predict. History is always similar, but details never repeat. Second, analyzing cycles. The three most common and important cycles are the credit cycle, the sentiment cycle, and the success cycle. Various cycles intersect and influence each other, forming a highly uncertain complex system. Facing the risks of "mistakes" and "missed opportunities," experts strive to act against the crowd. Third, dealing with cycles. There are three key points: first, don't predict the future, only judge the present. Second, identify extremes and use them. Third, use both quantitative and qualitative methods to determine the position of the extremes. When the crowd starts saying "This time is different," a person with a good sense of cycles will believe: This time is also different, and the cycle continues forever.
