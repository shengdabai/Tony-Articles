Have you ever encountered such a situation?

You share with someone, as thoroughly as you can, giving them all the content you've prepared, yet you don't get a positive response. The other person just talks to themselves.

After listening to someone's share, it seems very reasonable, but after a while, you can't remember much, only a few scattered words.

Or perhaps you strongly disagree with someone's share, so you spend more time thinking about your own views and trying to convince them.

All these situations, of course, are just a few possibilities. If any of them happens, what is the reason behind it?

As June exam season approaches, many families have children, especially those about to take the middle school or college entrance exams. Naturally, the conversations turn to exams, and even more so, to schools.

Even parents who don't care about their children's studies are now trying to find out various information, striving to find the best schools for their kids.

We don't need to discuss whether these efforts are effective. What's clear is that the focus on scores, grades, and rankings has become overwhelming.

With the arrival of exam preparation courses, career guidance, and various secret guides, everything seems to come at once. It's truly as if what we want is exactly what comes to us. We understand the hearts of parents and students well.

Moreover, a flood of information comes in, especially expert advice and friends' opinions. Among these, the most memorable is the emphasis on key schools, local key high schools, and 985/211 universities (even though the label classification for universities is no longer applicable, and the unified term is "double first-class," the two numbers still carry heavy weight in people's minds).

Here, I want to raise a question: Is going to a top university really important?

When facing questions of importance, we can refer to Li Xiaolai's three core questions for judgment:

Is X the reason for Y?

If so, is X the only reason for Y?

If not, what is more important, and what is the most important?

Let's think about the issue of key schools.

Why do we want to go to key schools? Is it because children can receive better education, or because they can be exposed to a better environment, or because they can have better jobs and higher income? Our answers vary, but one thing is certain: parents want the best for their children.

So the question is no longer whether going to a top university is important. Rather, when we want our children to become better, is going to a top university a factor? And even more so, is it the only factor? Are there more important factors?

After we ask these questions, we can already form an answer in our minds about whether top universities are really that important.

Don't rush. Once you have a preliminary idea, you'll naturally want to verify whether it's correct. How would you go about verifying it?

You might refer to books, the internet, or expert advice. You might even suddenly recall a phrase: "So-and-so said, you must go to a top university."

Look, the problem arises. Who said it, what exactly was said, and how was it said? Do you really remember every detail?

Most of the time, we look for various pieces of evidence to support our own ideas, regardless of the facts or the logic.

Don't worry, there are many people like you. For example, me, who is communicating with you now. This phenomenon is called the backfire effect.

The backfire effect refers to: when a mistaken piece of information is corrected, if the correction contradicts a person's original belief, it can actually deepen their trust in the original mistaken information.

The backfire effect intensifies our subjective bias. This is because it leads people to overfocus on certain information or events, thus ignoring or rejecting other information or events.

For example, on social media, if we see a news story that receives a lot of attention and generates a lot of comments and discussions, we might overfocus on that story and ignore other important news and events. The backfire effect can also trigger confirmation bias, making us stubborn and unwilling to listen to others' opinions or ideas, further intensifying our subjective bias.

Another example is if we are told from a young age that a certain idea or belief is correct and others are wrong, we might become very stubborn and unwilling to accept any challenge or doubt to our beliefs. This can lead to an intensified subjective bias, putting us in a dangerous situation where we only accept one idea.

The backfire effect intensifies our subjective bias, making us more rigid or stubborn. To avoid this, we need to be aware of this phenomenon and try to maintain an open-minded approach, while continuously engaging with and understanding different perspectives and beliefs, expanding our understanding and learning.

Have you noticed that most of us only remember the conclusion, or even just a single point, and pay no attention to the details?

Why do we assume that a small part represents the whole? Have we really thought through this and considered how it might affect our future decisions?

One day, a man on the subway sees a little boy crying, and a man is sitting nearby, showing no reaction. The man seems to be deaf to the child's crying and the complaints from other passengers, still remaining silent.

Other passengers feel awkward, unsure whether to help or comfort the boy. Later, a female passenger notices something behind the man. On the back of his seat, there is a photo turned upside down, with the words: "We love you forever, Mom."

The woman immediately understands that this giant man isn't indifferent to the child, but is struggling with the pain of losing his wife. At this moment, she approaches him gently and asks about his feelings. He slowly tells her his heartache.

Passengers realize their actions can support this man and ease his burden. The woman gives him a smile, and other passengers also offer small gifts and warm wishes. The man is no longer alone; all of them are supporting him through this difficult time.

Eventually, the little boy calms down, and his tears stop. He doesn't speak, but in this emotional atmosphere, it seems as if all the passengers are together, sharing his pain and his courage to start anew.

This story is based on Shawn Achor, an American writer, speaker, and educator. His speeches and books focus on positive psychology, emotional intelligence, and the science of happiness. The story described is a real experience he shared in a TED talk.

Our partial understanding has caused many problems and influenced our decisions in countless ways. It's a bit scary to think about.

Take the conversation I had with my younger brother after an exam. When we analyzed the reasons for our poor performance, my brother used a lack of foundational knowledge as the basis for his analysis. I immediately had a defensive reaction, thinking it was an excuse for not working hard enough.

In fact, repetition is a crucial method in learning. In *Make It Stick: The Science of True Learning*, the book discusses the most effective methods for learning, drawing on extensive research from psychology and cognitive science. One core idea is that making the goal relevant to oneself is more important than effort alone.

In *Rich Dad, Poor Dad* (author: Robert Kiyosaki), the book also emphasizes the importance of making things relevant to oneself. When something is connected to us, our repeated exposure becomes more effective.

This book reminds us that building a connection with ourselves is more important than just working hard. The author argues that a person must find their own sense of purpose within themselves to better manage their finances.

Li Xiaolai's definition of effort is worth reflecting on: effort is simply the act of repeating something sufficiently in a short period of time.

I didn't listen patiently but made a subjective judgment, thinking I understood everything, while ignoring other factors, such as the child's physical condition during the exam, the lack of attention to classroom tests, spending more time on difficult problems, and the consideration of the review plan.

There is so much that children can learn. Who should be educated—the child or the parent? Adults should also be educated.

To avoid our partial understanding, I think there are some practices that can help us grow and improve.

Recognize our biases: realizing the existence of bias is the first step in breaking it. Understanding ourselves and our beliefs more deeply can also help us identify our biases and taboos.

Exposure to diversity: interacting with people from different cultures, backgrounds, beliefs, and opinions can break down our biases and social norms. Diversity not only brings new insights but also helps us better understand our own limitations and broaden our perspectives.

Learn to listen: actively listening and understanding different perspectives and opinions helps us see other viewpoints, thus breaking our own biases. Listening is also a crucial part of building connections with others, helping us better understand people and form deeper relationships.

Seek new experiences: by seeking new experiences and encountering new things, we can challenge and break our previous ways of thinking and biases. These experiences can help us better understand ourselves and maintain an open and flexible attitude toward others.

Self-reflection: carefully thinking about our past experiences and the origins of our biases can help us better understand ourselves and our biases. Considering problems from different angles can help us better recognize our limitations and better understand and accept others.

We also need to seek more information, try to learn more about the subject or issue from various sources and different perspectives.

Challenge our own views: after gathering and understanding more information, try to challenge our own views, consider others' perspectives, and think about how to change our opinions.

Stay neutral: in debates or arguments, try to maintain a neutral stance and carefully consider the pros and cons of different viewpoints.

Critical thinking: use critical thinking to analyze problems and consider possible biases or flaws, striving to view issues more objectively and comprehensively. But remember, it's not about criticism—it should be logical.
