# Cheap Answers Can Be Expensive to Check

> 发布日期:2026-10-21 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-21-答案便宜，验货可能很贵.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-21-price-of-checking-ai-answers.md)

---

In a writing experiment, people given access to ChatGPT took an average of 17 minutes to complete their second task. The control group took 27 minutes, and evaluators who did not know who had used AI rated the AI group's work more highly. I want those ten minutes back, too. But when I am responsible for the finished work, how much time will I spend checking a fast, cheap answer? My current view is that a service's quoted price is only part of what an AI answer costs. Verification, rework, and errors that escape detection belong on the same bill. Otherwise, a saving may simply appear somewhere else as work.

[Noy and Zhang's 2023 experiment](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf) assigned short professional writing tasks to 444 college-educated professionals. It measured immediate completion time and ratings of the resulting work. It did not compare providers or measure long-term learning. So my question here is not which AI company is strongest. It is how to calculate the price of an answer we can actually use.

## Choosing the cheaper option can be sensible

The case for choosing on price deserves to be made properly. Many pieces of work do not merit a solemn review of every sentence. Drafting a low-stakes email, arranging an already-reviewed meeting outline, or rephrasing an idea that may soon be discarded: speed has value in each case. Paying for the most expensive service and inspecting every word could erase the benefit of using the tool.

The writing experiment offers a stronger argument still. Among participants allowed to use ChatGPT, 68% reported submitting its first output without editing it. On average, the researchers also found no improvement in ratings from human editing over the raw AI output. For those short, self-contained tasks with relatively legible assessment criteria, reserving a great deal of human time for line editing did not necessarily produce a matching gain. Someone choosing the cheaper route may be using scarce attention quite reasonably.

There is another practical argument: if I can spot a mistake at a glance and simply ask again, why pay more? Fair enough. When a result can be withdrawn immediately, mistakes do not affect other people, and switching services is easy, paying for protection you will never use makes little sense. Demanding deep review for everything can become its own form of busywork.

But that calculation quietly assumes two things. First, the user knows what to check. Second, fixing a mistake will not interrupt the original job. The second assumption may fail: you may think you bought a ready-to-use answer and instead inherit a draft with no indication of where its weaknesses might be. The cheaper service may still win. The calculation has to include that work before anyone can say so.

## Where the saving goes

A clipped AI summary about buying access to an AI model through an intermediary suggests a question: when two services advertise the same model name, how should a buyer decide whether either suits the job? The summary suggested that price, speed, and availability need not move together. Those comparisons have not been checked against a public original in this review, so they cannot serve as a finding about today's market. The question here is what happens after an output reaches a buyer: how much work remains before it can be used responsibly?

Suppose you are writing instructions for a publicly available software product. You ask AI to turn scattered feature notes into a page of clear instructions. The draft arrives quickly and reads well. You still have to check whether button names match the current version, whether the steps work, whether any limits have disappeared, and whether a reader who follows them ends up on the wrong screen. One wrong sentence may send you back into the product to retrace the workflow and revise the surrounding paragraphs as well.

Those checks are not a moral performance added to the job. They are part of delivering the instructions. If a cheap output makes you guess the basis for every claim, the saved generation time may have become your checking time. A more expensive output might take less total time if it makes its uncertain points clear. This is the author's proposed way of comparing services, not a ranking established by the sources available here.

In a [2025 study by Lee and colleagues](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf), researchers surveyed 319 knowledge workers who used generative AI at work at least weekly and gathered 936 examples of its use. Participants described shifting some of their thinking from producing material themselves to verifying information, integrating responses, and keeping the task on course. Greater confidence in AI on a particular task was associated with less self-reported critical thinking: deliberately judging an answer against a goal and the available evidence. This is a correlation in a survey of people's own accounts. It cannot show that AI caused anyone to lose the ability to think. It does remind me that checking work does not vanish just because the interface feels effortless.

Verification costs more than minutes. To spot an out-of-date button name, you must know what the product currently shows. To judge whether advice is workable, you must know your constraints. To stop revising, you must be able to say what counts as good enough. Without that knowledge, a human review may amount to proofreading the sentences AI produced. The invoice has no line for this expertise, but someone still has to supply it.

## The task changes the price of the same answer

Some answers are easy to check. If AI rewrites an opening you just wrote, you know the intended meaning and can compare the versions. If it extracts a date from a document you can open, you can return to the original. Other answers are difficult to check. A long plan in a field you do not know may rely on premises scattered across several sources. You may not even know whom to ask. Two equally fluent responses can therefore come with very different verification costs.

I find it more useful to separate tasks by how they can be checked than to separate models into expensive and cheap. In one kind of task, mistakes are cheap to discover: you have a source document, a test procedure, or familiar criteria, and a mistake is easy to undo. In another, mistakes are hard to find, or correcting one after delivery creates more downstream work. For the first kind, pursue speed. For the second, ask where the evidence will come from, who can verify it, and what happens if they cannot. This is a way to make a decision, not a preference for a price tier.

In a [2019 review, Ericsson and Harwell](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full) argue that practice cannot be assessed by hours alone. In their discussion of musical training, they emphasize a specific goal, feedback that identifies the gap, and another attempt aimed at closing it. Repeating a performance and practicing to improve a particular part of it are not interchangeable. Their paper examines definitions and existing evidence about skill training; it does not test AI services. I propose a question based on it: if I cannot describe what good performance would look like on this task, might more AI use merely make me accustomed to an untested answer? The review did not test that claim for AI services.

Nor can I assume a good trial result will automatically carry over. Transfer means using something learned in one task in another task. In a [2017 experiment by Bonney and colleagues](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0174214), 111 children aged six to ten trained with movement-controlled games for five weeks. The researchers compared repeating one game with varying the games. Both groups showed similar transfer to physical tasks; elements shared by the training and target tasks mattered more in their results. The study concerns children's motor learning, so it cannot predict AI-service quality. It suggests a question for a trial, rather than an established AI finding: how many of the critical steps in the demonstration also appear in the work to be delivered?

That instructions page makes the difference visible. An AI service may write a polished generic product introduction while never checking the version, controls, or limitations. That demonstrates writing ability, but it does not show that the service will reduce the hardest verification work. Another response may be less elegant yet show which source supports each step and what remains uncertain. The first may win a demo; the second may save more work at delivery. Until the real task enters the trial, price has no useful denominator.

## A trial that can reveal the difference

Here is the author's proposed trial method; these studies have not tested it. Treat a trial as a small delivery, rather than invite an AI to perform intelligence on cue. Start with one piece of work you genuinely expect to do soon, where a mistake can still be corrected. Keep the original material and the criteria for acceptance. Then you can at least tell whether each service answered the same question. Do not replace your actual task with a public example; that example may have its difficult context arranged in advance.

Record the whole path from request to delivery. How long did the response take? Which statements sent you back to an original source? What was wrong? Did one correction force others? Could the result go to its actual reader? Record the quoted price, certainly, but also your checking time. That is how you discover whether the cheapest call brought the most expensive cleanup. If a possible error has not yet surfaced, mark it as unverified rather than assigning it a cost of zero.

Change one thing next: try another service with the same material and acceptance criteria. Then repeat with fresh material of the same kind. The first task might simply have been easy. A second one helps you see whether the result holds up. I am not looking for a magical overall score. I want to know whether a service consistently reduces the work I really have to do. If your tasks differ greatly, compare them separately instead of hiding those differences in an average.

Set a stopping condition for the trial. If several services meet the standard on a low-risk job, choose the one with the lower total cost. If you lack a reliable way to check a high-stakes answer, narrow AI's role for now: ask it to list points that need investigation, for example, and leave the final judgment to someone able to take responsibility. Realizing that you cannot yet verify a result is useful information. Pretending you already have is where costs get out of hand.

In [Paul Graham's 2013 account of Viaweb's early work](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), the team built merchants' sites using its own software. When Graham found that the software lacked a feature in the middle of building a site, he implemented it and went back to the job. This is a founder's recollection, not a controlled study of startup success. What interests me is where the check occurred: in the middle of real use, with the change tested by the work that called for it. A trial of an AI service can be most revealing at the point where you are about to deliver something.

## The ability to check is part of the cost

Someone might say that, if checking is this expensive, the answer is to buy the best service every time. But “best” still needs an object: for whom, for what job, and with whom checking it? An expensive answer that sends you back to verify every source may still cost a lot. A cheap answer that an existing test can judge immediately may be an excellent deal. No tool erases the differences among tasks. When the material is incomplete or the standard is changing, clarifying that standard may help more than upgrading a subscription.

This is also a question about learning. If I can locate an error, I have a chance to make a quicker judgment next time. If I trust each answer because it sounds right, doing more work may not make me more capable. The survey above records reported checking behavior; the practice review concerns training in specific domains. Neither proves that my proposed habit will improve long-term ability. I would rather treat it as a rule to test in my own work: after a review, keep one check that can be used again. If the only lesson is “pay more attention,” the review has not yet become a skill.

Here is a question worth taking back to your own work: on the last task where AI seemed to save you money, who spent the time confirming that its output was usable? If the answer is “no one,” hold off on calling it cheap. If the answer is “I did,” add your time back to the bill. On your next trial, use a real, reversible task and compare the call, the checking, and the rework by the same standard. Name the price when the work is delivered.
