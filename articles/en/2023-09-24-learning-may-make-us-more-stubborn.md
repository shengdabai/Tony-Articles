# Learning May Make Us More Stubborn

Learning is one of the most important ways for human progress and growth, but sometimes we find that learning can actually make us more stubborn.

This phenomenon has attracted widespread attention. So why does learning lead to stubbornness?

The learning process often requires us to encounter new knowledge and think about new perspectives.

However, as knowledge accumulates, we gradually form our own thinking patterns and belief systems.

This accumulation results in us becoming more stubborn, believing only in our own views and ignoring other possible explanations and perspectives.

This is a kind of psychological comfort zone. People don’t want to step out of it because stepping out may mean facing uncertainty and challenges.

Additionally, the authority and professionalism involved in the learning process are also reasons for stubbornness.

When we come across the views of someone regarded as an authority, we tend to accept and believe them without hesitation.

This blind worship of authority can make us resist other viewpoints and become more rigid in our beliefs.

However, learning also provides opportunities to change stubbornness. Through learning, we can gain a more comprehensive understanding of issues, broaden our horizons, and become aware of our own limitations.

At the same time, learning cultivates our critical thinking abilities, allowing us to approach different viewpoints more rationally and seek a balance.

The reasons why learning can make us stubborn are diverse and complex, involving psychological comfort zones, authority, and professionalism.

Yet, learning itself is also a way to change stubbornness.

Through critical thinking and an open attitude, we can challenge our own stubbornness, embrace diverse perspectives, and continuously surpass ourselves, achieving personal growth and development.

Let learning be the wings that expand our thinking, so our thoughts and beliefs are no longer rigid, and we can embrace change and progress.

This is because, recently, in my conversations with my father, I noticed that his understanding of the health industry had been influenced by community learning, and he had become somewhat stubborn. For example, he believed he could treat himself, which I could not accept. Professional matters should be handled by professionals.

The body isn’t comfortable, and if certain symptoms persist, please seek medical attention promptly. Don’t listen to what’s on the internet or what someone else tells you.

Of course, I also admit that after learning, I too can fall into stubbornness. For instance, when it comes to health and nutrition, I tend to trust academic literature and experimental research.

The learning process often earns us respect and recognition. We gain honor and a sense of achievement through learning.

This positive feedback enhances our confidence and can lead to a sense of arrogance, making us more stubborn in holding onto our views.

We may feel our knowledge is sufficient and professional, no longer willing to accept or consider others' opinions.

In this way, some communities might be doing exactly this—reinforcing your sense of belonging within the group, gradually making you feel only those within the group truly understand you.

Learning can form our own “knowledge circles,” where we interact with people who share similar views, developing a mindset of “only I can do it.”

This formation of circles deepens our belief in our own views and makes us ignore the existence of different perspectives.

We become accustomed to interacting with people who think like us, forming a group that supports and confirms our views, further deepening our stubbornness.

However, we must recognize that learning should not become an excuse for stubbornness.

The essence of learning should be to broaden our thinking and increase our understanding and respect for diverse perspectives.

We can break out of our knowledge circles by engaging in diversified learning and interactions, and by exposing ourselves to different viewpoints and jointly exploring issues.

Only by maintaining openness and humility can learning truly become a force that guides us to embrace diversity and avoid stubbornness.

Insight into the psychological mechanisms of learning stubbornness: “Obedience” and “Cognitive Closure”

One psychological mechanism behind learning stubbornness is “obedience”—that is, conforming to already accepted ideas and the views of authority figures, while resisting new perspectives.

In learning, people often seek correct and stable answers, willing to believe and conform, because this can earn social recognition and a sense of security.

In the process of obedience, people find it difficult to evaluate and weigh the correctness and probability of facts, leading to phenomena such as “path dependency” and “cognitive closure.”

Another psychological mechanism of learning stubbornness is “cognitive closure”—that is, sticking to our existing cognitive frameworks and refusing to accept new ideas and facts.

This pattern makes us look for answers to world issues only within our existing cognitive frameworks, ignoring the possibilities of new things, thus affecting the quality and effectiveness of our learning.

We can use another learning method—reflective learning—to overcome this psychological mechanism of learning stubbornness.

Reflective learning allows us to ask ourselves questions based on thinking, raising awareness levels, and helping us view our cognitive models from a higher perspective, thus escaping the state of cognitive closure.

This learning method can enhance our ability to accept new ideas and promote our self-growth and development.

Learning stubbornness may be an inevitable emotional and psychological process in our learning journey, with “obedience” and “cognitive closure” being two main psychological mechanisms.

We can overcome this by changing our learning approach and adopting a more reflective learning style, thus guiding us out of the trap of learning stubbornness, overcoming the limitations of cognitive frameworks, and improving our self-awareness and thinking levels to achieve more comprehensive and innovative learning outcomes.

Finally, here’s a clever tip I learned from Li Xiaolai. When dealing with stubborn learners, especially family and friends, we should learn to start a “virtual machine” when we have to communicate with them.

Li Xiaolai’s teacher, in the course *The Road to Financial Freedom* on Dedao, concept 49, “Ultimate Question,” in part 02, has a brilliant insight:

Often, I feel that knowledge from the computer field can help us solve many problems. For example, the concept of operating systems and software upgrades are all derived from computers. So, when facing such issues, can we also find a similar solution in the computer field?

The answer is: yes.

For instance, you can start a “virtual machine” within your own operating system. When you have to communicate with someone who has conflicting views, you can use this as a tool.

Although I don’t believe in things like horoscopes or blood types, that doesn’t stop me from learning about them and building a virtual system. When I meet people who like these concepts, I can start a virtual machine and run their operating system. The result is a win-win situation—nobody gets hurt, and my operating system remains intact.

We cannot force anyone to progress, and we must also learn to protect ourselves from being held back by others.

Start your virtual machine, and you can communicate better, maintain your own pace, and protect the people who matter most.
