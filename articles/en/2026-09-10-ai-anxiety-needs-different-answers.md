# Not Every AI Anxiety Can Be Solved by Learning More AI

> 发布日期:2026-09-10 · [中文](../zh/2026-09-10-不是所有 AI 焦虑，都能靠多学 AI 解决.md) | [English](../en/2026-09-10-ai-anxiety-needs-different-answers.md)

---

This morning, I placed two notes beside each other.

One was about someone with a humanities background building an iOS app from scratch with AI. There was no conventional programming apprenticeship first. Curiosity came before credentials, and the tool helped that curiosity cross a professional boundary.

The other note was about teachers facing AI. Their questions were heavier. What happens when students use it to avoid thinking? What if the profession changes faster than teachers can adapt? Who is responsible when a system gives a confident but harmful answer?

The same technology produced excitement in one scene and unease in the other.

We usually compress the second reaction into two words: AI anxiety. Then we offer one standard prescription: learn more AI.

I no longer think that prescription is good enough.

If I am anxious because I cannot operate a tool, practice may help. If I am anxious because my role is being hollowed out, another tutorial may only make the threat clearer. If I am worried about a system making consequential decisions without an appeal path, telling me to write better prompts misses the point entirely.

AI anxiety is not one problem. It is several different problems wearing the same name. Until we separate them, we will keep giving technically correct answers to the wrong fear.

## One label is hiding four different fears

Researchers who developed the 21-item [Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale](https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2019.1674887) divided the idea into four dimensions.

The first is learning anxiety: the feeling that AI is too difficult to understand or use.

The second is job-replacement anxiety: the fear that a machine will make part of your work, or perhaps your whole role, less valuable.

The third is sociotechnical blindness. The phrase is awkward, but the underlying concern is familiar: people may deploy AI without understanding how much it still depends on human choices, and humans may be pushed aside by systems they do not adequately govern.

The fourth is configuration anxiety: discomfort with the form AI takes, especially when it becomes more humanlike or physically embodied.

These fears can arrive together, but they do not behave as one emotion.

A 2026 study of 396 pre-service teachers in Northern Cyprus found that overall AI anxiety did not predict their 21st-century skills as a single block. Specific dimensions, however, had different relationships with critical thinking, problem-solving, career awareness, social responsibility, and leadership. Learning anxiety, for example, was a negative predictor of critical thinking, problem-solving, and career awareness in that sample. The authors therefore argued for responses aimed at particular concerns instead of broad AI-literacy programs alone. The study was cross-sectional and focused on one population, so it does not establish cause and effect. Its useful contribution is more modest: [the dimensions should not be treated as interchangeable](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1810689/full).

That changes the first question we should ask.

Not “How anxious am I about AI?”

But “What, exactly, am I afraid will happen?”

## More training can solve one fear and expose another

There is a small but revealing study that made this distinction feel practical to me.

Researchers gave 195 pre-service teachers a four-week program on using AI in education. After the program, anxiety about learning AI went down. Anxiety in the other measured dimensions went up. Because the study used a single-group design, we should not treat the training as proven cause. Still, the result is a useful warning: [greater familiarity reduced the fear of using AI but did not make every other concern disappear](https://ijte.net/index.php/ijte/article/view/1521).

That makes sense.

Before you use an AI agent, you may vaguely fear that it is complicated. After you use one, that fear can shrink. You now know where to click, what to ask, and how to recover from a bad answer.

At the same time, you may notice new problems. The agent can send a message too quickly. It can summarize a person unfairly. It can make a polished recommendation from incomplete evidence. Skill removes mystery, but it also reveals consequences.

This is not a failure of education. It may be evidence that education worked.

Some anxiety comes from ignorance. Some comes from seeing more clearly.

If we insist that all discomfort is a knowledge deficit, we train people to silence legitimate judgment. A person who asks where the data came from, who can reverse a decision, or who carries the loss is not necessarily resisting progress. That person may be doing the most important work in the room.

The goal, then, is not zero anxiety. The goal is to convert each kind of anxiety into the right kind of action.

## Four questions, four different responses

I now use four questions to make the feeling more concrete.

### Do I know how to begin one real task?

This tests for learning anxiety.

Imagine opening an AI coding tool to make a small personal webpage. Five tabs of tutorials are open, unfamiliar terms keep appearing, and you still have not created a file. The problem is not your future career. The problem is that the first step is too large and too abstract.

The response is a bounded practice loop. Choose one result that can be finished in 20 minutes: rename a batch of files, turn notes into a table, or change the color of one button. Do it with AI, inspect the result, and repair one mistake yourself.

Do not study the entire tool. Complete one loop.

### Which part of my work am I afraid will become cheap?

This tests for replacement anxiety.

“AI will replace my job” is too large to act on. A job is a bundle of activities. Some produce an output. Some require judgment. Some create a promise for which a person or organization remains responsible.

Suppose AI can draft a client proposal in minutes. Drafting has become cheaper. Deciding what not to promise has not. Understanding an unstated constraint has not. Standing behind the result when reality disagrees has not.

Split the role into three columns: execution, judgment, and consequence. Let AI compete for the execution column. Deliberately train the other two. The useful question is not whether your old bundle survives intact. It is whether you are moving toward the parts whose value rises when output becomes abundant.

### What harm worries me even if I become highly skilled?

This tests for sociotechnical concern.

If your worry would remain after you became an expert user, another course is not the answer. You need a system boundary.

Consider a team that lets AI prepare customer messages. The operator may be excellent at prompting, but skill does not answer whether the tool may send without review, use private records, or make a promise that cannot be withdrawn.

The response is governance made concrete: define what data the system may see, what actions it may take, what must be logged, where a human must approve, and how someone can appeal or stop the process.

That is not fear blocking the work. That is judgment shaping the work.

### Which form of AI makes the discomfort appear?

This tests for configuration anxiety.

Some people are comfortable asking a text box for ideas but uncomfortable with an always-listening voice assistant. Others accept software that recommends an action but resist an agent that takes the action by itself. A humanlike robot can provoke a reaction that an invisible algorithm does not, even when their underlying capabilities overlap.

Instead of arguing with the feeling in the abstract, change the form. Turn off the voice. Reduce autonomy. Keep the tool inside a sandbox. Require confirmation before external actions. Then test again.

The shape of a technology is part of the experience. We do not have to adopt every shape at once to learn what is useful.

## A twenty-minute anxiety audit

Here is a practice you can do today.

Write one sentence at the top of a page: “When I think about using AI for ______, I am afraid that ______.”

Then ask the four questions:

- Do I know how to begin one real task?
- Which part of my work might become cheap?
- What harm would still worry me if I were highly skilled?
- Which form or level of autonomy makes the discomfort appear?

Circle the question that carries the most weight. Do only the matching action.

Finish one tiny task. Redesign one part of your role around judgment and responsibility. Add one boundary to an AI workflow. Or test the same capability in a less autonomous form.

Then stop reading AI news for the day.

This last step matters. News can enlarge a fear without telling you which kind it is. A small experiment gives the fear an object, a boundary, and a result you can examine.

## Do not waste the signal

I still believe curiosity can carry ordinary people across borders that once looked professional and permanent. The person with a humanities background who builds an app is not proof that expertise no longer matters. It is proof that the entrance has moved.

But an open entrance does not remove every reason to hesitate.

Some hesitation says, “I need a smaller first step.” Some says, “I need to rebuild my role.” Some says, “This system needs a brake.” Some says, “I am not ready to let the machine take this form.”

Those sentences should not receive the same reply.

Using AI to make people stronger does not mean pushing everyone toward maximum adoption. It means helping people preserve agency: enough skill to act, enough judgment to set limits, and enough responsibility to own the outcome.

Anxiety becomes expensive when it stays vague. Once named precisely, it can become a curriculum, a career decision, a system requirement, or a boundary.

Before trying to defeat your AI anxiety, ask it one honest question:

What job are you here to do?
