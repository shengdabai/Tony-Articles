# When Tools Grow Senses

> 发布日期:2026-07-27 · [中文](../zh/2026-07-27-当工具长出感官.md) | [English](../en/2026-07-27-when-tools-grow-senses.md)

---

For years, the phone was the place where the world became usable.

If you wanted a car, you opened an app. If you wanted food, you opened an app. If you wanted a meeting note, a map, a message, a payment, a song, a camera, a classroom, a store, or a bank, you returned to the same rectangle. The phone did not only connect us to the internet. It trained us to believe that digital life has a center.

That center is beginning to loosen.

Not because the phone will disappear next year. It will not. The phone still has identity, payment, security, compute, distribution, social habit, and the most mature developer ecosystem in consumer technology. Any new device that tries to bypass the phone has to fight too many layers at once.

But the phone is no longer the only imaginable interface.

Today's notes had a useful phrase: AI-native hardware is not just another connected terminal. It is intelligence growing organs. Glasses become eyes. Earbuds become ears. A watch becomes a pulse and context sensor. A car becomes a moving room. A robot becomes a hand. The phone remains important, but it changes from the only entrance into a core node among many.

That shift is easy to underestimate because it sounds like a hardware story.

I think it is really an attention story.

## The Screen Taught Us to Fetch

The smartphone era trained a simple behavior: when you need something, go to the screen and fetch it.

That is an extraordinary achievement. A person can stand on a street corner, ask a question, compare prices, call a car, transfer money, translate a sentence, publish a video, and learn almost anything. For most of human history, this would have looked like magic.

But every magic has a posture.

The posture of the phone is: stop, look down, choose, tap, wait, switch, return.

Even when the app is beautifully designed, the human still has to become the operator. We learn menus. We remember where the feature lives. We tolerate notification layers. We move between contexts. We translate intention into command.

This is why so many people describe the next wave of devices as "more natural." The word is vague, but the direction is real. Apple's Vision Pro, available in the United States on February 2, 2024, was marketed around eyes, hands, and voice as the primary inputs; Apple says its R1 chip processes input from 12 cameras, five sensors, and six microphones.[^1] Whether or not the product becomes mainstream, the message is clear: the computer is trying to leave the flat rectangle and read more of the situation.

The same pattern appears in smart glasses, AI earbuds, meeting devices, car assistants, and robots. The important move is not that every device becomes successful. Most will not. The important move is that interaction starts shifting from command-based to intent-based.

In the old model, I adapt to the machine.

In the new model, the machine tries to understand where I am, what I am doing, and what I probably mean.

That is powerful.

It is also dangerous.

## A Sensor Is Not Yet a Servant

The failed AI hardware cycle has already taught us something valuable: a device does not become intelligent because it has a microphone, camera, model, and a dramatic launch video.

The Humane AI Pin is a useful warning. It was sold as a screen-free AI wearable, but after HP acquired much of the company in 2025, reporting from multiple outlets said the device business was discontinued and existing Pins would lose server-connected functions by February 28, 2025.[^2] I do not bring this up to mock one company. The lesson is broader than one product.

The device was not weak because the dream was silly.

The dream was too large for the loop.

AI hardware lives or dies by loops, not by form factor.

First is the perception loop. Can the device reliably capture the right context at the right moment? A camera that sees badly, a microphone that hears poorly, or a model that misunderstands ordinary situations does not feel futuristic. It feels rude. It interrupts life and then asks the user to repair the misunderstanding.

Second is the permission loop. What is this device allowed to know, remember, suggest, and do? A phone is already intimate, but it usually waits behind a screen. A wearable that sees and hears the world changes the social contract. It involves other people, not only the owner. It enters classrooms, elevators, meetings, streets, kitchens, and bedrooms. The question is no longer only "Can it help me?" It is also "What am I authorizing it to notice?"

Third is the practice loop. Does the device make the user stronger over time, or merely more dependent? This is the part I care about most. A good AI device should not turn the human into a passive passenger. It should make attention sharper, memory more useful, action easier to review, and judgment more explicit.

The best future is not a world where every object whispers suggestions at us.

The best future is a world where intelligence enters the environment in a way that helps us act with more awareness.

## The Real Device Is the Relationship

This is why I do not like the question "Will smart glasses replace the phone?"

It is too small.

The better question is: which human behavior becomes easier to perform without shrinking the person?

Ray-Ban Meta glasses have reportedly sold in the millions, and their relative success is not mysterious.[^3] They look like something people already understand. They sit in a familiar place on the body. They let people capture a first-person view, listen, speak, and use AI without pulling out a phone. The point is not that glasses are magic. The point is that the device borrows an existing habit instead of demanding that the user learn a whole new lifestyle script.

That is a good general rule for AI hardware.

Do not ask, "What device can we invent?"

Ask, "Where is the human already trying to perceive, decide, remember, or act?"

An earbud is not interesting because it is small. It is interesting if it makes a live conversation easier to understand, preserves a useful meeting memory, or helps a person move through language barriers without turning every exchange into screen work.

A camera is not interesting because it sees. It is interesting if it helps a child explore the world, helps a worker document reality without stopping the task, or helps a learner ask better questions about what is in front of them.

A robot is not interesting because it looks human. It is interesting if it can handle a narrow physical responsibility well enough that trust can accumulate.

The real device is not the object.

The real device is the relationship between human intention, machine perception, permission, and feedback.

When that relationship is weak, the object becomes a gadget.

When that relationship is strong, the object becomes an organ of a larger system.

## Personal AI Needs a Body, but So Do We

There is a tempting fantasy in all of this: once AI enters the physical world, it will finally handle life for us.

I think the opposite is more useful.

When AI grows senses, we have to grow new discipline.

If an assistant can hear more, I need clearer boundaries. If a wearable can remember more, I need better review habits. If a device can suggest more, I need stronger taste about what to ignore. If an agent can act across devices, I need to define what kind of person I am trying to become, not only what task I want finished.

Otherwise the environment becomes a giant slot machine of helpfulness.

This is already visible in software. A person gives AI access to notes, messages, calendars, files, browser history, and tasks, then feels both empowered and invaded. The problem is not only privacy. It is self-command. A tool that can see everything does not automatically know what matters. If I have not designed my own priorities, the system will optimize around whatever is easiest to infer.

The same will be even more true in hardware.

When intelligence lives only in a chatbox, the boundary is obvious. You open the box. You ask. You close it.

When intelligence lives in glasses, earbuds, cars, home devices, cameras, and robots, the boundary becomes behavioral. It is shaped by defaults, rituals, permissions, review cycles, and social norms.

That is why the next literacy is not only prompt writing.

It is environment design.

Can I arrange my tools so they capture what helps me learn, not what feeds distraction?

Can I give AI enough context to help without giving it authority I have not earned the right to delegate?

Can I turn captured experience into better decisions instead of a larger archive?

Can I use sensors to become more present, not less present?

These questions sound personal, but they are also practical. A teacher, consultant, builder, or small-team owner does not need a science-fiction device. They need small loops that make work remember itself: meetings that produce decisions, conversations that produce follow-up, failures that improve checklists, examples that become reusable teaching material, repeated tasks that become clearer systems.

Hardware may make those loops more natural.

But only if the human asks for loops, not spectacle.

## The Phone Becomes the Spine

So no, I do not think the phone simply dies.

More likely, it becomes a spine.

It will hold identity, payments, privacy controls, high-friction operations, and deeper editing. Around it, smaller organs will handle perception and quick action: glasses for first-person view, earbuds for voice and translation, watches for body signals, cars for movement, home devices for domestic context, robots for physical execution.

That network will not be automatically good.

It can become surveillance with better UX.

It can become dependence with prettier language.

It can become a thousand tiny interruptions pretending to be assistance.

Or it can become a real extension of human agency.

The difference will not come from the hardware alone. It will come from the discipline of the user, the ethics of the builder, the design of permission, and the quality of the feedback loop.

The old phone era asked us to learn apps.

The AI hardware era will ask us to learn ourselves again: what we notice, what we permit, what we delegate, what we review, and what kind of attention we want to protect.

Tools are beginning to grow senses.

That does not mean we can afford to become numb.

It means we need to become more awake.
