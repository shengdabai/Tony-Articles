# The Minority View in a Team Forecast

Even when a team agrees, I'd ask what each person checked. Four people can all put the chance at 70 percent after watching the same demo. If one says 30 percent and the other three say 70, I'd ask what the person at 30 percent saw and what the other three relied on. A person who said 70 percent might be the only one who read a policy that could affect delivery.

When someone suggests moving a task up the schedule, I'd ask what they've learned that makes it worth rearranging the work. I'd like to try that in team forecasting too. The team can start planning around a shared estimate and keep at most one reason the original plan missed. It needs a source and someone who can check it while there's still time to change the plan.

## Agree on What You Are Forecasting

Suppose you're planning a software pilot for ten people who've signed up to use their own computers. Will at least eight export and open a file on their own before 5 p.m. on Friday? Ask the pilot lead, the developer, the designer, and the person handling questions to each write down a probability and the one piece of evidence that most shaped it. Once all four have written theirs, look at the numbers and reasons together.

Agree up front on what counts as finishing. Participants can follow the public instructions, but they have to export and open the file themselves. A remote takeover doesn't count, and anyone who misses the pilot still counts among the original ten. You could ask a separate question. Will anyone attend, and will at least 80 percent of those who attend export and open a file on their own before 5 p.m. Friday? That question measures the completion rate among attendees. Keep the estimates separate even if someone gives both questions 70 percent.

Say one group gives estimates of 65, 70, 70, and 75 percent. Another gives 30, 70, 70, and 75 percent. Sort each set of four numbers and average the middle two to get the median. Both groups land at 70 percent, the estimated chance that at least eight people finish. Both can use it to plan the instructions and support. In the second group, I'd also ask what the person who said 30 percent saw.

Suppose three people in the second group watched the same demo on the developer's computer. The person who said 30 percent has read a device policy, a document that says what people can do on the computers it covers. It lists places where files can't be saved on a type of computer some participants might use. She hasn't seen an export fail and doesn't know how many people might run into the restriction. I'd write down which policy she read and what still needs checking. I'd do the same if the person who said 75 percent brought in the policy.

Barbara Mellers and colleagues studied a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament held from 2011 to 2013. Participants put probabilities on political events around the world, such as whether Italy's prime minister would leave office before January 1, 2012. The questions had clear rules for determining the outcome, and researchers scored forecasts by day and by question. [Mellers and colleagues' tournament questions and scoring](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---psychological-strategies-for-winning-a-tournament.pdf)

In the first year, team members could share sources and reasons. They also got extra training in how to explain their judgments and raise objections. They were more accurate than forecasters who worked alone but could see which probabilities others gave and how often each was reported. Those forecasters, in turn, were more accurate than people working alone with no access to anyone else's estimates. The team setup combined discussion and extra training, so the comparison couldn't isolate the effect of sharing information. [Mellers and colleagues' first-year comparison](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---psychological-strategies-for-winning-a-tournament.pdf)

Back at the pilot meeting, suppose two people say 70 percent. One has read the policy but assumes no participant will use that type of computer. I'd ask the person handling questions to find out which computer each participant will use and who manages it. Even if both forecasters read the same policy, I'd ask each of them who they expect to use a restricted computer. Once that assumption is out in the open, the person handling questions knows what to check.

Think of the weather apps on your phone. Suppose both say it's likely to rain over the weekend. I'd check where each got its forecast. If both pass on the same one, a different icon still gets you the same forecast. If one draws on observations released later, there's something new to look at, and I'd ask how much that changed the forecast. Carrying an umbrella won't change the weather. Switching to a folder people can save to before the pilot might change how many finish on Friday, so I'd also record what the team changed.

## Which Disagreement Is Worth Keeping?

I'd ask the person handling questions to find out whether the policy covers the participants' computers. At the same time, I'd ask the developer to find a computer with the same restrictions, follow the public instructions to export a file to the default save location, and try to open it. The default save location is the folder the software picks in advance. Neither check was in the original plan. The developer can get started while the 30 percent sits where it is.

To get eight finishers out of ten, you can afford at most two people falling short. If three get stuck at the save step and can't finish on their own in time, you get at most seven, even if everyone else finishes. If just one person is affected and the team can help without much extra work, I'd still address it. Whether to move the 70 percent, and by how much, also depends on what the other nine can do. A few more attempts on the developer's own computer still only tell you what happens on that machine. To answer this objection, try a computer with the same restriction.

If two device policies say different things, check their dates and which computers each covers. If the old version has expired, use the current one. If both are current and cover different computers, find out which kind each participant will use. You may still leave the meeting with the same two numbers, 70 and 30 percent. Write down the version and the devices it covers so the next person knows whether to go back to the policy or ask the participants.

In 2014, Ville A. Satopää and colleagues analyzed 166 yes-or-no questions from the same tournament program, covering 2011 to 2013. Participants could submit or revise probabilities at different times and rated their expertise on each question, from 1 for no expertise to 5 for a great deal of expertise. The researchers grouped forecasters by those ratings and used the submission times when combining forecasts. In the recorded data, they flipped some outcome labels and replaced the corresponding probabilities with one minus the original value, giving yes and no equal numbers of questions and nearly equal total days open. They took turns setting aside batches of questions from that same dataset for testing, using the remaining questions to fit the method for combining and adjusting probabilities. On the questions set aside, the share of yes outcomes among combined forecasts with similar probabilities roughly matched the probabilities reported. [Satopää and colleagues' data, model, and tests](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.0087)

For the pilot, I'd record two times beside the shared estimate. One is when we last changed the number. The other is when the main evidence is from. You might find a report today that describes last month's data. A policy you just opened still has a version and an effective date. When someone revises the estimate, those two times help the team see whether something new happened or someone just found an older source.

Whether I'd spend half a day tracking down a computer depends on how much room we have to help during the pilot. If the person handling questions can quickly show participants how to finish on their own and a short delay costs little, I'd spend that half day on the original preparations. If participants can only use their own computers and get just one chance to attend, I'd borrow a comparable computer and try it beforehand. Find out how late you can change the public instructions or the save location, then set the deadline for the check. A correct answer at five on Friday comes after the pilot has handed in its work.

Mellers and colleagues' 2015 follow-up covered three rounds of the same program from 2011 to 2014. They looked at how far apart different people's estimates for the same question were on a given day, then averaged the pattern across questions. Among forecasters picked for special teams after doing well in earlier rounds, estimates generally drew closer as questions neared resolution. Estimates in the two comparison groups generally spread farther apart. Who was picked and who they worked with could both have shaped the comparison. [Mellers and colleagues' follow-up study](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf)

I'd cross the pilot issue off the list once we know which participants' computers are affected and we've tested whether a file can be exported and opened under the same restrictions. If we confirm that no participant will use an affected computer, we can record that finding and close the issue. Even if everyone in the group chat acknowledges the message, I'd still wait for the check. If a participant hasn't replied, their device status stays open.

## Check on Wednesday, Count on Friday

My proposal is to put the shared estimate and the reason to check in one group message, one line each. The first line keeps the original question, estimate, conditions, and deadline. On Friday, add how many finished and whether that reached eight. On the second line, put the source and check, then fill in what you found, what you did, and when. Here's how I'd fill it in for the hypothetical pilot.

**Forecast**  At least eight of ten sign-ups export and open a file on their own before 5 p.m. Friday | Shared estimate 70%, time pending | Default save location, device restrictions still to check | Friday count and whether it met the target pending  
**Reason**  Device policy, version and effective date pending | By close of business Wednesday, support confirms device type and who manages it, and the developer follows the public instructions on a computer with the same restrictions, exports to the default location, and tries to open the file | Finding, action, and time pending

Welton Chang and colleagues compared ways of evaluating forecasters in the fourth year of the same program, the 2014–2015 season. Process evaluation looked at how forecasters found information and explained their judgments, including whether they clicked on relevant news and whether their reasons helped other forecasters. Accuracy evaluation measured how far reported probabilities were from actual outcomes. The researchers averaged the forecasts within each evaluation group and scored those averages. In this experiment, the group evaluated on both measures was more accurate than the group evaluated on process alone. [Chang and colleagues' experiment on evaluation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/accountability-and-adaptive-performance-under-uncertainty-a-longterm-view/A16B2380B817354B0F39A392BFD1FD5C)

A separate group of trained forecasters rated the explanations. The researchers measured the update rate using impressions, counting how many times an explanation was shown to a rater. Of the impressions for explanations from the process-only group, 10.6 percent prompted an update. The figures were 9.9 percent for the group evaluated on both measures and 8.2 percent for the accuracy-only group. The researchers also compared scores before and after updates that raters credited to a particular explanation, to see how much accuracy improved per update on average. That comparison detected no difference among the three groups in average improvement per update. [Chang and colleagues' explanation impressions, updates, and scores](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/accountability-and-adaptive-performance-under-uncertainty-a-longterm-view/A16B2380B817354B0F39A392BFD1FD5C)

Suppose the team finds on Wednesday that some computers can't save to the default location. The team switches to an allowed location that day, and exactly eight people finish on Friday. The objection prompted the switch, and those eight people finished after the change. How many would have finished under the original plan is still unknown. Now suppose Wednesday's check shows the policy doesn't apply to these computers, but only seven people finish on Friday. It's a little early to hand the person who said 30 percent a prophecy award. The policy has been ruled out, so the next thing to check is where someone actually got stuck.

I'd ask AI to tidy up the record. I'd have it flag statements that cite the same document, mark where a possible restriction has been written up as a confirmed one, and turn a missing check into a task I can hand to someone. The developer could take the assignment in the line above, follow the public instructions, and verify the export and opening results in person. Before asking the model for a probability, I'd ask what material it has that the team hasn't seen.

Philipp Schoenegger and Peter S. Park used a tournament run on the Metaculus forecasting platform from July to October 2023 to compare GPT-4 and human crowd forecasts on 23 yes-or-no questions. They used a Brier score to measure how far a forecast probability was from the outcome. Under the rule in their preprint, write down 1 if the event happened and 0 if it didn't. If you gave it a 70 percent chance and it happened, subtract 1 from 0.70 and multiply the difference by itself to get 0.09. Score each question this way and take the average. A lower average means more accurate forecasts. [Schoenegger and Park's scoring rule](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13014)

The researchers gave GPT-4, through its web interface, the platform's background information and outcome criteria. For some questions, they also supplied the human crowd median. The researchers entered the model's forecast on the platform once per question and left it unchanged. The model averaged 0.20. For the human comparison, the researchers took the crowd median at the end of the day they entered the model's forecast, which could already include updates from that day. Using the same scoring rule, the human forecasts averaged 0.07. Those scores describe that comparison. An assistant that keeps gathering information and revising its estimates would need a separate test. [Schoenegger and Park's preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13014)

Four of the five papers here draw on the same geopolitical forecasting program. The two Mellers papers and the Satopää analysis draw on overlapping early rounds, and Chang's experiment comes from the fourth round. Schoenegger and Park used data from a separate Metaculus tournament in 2023. Taken together, the five papers cover two tournament settings. [Mellers 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---psychological-strategies-for-winning-a-tournament.pdf) · [Mellers 2015](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/mellers15.pdf) · [Satopää 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.0087) · [Chang 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/accountability-and-adaptive-performance-under-uncertainty-a-longterm-view/A16B2380B817354B0F39A392BFD1FD5C) · [Schoenegger and Park 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13014)

The two-line record is something I'd like to try in everyday work. How much it helps is for your team to find out. Pick something due in the next few days, with a result you can verify and enough time beforehand for a cheap check. Before you start the check, write down whether it was already in the plan. Once the result comes in, see what it changed. That way, come Friday, the reason only gets credit for work it actually prompted.

1. Set the question, deadline, and success criteria. Have everyone write their own estimate and main reason, with a source if they can trace one, then share them together.
2. Set an estimate you can plan around for now. Keep at most one reason the original plan missed that you can trace to a source, then write down who will check, when they'll report back, and what finding would change which part of the preparation. Leave the line blank if nothing qualifies.
3. Record the finding, action, and time, then fill in the outcome at the deadline. If the conditions change, keep the original estimate with its conditions and add an estimate for the revised plan. After several similar tasks, see whether the reasons you kept repeatedly prompted worthwhile checks outside the original plan, then see whether estimates made under comparable conditions are getting closer to what happens. If one forecast turns out to be right, just record it.

Pull up the next thing you're due to deliver. When everyone says it'll probably work, what piece of evidence does only one person have? Ask today. If you find a reason worth checking, put it beside the shared estimate in the same group message.
