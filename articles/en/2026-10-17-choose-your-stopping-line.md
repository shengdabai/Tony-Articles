# Choose Your Stopping Line While Things Are Calm

> 发布日期:2026-10-17 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-17-顺利时先定停手线.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-17-choose-your-stopping-line.md)

---

Two people both say they will stay calm when something goes wrong. One plans to decide what to do when it happens. The other, while things are quiet, writes down the signal that will make them stop, check, or ask for help. You might not notice a difference on an ordinary day. When trouble arrives, it matters. My view is that the most valuable part of a contingency plan is not predicting the surprise. It is making one decision before emotion can seize it: when to stop pushing ahead with the original plan.

You may already know that you ought to have a fallback. Why, then, is it still so easy to make a bad situation worse? Knowing the principle and acting on it under pressure are separated by a specific stopping condition. You can practice that condition, and the time to practice is when things are going well.

## Recognize where control starts to slip

An AI-generated summary of a recording dated today discussed a habit from engineering: prepare for unusual cases before they appear. It used conflicts in everyday life to make the point that waiting for a situation to escalate is a poor time to invent your next move. It raises a narrower question: when you feel sure you are right, and capable of handling the matter yourself, what signal will make you pause? I have not independently checked the summary's particular incidents or its probability estimates, so neither is evidence for this essay.

Engineers sometimes call a case that the normal process fails to cover an “edge case.” In plain terms, it is the thing that falls outside the usual steps and can make the result worse. Suppose you run a small webpage that lists times for public events and use AI to help organize information from public sources. Usually you look things up, prepare the page, publish it, and readers use it. Now imagine a reader says one date is wrong. A screenshot has been shared. Someone else posts a different date. You do not have time to write a treatise on who is right, yet you must choose what happens next.

You could answer at once to defend yourself. You could ask AI to produce a more polished explanation. Or you could mark the disputed entry as unverified, open the organizer's original announcement, and correct the page once you know. Which step is hardest? My guess is that looking up a date is easier than admitting you cannot yet speak for the reader with certainty. A machine can draft a reply fast; its speed cannot authenticate the date. A fluent defense may even make you less willing to pause.

A stopping condition must describe something you can observe. “Stay calm” does not. In this imagined example, it could read: if two sources give conflicting dates and I have not seen the organizer's original page, stop displaying a single date as certain. If the original page is ambiguous too, label the date unverified and link to that page. The condition and the first action belong together. You do not need to wait until you feel panicked to decide whether the issue is serious.

It is a bit like checking the weather before leaving home and bringing an umbrella. Carrying one does not mean you know when it will rain; you have simply chosen your first response in advance. The similarity ends at reducing the choices you must make in the moment. You can correct a webpage. Damage to someone's safety, reputation, or money may be much harder to undo, so its stopping condition should be earlier and stricter. Not every problem is a little rainstorm that gives you another try.

You might object that reality will never follow a detailed script. Quite right. A plan built from scenes that must happen in a set order will fail as soon as life changes the order. What I would write down is a trigger and a first move: what tells me the old approach is no longer reliable, what I stop doing, and which person or source I consult next. That does not promise a life without mistakes. It gives you a brake at the moment when the second mistake is easiest to make.

That brake cannot settle every question. In a situation involving physical safety, leaving danger and seeking help on site or from a qualified professional matter more than defending a position. Complex questions of responsibility also require the applicable rules and appropriate advice. Most irregularities in everyday work have much lower stakes. That is precisely why you can learn to pause on small, reversible tasks before you need the habit for a larger decision.

## A smooth process can remove chances to notice trouble

There is an awkward question here. If AI prevents even the ordinary mistakes you used to encounter, how will you learn to recognize an unusual one? I am not interested in praising inefficiency. Repetitive copying, mechanical formatting, and finding clearly identified information are fine things to save time on. But if you also hand over the basis for selecting a date, the act of checking conflicting sources, and the response to a challenge, you may end up with a page ready to publish without knowing when that page should no longer be displayed as certain.

[An Anthropic experiment published in 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) offers one small piece of relevant evidence. Fifty-two developers who already knew Python, but were unfamiliar with the Trio library, learned it while doing a task. A library is a set of ready-made code that Python programs can use. The group using AI averaged 50% on a quiz given immediately afterward; the group coding by hand averaged 67%. The difference in task completion time was not statistically significant: the observed speed gap could still be due to chance. This was a test of immediate understanding after one programming lesson. It cannot establish that long-term AI use makes everyone less skilled, or that a programming score predicts how people handle conflict in daily life.

There is another detail worth keeping. Not everyone who used AI learned less. In the researchers' observations, participants who asked for principles and explanations, or asked conceptual questions while writing code themselves, generally did better than those who handed both coding and debugging to AI. The researchers explicitly cautioned that these observed interaction patterns do not prove cause and effect. My practical takeaway is that the same tool can perform an action for you or help you understand why the action works. Which part you keep for yourself matters.

[A 2025 survey by Microsoft Research and collaborators](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) looked at a different group: 319 knowledge workers who used generative AI at work at least weekly, reporting 936 examples of use. Here, “critical thinking” includes deciding what the task must achieve, checking whether an AI answer has support and meets those requirements, and deciding how to use it. In their self-reports, greater confidence in AI doing a particular task was associated with reporting fewer of these judgments on that task. The thinking respondents described also shifted from producing material by hand toward verifying and integrating it. This is a survey about reported experience and an association. It did not measure a loss of actual skill, and it cannot show that confidence in AI caused one.

Taken together, these studies raise a concern. What follows is my proposal, not a result the studies tested: the smoother automation becomes, the more deliberately we should make room to practice judgments that are easy to miss on a normal day. If the event page updates automatically every time, you may rarely have to ask why two places list different dates for the same event. When they finally do, the section you are asked to take over may be the very section you never touched. There is no special malice in AI here. Any process that removes routine actions can leave this gap.

Think of training wheels on a bicycle. They help a learner get used to pedaling and steering. To learn to balance without them, the learner eventually has to feel a manageable tilt in a safe place. The shared mechanism is that assistance helps you begin, while some abilities grow only when you encounter feedback yourself. The analogy has a limit: checking an event date depends on sources, process, and other people, not muscle memory. Some high-stakes work must never be made unsafe just to create a learning opportunity. I want controlled practice with small discrepancies, not a gamble during a real delivery.

If you are new to a skill, your practice might be a brief independent judgment: write down which date you think is right and why, ask AI to look for a possible contradiction, then return to the original announcement. If you know the task well, move the practice to rarer exceptions. What if sources disagree? What if the announcement changes after your page is published? What if the fact is technically correct but the wording misleads? Practice the decision you are most likely to be responsible for at a crucial moment. There is no need to redo every step by hand.

You might reasonably ask why a busy person should spend time on unlikely problems. If an error is easy to spot, easy to undo, and unlikely to affect anyone, let the automation handle it. If a mistake may be widely shared, or you would struggle afterward to explain what your judgment rested on, a few minutes spent defining a stopping condition are a good investment. A case deserves rehearsal when you will not have the time to learn its response while panicking, not merely because it sounds frightening.

One more cost is easy to miss: plans expire. The event organizer changes where announcements appear; your webpage gains an automatic update; the old verification path may stop working. An error you used to recognize at a glance may arrive in a new form. A rule written as if it will last forever can quietly become an old answer to a new problem. Actual feedback should change the plan.

## Give the stopping condition a small test

How should you revise it? Do not begin by writing a manual for every imaginable surprise. Start with one specific task and identify where it is most likely to drift off course. Keep the event page in mind: what you owe the reader is timing information they can rely on. List conflicts readers have actually raised, ones you have encountered yourself, or ones visible in original source material. If you have no real record, begin with a small, constructed situation such as two sources giving different dates. Keep the constructed case clearly separate from an actual incident.

In [Paul Graham's 2013 essay on early startup work](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), he describes Stripe's founders installing their product for early users on the spot and the Airbnb team visiting hosts to help improve listings. These are cases from startups he knew, not proof that every product needs the same approach. They remind me that being close to use reveals where someone gets stuck. A report that says only “process completed” is unlikely to tell you whether a reader was led astray by a date that looked certain.

[A 2014 case recorded by Steve Blank](https://steveblank.com/2014/02/19/how-to-be-smarter-than-your-investors-continuous-customer-discovery/) is more specific. The Ceres Imaging team initially wanted to build drones to gather images of fields. Conversations with potential customers showed that farmers cared about useful field data and alerted the team to existing agricultural aircraft that could carry cameras. Blank wrote that the team was piloting its application at the time; his post does not demonstrate lasting commercial success. The useful lesson for revising a plan is this: when reality diverges from your proposed route, check which value you meant to preserve before racing harder along the old route.

For the event page, the value is not “I have already published a date.” The value is that readers can plan around trustworthy timing. If a correction becomes an effort to prove you had reasons for the original entry, the means have displaced the purpose. Look at what readers will do with the information. Then trace the error to the step where it entered and the signal that might have made you pause sooner. Reader feedback sometimes reveals an unexpected failure and sometimes reflects one person's misunderstanding. Check the original source and the scale of the effect before changing the process.

Two kinds of plan fail easily. One is too vague: “Handle problems promptly.” It tells you neither what counts as a problem nor who acts. The other is too complete, with a prepared response to every possible remark. That script can make you reluctant to change course when new evidence appears. A useful plan is short enough to read under pressure and specific enough that two people can recognize the same trigger.

For the imagined event page, you might write a card: when event dates conflict, stop displaying one unverified date as certain; open the organizer's original announcement and record when you viewed it; if the date is still unclear, label it unverified for readers; once confirmed, correct the page and say what changed. The card does not make the final judgment for you. It reduces the number of things you must juggle at once: defending yourself, checking, explaining, apologizing, and maintaining the page. You no longer need to vote on the order in the heat of the moment.

I especially value the pause in displaying an uncertain date, because it limits the spread of a possible error. It is not a universal move. Removing some information can leave readers more confused; in that case, retain a clear update notice and the original source. Some services need to run continuously, and taking them offline may cause more harm than marking a small error. Design the stopping condition around consequences. Caution should not shut down what remains useful.

Sometimes the hardest thing to stop is yourself. A reader sounds hostile, or you have spent hours gathering material, and admitting uncertainty stings. Writing the condition while calm takes one decision about pride off your plate when trouble comes. It does not guarantee you will follow it. So rehearse: in a copy of an old page, insert two conflicting pieces of information that both look plausible. Time yourself to see whether you pause under the rule before finding the primary source. Keep the exercise in a copy. Do not plant errors for real readers.

Do not judge the exercise by whether you can recite the steps. Look for three simpler results. Did you notice the trigger before defending yourself? Could you find a more reliable source without AI deciding the answer for you? Could you tell readers clearly what was confirmed and what remained uncertain? If any one of these fails, revise the card, or reconsider whether this task is ready for full automation. One successful rehearsal does not mean a skill is fully formed.

You can try this today. Choose a small task you repeat that occasionally goes wrong. Write one sentence: “If I see this observable signal, I will stop this step first, then check here.” Create one small discrepancy in a copy of the work and follow your sentence. If you notice the signal before rushing to explain or repair, and can state the basis for your decision after checking, the stopping condition has begun to help. If you cannot, revise the sentence before blaming your willpower.
