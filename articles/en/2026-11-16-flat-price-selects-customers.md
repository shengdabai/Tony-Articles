# A Flat Price Can Change Who Stays

> 发布日期:2026-11-16 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-16-一口价也在挑客户.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-16-flat-price-selects-customers.md)

---

A clipped summary of an article about AI application pricing drew my attention to an easily overlooked consequence: how you charge can change which customers stay, and which jobs they bring you. The price on the page can remain the same while the business underneath it changes.

When customers can handle simple tasks themselves but still need you to take care of difficult ones, a flat price can send the easy work away and keep the hard work coming. Your tools become more efficient, yet providing the service becomes more exhausting. I think this deserves a careful look from anyone building a small product, or turning a personal skill into a service.

The question goes beyond whether you charge enough. A price also encourages people to hand you particular kinds of work.

## How can the same price become less profitable?

The work that remains can change. Once we separate the steps, the puzzle becomes less mysterious.

By a flat price, I mean charging the same amount for a service within an agreed scope, without adjusting for the difficulty of each job. Both monthly subscriptions and per-item pricing can encounter this problem.

Suppose an AI assistant writes shipping emails for small shops and charges the same amount for each email. A routine notification needs product and tracking information inserted into a message. An incorrect address or contradictory order notes require checking records, asking questions, and revising the reply. Each produces one email, but the work behind it differs.

At first, the assistant receives a mixture of both. Easy jobs cost little and help cover the cost of difficult ones. A uniform price can work perfectly well if the overall economics hold up, and customers are spared the trouble of guessing what each job will cost.

Then a shop gets a ready-made template and starts sending routine notifications itself. It continues passing emails that require judgment to the assistant. That is reasonable too. The customer has not become less considerate, and the supplier has not suddenly become less competent. Both are choosing what makes sense for them.

But each email left with the supplier now involves more work on average. A cost calculated across the original mixture no longer describes the emails arriving today. Even if generating text gets cheaper, more checking, clarification, and rework can still leave the supplier losing more money.

This reasoning depends on several conditions: easy and difficult tasks must actually differ in cost; customers must be able to take the easy part out separately; and doing that part themselves must be convenient enough. Without those conditions, the customer mix need not change this way. Some people will keep paying simply to avoid managing another step, however easy that step is.

So a falling profit does not immediately establish that the price is too low. We also need to check whether the work covered by that price has changed. An email a month ago and an email today might have little in common beyond their billing unit.

There is another distinction worth catching: the number of customers can stay constant while the tasks change. The same shops may still be paying, but they have taken the easy work back. Renewal counts can look reassuring. The actual work records tell a different story.

## Where did the savings from automation go?

The savings and a changing workload can coexist. Both can be happening at once.

In a [2011 retrospective](https://sive.rs/cdbe), Derek Sivers describes spending twenty minutes turning CD Baby's routine shipping notification into a playful email that was then sent automatically with every order. This is the founder's account of what he did, without a controlled comparison of the email's commercial effects.

What interests me is the cost structure: put thought into writing it once, then use it repeatedly. Eliminating the need to rewrite every notification does not eliminate the initial work of deciding how it should sound. Those are different kinds of work.

Back at our shipping assistant, customers may be taking away precisely the part that is easiest to reuse. What remains requires checking new circumstances again and again. The assistant does not receive all the benefits of repetition it originally expected, because some of the most repeatable work no longer passes through it.

A subtler mistake is to treat the average cost of past tasks as a permanent property of the product. That average was simply a result of the particular tasks included at the time. Change the tasks in the calculation and the average changes. The number never promised to protect your profit forever.

Continuing to optimize writing speed could now mean improving the wrong step. If checking orders accounts for most of the remaining work, faster drafting can save only a small portion of it. We need to follow the main costs in the work arriving today, rather than our impression of the previous batch. A tool upgrade needs to be assessed against this current workload too.

In his [2002 Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/kahnemann-lecture.pdf), Kahneman discusses how people facing a difficult question can unknowingly answer an easier one instead. The lecture reviews research on judgment and choice; it does not study AI pricing.

Applied to these accounts, the substitution I would watch for is this: we ought to ask what it costs to complete the service, but look only at what it costs to generate the text. The second figure is easier to see and compare. The first includes finding mistakes, asking for clarification, starting over, and attempts that never succeed. Leave those out, and a low cost may simply be an incomplete account.

It resembles costing a dish in a restaurant. Ingredients are visible, but sorting, preparation, and making a returned dish again also count. If fewer orders are easy to prepare, the labor cost previously spread across all orders may no longer fit. Emails and dishes share this feature: the finished item can hide the preparation behind it. Recipes, however, are usually more stable than the exceptions arriving in email, so a restaurant's cost proportions cannot simply be transferred across.

## Couldn't we just raise the price?

It depends on who keeps paying. A higher price can cover costs and change customers' choices again.

For the shipping assistant, another across-the-board increase might first drive away customers who occasionally need help but mostly send routine notifications. Customers dealing with many complicated situations could still find the service a good bargain. The price rises, but so does the difficulty of the remaining work.

That is a conditional argument, not a prediction that every price increase will fail. Customers who strongly value convenience, or face greater difficulty switching tools, may stay after an increase. We need to examine their genuinely workable alternatives, rather than use our own costs as the only reference.

High cost does not automatically mean high value, either. An email can take a long exchange to explain without solving a more important problem for the customer. The supplier may be working flat out, but the customer need not be willing to pay in proportion to that effort. Effort belongs in the cost calculation; the price still needs a reason the customer recognizes.

In his [2013 essay](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham emphasizes that the product is only one component of the user's experience, and that early founders can compensate for an incomplete product through close attention to users. This is a practical argument drawn from early companies, rather than an experiment identifying the most profitable pricing system.

The distinction helps with our assistant. The customer may be paying for someone to investigate exceptions and recognize when the shop owner needs to decide, as well as for the email itself. If those services matter, we need to explain and deliver them well. Silently doing the extra work and expecting customers to infer why the price rose is a poor arrangement.

But turning every complication into another paid button is no easy answer. If a customer must decipher an elaborate bill before feeling comfortable pressing Send, we have moved our uncertainty onto them. I would rather explain what routine notifications cover and what happens with exceptions, so people can judge the arrangement in advance.

## Is a flat price still useful?

Yes, if you can absorb the variation. I do not want every service broken into separately priced pieces.

A flat price offers real benefits: customers know what they will pay, and suppliers have an incentive to handle the same kind of work more efficiently. CD Baby's automated email is a reminder that reusing something thoughtfully designed can preserve a service's character without requiring fresh labor every time.

The shop owner in our example might know how to send routine notifications and still choose to hand them over. Splitting the work would mean managing two processes, then explaining the history again when something unusual happens. If an integrated service removes those difficulties, the easy tasks might stay. Convenience can have value. A willing buyer is not necessarily someone who has simply not learned to do the job yet.

Nor does the arrival of difficult tasks automatically mean we should discourage those customers. They might reveal a shared problem worth solving. If emails with incorrect addresses repeatedly need manual investigation, improving the address-checking process may be more useful than attaching a separate charge to each lookup.

We need to distinguish difficulties that become smaller through improvement from those that do not. If a recurring exception can become a stable process, higher effort today may make future work easier. If every job brings a new set of requirements and almost nothing carries over, the promise needs a narrower scope. Both look complicated, but their implications for the business differ.

That accumulation needs evidence later. On the next similar problem, is finding the information quicker? Are there fewer misunderstandings? Does the customer have less to explain again? If nothing changes, we cannot keep calling every round of rework an investment in the future. A willingness to learn matters. So does admitting when a type of work is not yet producing reusable knowledge.

Some difficulties should never be promised as tasks the assistant can resolve independently. If order records are inconclusive and the owner has not confirmed the facts, faster writing cannot supply what is missing. The assistant should stop at that gap and return the questions that need confirmation. An unlimited promise can turn missing information into labor the supplier is expected to absorb.

In his [2016 shareholder letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders), Bezos argues for lighter decision processes when a choice can be reversed and the previous arrangement restored, together with quick recognition and correction of mistakes. This is a business leader's advice, whose suitability depends on the consequences. I would use it to try a clearly bounded service on a small scale and observe costs and customer choices before permanently changing the price.

Within this same shipping service, for example, routine notifications with complete information could be covered by a fixed price. Contradictory information would move a task into a pending-confirmation state, with human assistance taking over under a separate agreement. Customers would know what they were buying and what would happen when an exception arose. A smaller promise would make the part we can deliver clearer.

## Where can we start today?

Start with a job that took effort. What I plan to try is identifying the different work hidden under a uniform price, before changing the name of the charge.

One exceptionally difficult job should not stand in for an entire customer. The first setup may require organizing information that will not need organizing again. An unusual exception may not recur for a long time. We need to compare the overall work across a reasonable period of use, neither omitting difficulties nor imagining that a one-time difficulty occurs on every job.

1. Gather actual work records for a period, including completed tasks, rework, and attempts that never reached completion. Define completion first. Sending some text and resolving a problem should not quietly become the same thing.
2. Record costs by the steps actually performed: generating content, checking information, resuming work after clarification, and human corrections. Waiting is not all active labor, but record elapsed time separately too. One model bill should not stand in for the cost of the whole service.
3. Compare earlier tasks with recent ones. Has repeatable work declined? Have exceptions increased? Also check for changes in model prices, service scope, and recording methods. An increase in average cost points to questions worth investigating; it does not establish which customers caused it.
4. Design a small new trial around one recurring exception. Improve the handling process or specify the conditions for handing it back, then compare completion quality, total cost, and whether customers continue using the service. Deliver existing commitments as agreed, and explain any new boundary before it applies.

The result I most want from this check is not necessarily a price increase. Sometimes the product needs improvement so a common, difficult problem becomes easier. Sometimes the description needs changing so we stop accepting tasks with unclear boundaries. And sometimes the flat price already works and needs no change at all.

Small sets of records will have gaps, and the customers who stay in the short term may not represent future customers. I would use this exercise to discover questions, then check the actual work. Establishing which step caused a change requires more comparable tasks and conditions. A table should not be given certainty it has not earned.

This also matters for personal growth. If I turn a skill into a service and look only at how many jobs I finish, I could miss the fact that their difficulty has changed. I want the complex work I keep to help me become better at handling similar problems. For work that consumes fresh effort every time, I need to understand why I accept it.

I want AI to do more work, and I am happy to pass its efficiencies on to customers. Before promising a uniform price, though, I want to ask what kind of problem this arrangement will help me become better at solving over time.

What kind of work is your apparently simple pricing arrangement encouraging other people to leave with you?
