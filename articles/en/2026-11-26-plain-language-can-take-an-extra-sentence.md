# The Extra Sentence in the Simpler Version

> 发布日期:2026-11-26 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-26-%E7%AE%80%E6%98%8E%E7%89%88%E9%87%8C%E5%A4%9A%E5%87%BA%E6%9D%A5%E7%9A%84%E9%82%A3%E4%B8%80%E5%8F%A5.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-26-plain-language-can-take-an-extra-sentence.md)

---

When I edit, I want to know how much work the words I cut actually save the reader. Suppose I ask AI to shorten an explanation and it keeps the formula and the answer but drops the step that’s easiest to get wrong. A reader checks their own answer, finds it doesn’t match, and has to guess where they went wrong. My editing suggestion is to look for the gap that changes the answer. Sometimes it’s worth an extra sentence.

In a reading study published in 2014, the version with the highest comprehension scores also had the highest average word count among the selected passages. Forty-eight students whose first language was Spanish read English news paragraphs. They got an average of 81% of the true-or-false comprehension questions right for the versions simplified for beginners and 72% for the originals. [Reading experiment](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1031308.pdf)

The higher average word count describes the passages selected for the study. The differences in word count were not statistically significant, meaning these data fall short of establishing a systematic difference in length between versions. Vocabulary, sentence structure, and the links between ideas also changed together, so the study can’t tell us which kind of edit accounted for the difference in scores. Back to the draft in front of me. How do I tell which sentence I can cut and which deserves a few more words? [Reading experiment](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1031308.pdf)

## Cutting words can leave the reader with the work

I’d start by looking at what the cut was doing in the original. Finding a piece of reading already takes some attention. Making sense of it takes more. When the writer leaves something out, the reader may have to stop and guess what the sentence means. If two interpretations fit and lead to different answers, the reader has to choose one on the writer’s behalf.

Suppose I’m writing for someone new to cycling and want to explain how to find their average speed for a round trip along the same route. It’s 10 kilometers each way. Assume they ride at a steady 20 kilometers per hour on the way out and 10 on the way back, with no stops. The tempting calculation is to add the two speeds and divide by two, which gives 15 kilometers per hour. I could make the explanation very short by saying to divide total distance by total time and giving the answer of about 13.3 kilometers per hour.

We have a formula and an answer. What’s missing? The two legs took different amounts of time. The ride out took half an hour and the ride back took an hour. Divide the full 20 kilometers by the hour and a half it took to ride them and the answer checks out. Adding the sentence about the two times also explains why the first calculation came out too high. The rider spent longer at the lower speed.

When would averaging the two speeds give the right answer? Change the example so the rider spends half an hour at each speed. They cover 10 kilometers on one leg and 5 on the other, for exactly 15 kilometers in an hour. Back in the original problem, the distances are equal at 10 kilometers each, and the times differ. If the short draft says only that the two legs were the same, the reader has to decide what was the same. I’d keep the 10 kilometers each way and the two times that follow from that condition.

If I need to cut words, I’ll cut the enthusiastic opening to the explanation. I’ll keep the two times and put them before the answer. A reader who has already come up with 15 might look at the formula again and still plug in the same numbers. Give them half an hour and an hour and they have something they can check step by step, including where to go back when their answer differs.

The study at the start tested how accurately students answered true-or-false questions after reading short news paragraphs. It gives me an idea for editing. I’d look at what a shorter draft has combined, then see whether the reader can still tell how the ideas relate. If both cuts work, keep both. If one leaves the reader with little more than an answer to memorize, put a sentence back there. [Methods and results](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1031308.pdf)

## Headings help you find it. Sentences show how it fits.

I like opening an article and being able to see at a glance what it covers. A heading like How to Calculate It tells the reader where to look for the formula. Once they get there, they still need to see how half an hour and an hour relate to the two speeds, and which quantities to add together. The sentences have to do that work.

In a study published in 2013, Lorch, Lemarié, and Chen had college students read informational texts. In the first experiment, the same information about the topics appeared either in headings or in a sentence at the start of each section telling readers what it would cover. Readers with headings remembered more subtopics. The two groups showed no reliable difference in recall of factual details. In another experiment, readers were asked to write an outline. Both kinds of cues helped them include more topics than when there were no cues, and there was no reliable difference between headings and opening sentences. I’d use a heading to help someone find the calculation, then check the sentences around it separately. [Two reading experiments](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/archivos/ed2013v19n2a2.pdf)

In the cycling passage, I’d pair each speed with the time spent riding at that speed. Then I’d use a word that signals a reason to explain why the calculation needs both times. By linking words I mean words like because and so, which show how two statements fit together. For example, because we’re calculating the average speed for the whole trip, we need to count the time spent riding out and the time spent riding back. The reader can see why both times go into the calculation.

In a 2000 experiment, Shuichi Ozono had Japanese university students read short science passages in English. For two of the three passages, answers to a question about cause and effect differed reliably across versions that handled linking words differently. In those two passages, the version without an explicit signal of cause and effect had the lowest share of correct answers. The questions about contrast tested whether readers understood how one idea contrasted with the one before it, a relationship that *however* can signal. For each passage, the share of correct answers to the question about contrast showed no reliable difference across versions. That makes me want to fix the one causal link in my draft that readers are most likely to misread. Before making the wording stand out on the page, I’d check whether what comes after because actually gives the reason. [Experiment on linking words](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/arele/13/0/13_KJ00007108328/_pdf/-char/en)

## A clear link can still leave something missing

Suppose a recipe says the dough has finished rising, so you can shape it. The first part gives the reason. The second tells you what to do next. Someone who makes that bread regularly may know how to tell when the dough is ready, while a first-time baker might get stuck there. If the recipe uses the size of the dough as its cue, I’d say when to measure it against and how much bigger it needs to get. The recipe needs a way to tell when to act. The cycling passage needs a step in the calculation. In both cases, the reader sees how the statements are linked and still needs one more step to carry on.

Back on the bike, someone who knows how to get time from distance and speed can work it out from the two speeds and the distance of each leg. A beginner who hasn’t connected 20 kilometers per hour with riding 10 kilometers might read that the ride out was faster and therefore took less time, and still only know which leg took longer. Add a sentence. At that speed, the rider covers 20 kilometers in an hour, so half the distance takes half an hour. The reader now has enough to work out the return leg. I’d give them that first step and leave the next one for them to do on paper.

In a study published in 2011, McNamara, Ozuru, and Floyd had 65 fourth graders each read four passages, including stories and science texts. Prior knowledge means what readers already know before opening the text. The researchers measured it with a general knowledge test. On the multiple-choice questions, prior knowledge was more strongly related to scores for the science texts than for the stories. The researchers revised references, added linking words, moved information, and made other changes. For instance, they replaced pronouns with specific names or descriptions so readers could tell who or what a sentence meant. Scores on the multiple-choice questions for the science texts showed no reliable improvement. The researchers suggested that children tackling difficult material may also need more explanatory information. That fits a question I want to ask when editing. Does the reader need a sentence that points the way, or one that actually explains how to calculate something or gives the reason behind a claim? [Fourth-grade reading study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1070456.pdf)

Some readers would find me long-winded. They already know the two legs took different amounts of time and have come to look up the formula. Making them read through the whole thing would waste their time. A reader might even want to use the example for practice and work out the half hour on their own. For these readers, I’d make the formula easy to spot and give the worked calculation its own paragraph. An experienced reader can go straight to the formula. Someone practicing can work it out and check afterward. A beginner has somewhere to look when they get stuck.

If the exercise is specifically about calculating time from distance and speed, giving away the half hour and the hour in the question does the very step the reader came to practice. I’d keep the two speeds and the distance of each leg in the question, then put the times in the solution below. If the passage is meant to teach the method to someone seeing the problem for the first time, I’d work out the ride out in the main text and leave them the ride back. Where I put that sentence depends on which step I want the reader to do.

If the explanation is missing from the original draft, I’d check the material first. In the cycling case, the necessary information is there and I can do the calculation. Now suppose a product write-up records that a page changed and that fewer people asked for help afterward, and the short draft connects the two with so. I’d go back and check which requests for help went down and what else changed around the same time. If the material establishes only the sequence, I’ll replace so with afterward and describe what happened in order.

## Let AI try reading it. You decide what to add.

When you have an original draft and a version AI has shortened, I’d suggest a small editing check. Pick a passage readers are meant to use to calculate a result, explain a reason, or make a decision. Save both drafts. For an everyday edit you’re doing on your own, the first two steps complete the check. You can add the third for an important explanation or a draft where a misunderstanding could lead to a wrong decision. If you make a change, edit one place this round so you can go back and see what that sentence changed.

1. Write a short question tied to what the reader needs to do. Work out and write down the answer from the original draft or from material you can verify. For the cycling passage, you could ask why 15 kilometers per hour is too high. The answer needs to say that the return leg was slower and took longer. Let the reader look at the passage while answering. The point is to see whether the writing helps them find the reason. Looking things up is part of the task.
2. Start by giving AI only the short draft and the question. Keep the original draft and your answer to yourself. You can ask it directly: “Answer using only this short draft. Point to the original sentences you used and mark each step you worked out yourself. If the draft leaves out a condition you need, say what’s missing.” Then check its response against the original and the answer you wrote. If AI flags a gap, do the calculation yourself or check the underlying material. Add the sentence you can verify that would change how the reader answers.

   Suppose AI comes straight back with 13.3. I’d still look at how it got an hour and a half. If the short draft already gives the distances and speeds, and working out the time from those quantities is something the reader is meant to know, I can leave that step to them. If it gives only the two speeds and the total distance, with nothing about how far each leg was or whether the distances were equal, AI may have assumed a condition the problem never stated. I’d put the equal-distance condition back in. Even when the number happens to be right, I’d check it against the sentences the answer used.

3. For an optional check with real readers, give the amended version and the original short version to separate target readers with about the same familiarity with the topic. Have each answer the same question with their version open and point to the sentence they used. If you can find only one reader, start with the short draft and record how they answer and where they pause. Then show them the sentence you added. Getting it right the second time may partly reflect having tried it once, so use the result as a clue for revision. If the reader can repeat the formula but still can’t explain why the slower return leg makes the simple average too high, I’d check whether I put the two times where they can find them.

When AI gives me a long list of suggestions, I pick one I can tie to how the reader answered. If it suggests another heading, I check whether the reader struggled to find the passage or found it and couldn’t connect the ideas. If it suggests adding background, I check whether that sentence helps the reader get one more step right. For things they could work out themselves, how much I spell out depends on whether they came to look something up or to practice doing it.

In the last passage you asked AI to shorten, what information disappeared that could lead a reader to a different answer? There may be none. Answer the question once with the short draft in front of you and underline the sentences you used. Then go back to the original and check any condition you relied on that the short draft leaves unstated.
