# Discovery Is Evidence Now

> 发布日期:2026-07-26 · [中文](../zh/2026-07-26-被发现正在变成证据工程.md) | [English](../en/2026-07-26-discovery-is-evidence-now.md)

---

For a long time, online growth had a comforting shape.

Find the keyword. Build the page. Publish the article. Get the backlink. Wait for ranking. Turn ranking into traffic. Turn traffic into users. Turn users into money.

It was never as simple as that, of course, but the mental model was simple. The internet looked like a giant set of doors. Search was the hallway. A good keyword was the sign above the door. If the sign was large enough and visible enough, people would walk in.

That model is not dead.

But it is no longer enough.

The deeper change is not that Google added AI summaries, or that users ask chatbots questions, or that social feeds have become another discovery layer. Those are surface symptoms. The real change is this: before a person reaches your homepage, the world has already told a story about you.

An AI summary may have framed the category. A comparison page may have defined your competitors. A forum thread may have created suspicion. A short video may have given someone an impression before they even know your name. A newsletter may have borrowed your positioning and compressed it into one sentence. Then, only after all of that, the person arrives at your site.

The homepage is no longer always the beginning of the relationship.

Often it is the verification room.

## The Click Is Too Late

Today's notes included a sharp reminder from the world of SEO and go-to-market work: the old keyword-first habit can create a lot of movement without creating much truth.

Keyword tools are useful, but they lag behind reality. New phrases appear before datasets become clean. Ranking still matters, but ranking does not guarantee attention. Traffic still matters, but traffic can be noisy, expensive, or even poisonous if it comes from people who were never going to become good users.

The most dangerous part is not that these metrics are false.

It is that they are partial.

They show the visible part of the journey. They show the click, the session, the page view, the conversion event. But a human decision often forms before the click. In B2B, education, software, consulting, and many trust-heavy categories, the buyer may have already asked friends, read reviews, compared alternatives, searched the brand name, watched a video, asked an AI assistant, and silently decided what kind of company you are.

By the time analytics says "new visitor," the real conversation may be halfway over.

Pew Research Center studied Google search behavior in March 2025 and found that users clicked a traditional search result link in 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared, compared with 15% of visits where no AI summary appeared.[^1] That number should not be treated as a universal law for every industry. But it points toward a direction: the answer layer is moving upward. More interpretation happens before the website.

Google's own language points the same way. In its AI Mode update from Google I/O 2025, Google described Search as moving "beyond information to intelligence," with AI Mode helping users compare, plan, reason, and take next steps inside Search itself.[^2]

That does not mean websites stop mattering.

It means websites must stop pretending they are the only place where trust is built.

## Do Not Sell the Engine

One line from the notes stayed with me: people do not buy the engine; they buy the destination.

Builders forget this constantly.

We describe the dashboard, the model, the automation, the integration, the workflow, the architecture, the number of templates, the cleverness of the stack. We think we are explaining value. Often we are only describing machinery.

The customer is trying to get somewhere.

They want Monday morning back. They want fewer status meetings. They want a proposal that does not sound generic. They want their small team to look less fragile. They want to stop losing promising leads because follow-up is slow. They want to trust that the thing will work when they are tired, busy, and not in the mood to learn one more tool.

The old SEO brain asks: what keyword has volume?

The better question is: what situation makes this person finally care?

There is a big difference between a page that targets a phrase and a page that gives evidence to a person in a situation.

A keyword page says, "Here is a term you searched."

An evidence page says, "Here is the problem you are actually facing. Here is how we understand it. Here is where we are strong. Here is where we are not for you. Here is how we compare with the alternatives you are already considering. Here is what will change in your day if this works."

That second page may still rank.

But ranking is no longer its only job.

It also teaches humans. It teaches future customers. It teaches reviewers. It teaches community members. Increasingly, it also teaches AI systems what you are, who you serve, and where you do not fit.

This is why comparison pages, alternative pages, use-case pages, customer stories, documentation, pricing notes, and even public changelogs matter more than they look. They are not just content assets. They are fragments of public evidence.

If those fragments contradict each other, people get confused.

If they are vague, AI systems summarize you badly.

If they are self-centered, customers feel the old problem: you are in love with your product, but not yet serious about their life.

## Evidence Engineering

I keep wanting to call the new discipline evidence engineering.

Not content marketing. That phrase has become too broad. Not SEO, because the job is wider than search ranking. Not branding, because the work is too concrete to stay at the level of feeling.

Evidence engineering asks a more practical question:

If a serious person tries to understand whether we are worth trusting, what public evidence will they find?

This shifts the work in several ways.

First, you begin with the customer, not the channel. An independent developer, a team lead, a teacher, a founder, and a procurement buyer do not gather trust in the same places. One person reads GitHub issues. Another reads peer recommendations. Another watches a demo. Another wants an invoice, a security answer, and a credible comparison with the incumbent.

The channel is not the strategy.

The customer's trust path is the strategy.

Second, you define your negative customer. This is uncomfortable, but it is necessary. If you chase everyone, you will attract people who drain support, distort feedback, and punish you for not being the thing you never meant to be. Bad-fit traffic is not a vanity problem. It can teach the builder the wrong lesson.

Third, you make each asset carry a distinct burden of proof.

The homepage should make the basic promise clear quickly.

The use-case page should show a real situation.

The comparison page should explain trade-offs without pretending every competitor is stupid.

The pricing page should reduce anxiety.

The documentation should prove that the product has a body, not just a slogan.

The public examples should show what changes after someone uses it.

The same logic applies to an individual, not only a company. A writer, teacher, consultant, developer, or creator is also discovered through scattered evidence. A stranger rarely believes your self-description first. They believe the trail: the essays, the shipped work, the comments, the demos, the way you answer objections, the way your claims survive contact with detail.

In the AI age, this trail becomes even more important because the cost of producing claims has collapsed.

Anyone can say they are strategic.

Anyone can generate a polished landing page.

Anyone can publish a thread that sounds like a framework.

So the scarce thing is no longer polished explanation. The scarce thing is proof that survives inspection.

## What Ordinary Builders Can Do

This sounds like a company problem, but I think it is also a personal discipline.

If you are building anything, stop asking only, "How do I get more traffic?"

Ask:

Where does the right person already go when they are confused?

What do they compare before they trust?

What words do they use before they know the category name?

What fear stops them from trying?

What kind of bad customer should I politely refuse?

What public artifact would make a serious stranger say, "This person understands my problem"?

AI does not remove the need for these questions. It makes them more urgent.

Because AI makes production cheap, but judgment expensive.

Because AI makes pages easy, but trust harder.

Because AI can help people summarize you before you enter the room.

The lazy response is to produce more: more posts, more landing pages, more tools, more keywords, more summaries, more versions of the same promise. That may work for a while. Noise can still create motion.

But durable discovery is not the same as noise.

Durable discovery happens when the right person meets the right evidence at the right moment and feels the quiet click of recognition: this is for me, and they know why.

That is not a keyword trick.

It is not an AI hack.

It is the old work of understanding another human being, made more demanding by new machines.

The future of being found will belong less to people who shout the loudest, and more to people who leave the clearest evidence.

Not evidence that they are impressive.

Evidence that they understand the destination.
