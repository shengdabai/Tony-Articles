# Two Indexes for the Same Pre-Meeting Briefing

> 发布日期:2026-11-27 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-27-%E5%90%8C%E4%B8%80%E4%BB%BD%E4%BC%9A%E5%89%8D%E6%9D%90%E6%96%99%EF%BC%8C%E4%B8%A4%E4%BB%BD%E7%9B%AE%E5%BD%95.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-27-two-indexes-for-one-meeting.md)

---

When a meeting takes up an issue, I'd start by asking the person responsible to explain what they plan to do next. To help them name a concrete step, you can give them a question before the meeting and point them to the passage with the evidence they need. Someone new to the discussion may need to catch up on the backstory first.

I'd like to bring that practice into the reading people do before a meeting by trying two indexes for the same briefing. Put one in chronological order for people who need the backstory. Organize the other around the decision at hand so people who already know what happened can jump to the evidence they need. Both indexes point to passages in the same document. At the meeting, people can start from what they read and discuss the same decision.

How do you make the reading order clear? A Spanish reading study offers an example. It used two passages. One told the story of how Greece gained independence in chronological order. The other went through the reasons Asia's rice supply was at risk. In the versions with cues, the researchers explained the structure up front so students would know whether to follow the order of events or look for each cause in turn. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10310-5)

## Where does one index fall short?

People come with different questions. When you write a briefing, putting events in the order they happened can feel natural. You remember what came up last time, who you asked afterward, and where things got held up. Writing it all down helps you make sense of it. Among the people who open the document, though, some want to know how things got here. Others know the history and only need to see what options are still open.

Suppose a community book swap is deciding whether to try an evening slot in addition to its Saturday midday hours. Some people have asked ahead of time whether they can come later because they can't make it at noon. The person who draws up the rota remembers those requests and first needs to find out whether a volunteer can handle closing. Someone who's just joined may have no idea how the midday session works. If everyone jumps straight into the rota, that person might assume the current opening hours are up for debate too. They're all reading the same document in good faith, with different jobs to do.

The person running the meeting can walk everyone through the story from the beginning. The trouble is that everyone moves at the speaker's pace. By the end, the people who knew the backstory have heard it again, and someone new may only just have worked out that the discussion is about adding a slot. I'd like participants to find the question they need to answer while they read the briefing. Then when it's the rota organizer's turn to explain their plan, they can start with the shift that still needs covering.

In the study with the Greece and rice passages, two age groups of Spanish secondary school students wrote summaries after reading, with the passages out of view. Both groups wrote better summaries of both passages when the versions they read included organizational cues. The researchers scored the number of main ideas and how those ideas were organized. They were measuring how the students retold what they'd just read. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-022-10310-5)

If the briefing has a general heading like *Progress Update*, I'd spell out the two routes below it. Follow the timeline to see where the proposal for evening hours came from. Go straight to the decision to see whether to try it and what's missing from the rota. Readers can tell at a glance where to start and where to go back if they need to fill in a gap.

## How would you arrange them?

Start with a document everyone can consult. A cookbook can have a table of contents organized by breakfast, lunch, and dinner, and an index at the back organized by ingredient. The person making breakfast uses the contents. The person with a piece of pumpkin to use up checks the index. They might land on the same page. The ingredient index gets you to the recipe. You still need the recipe to see how much pumpkin to use and how long to bake it. The two indexes in a briefing serve the same purpose. Each offers a way to find the details in the document itself.

The book swap's document could have sections on the midday arrangements, requests to come later, the evening staffing status, and the proposed trial. Read it in order of events and you start with the midday arrangements, see why evening hours came up, and finish at the proposal the group needs to decide on. A new volunteer can come away knowing what's already happening and what's still up for discussion. When someone says they are short a person in the evening, the volunteer knows which slot they're talking about.

Start with the decision and the first item is whether to try the evening slot. Under it, point directly to the section with the requests to come later and the one with the evening staffing status. The person organizing the rota can open the second section first, see who's committed and which hours still need coverage, then check whether the requests are specific enough to justify a trial. The index can put staffing first. The shared text should still spell out the proposed trial and the current staffing situation, including what's been confirmed and what still needs confirming.

When entries in both indexes cover the same point, they should lead to the same passage. When there's a staffing update, revise that passage. Receiving requests for a later slot and knowing how many people would show up in the evening are two different things. The text should make that clear. Writing a short briefing for each audience and sending both off can work, too. If the rota changes on Friday, though, someone has to remember both places where it was described. With a shared passage, you update it once and both indexes lead to the revised account.

Some headings look helpful at first glance. *Strong Evening Demand* might seem like a tidy label for the requests to come later. Then a reader has to go back and find out who decided demand was strong. I'd keep the heading close to what the material actually establishes and put the question for discussion beside it. The index can point readers to the relevant passage. The conclusion still has to hold up when people ask about the evidence.

## Does finding it settle the question?

Someone still has to explain the reasoning. In a paper published in 1994, Icy K. B. Lee analyzed scores from 232 Hong Kong secondary school girls who had read a relatively difficult passage in English. They were less-skilled readers learning English as a second language. For that passage and test, headings and sentences previewing the content helped students identify the stated main ideas. Inference questions asked students to use what the passage gave them to work out something it left unstated. None of the cues tested produced a statistically significant improvement on those questions. Here, statistically significant means that if the cues had no effect on scores, the test would put the chance of seeing a score difference this pronounced or more below the cutoff the study used. [Original study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED386051.pdf)

So I'd ask two things when someone tries the briefing. First, where does the document describe the evening staffing status? Finding the passage counts as a correct answer even if nobody has confirmed a shift. Second, the requests to come later still stand and evening staffing is still unconfirmed. What could you agree to today? The reader might suggest checking who is available first or propose a trial with clear conditions. What matters is that they can state their reasons and say what still needs confirming. Reading out the heading won't answer the second question.

In the trial reading, I'd pay particular attention to the passage about closing. People have asked whether they can come later, which gives the group a reason to consider a trial. If nobody can handle closing, the trial has to wait until that condition is met. A reader needs to hold both points in mind. The requests still count, and staffing still limits what the team can do. A heading like *Demand and Staffing* looks as if it covers everything. Someone may still have to spell out the relationship in the meeting.

Julia Knoepke and her colleagues asked German primary school children and adults to read or listen to pairs of German sentences and judge whether the two sentences went together. In both tasks, children and adults generally made more errors judging causal links that involved a contrast than links that followed the expected outcome. In a contrast, the first sentence sets up an expectation about what would normally happen, and the second gives a different outcome. The researchers tested links between short sentences. In a briefing trial, I'd ask readers which new information would change their initial judgment, then check whether the document makes that information clear. [Original study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/processing-of-positivecausal-and-negativecausal-coherence-relations-in-primary-school-children-and-adults-a-test-of-the-cumulative-cognitive-complexity-approach-in-german/DC3299ABA5F0FED2343B11463433F0D3)

When two people come away with different views, ask each to point to the evidence they're using. One may be worried that nobody will be there to close. The other may be able to explain why it makes sense to ask who is willing to take a shift first. Those views can be discussed together. If both readers have mistaken requests for commitments to attend, go back and clarify the facts in that paragraph. Finding the same paragraph is one step in the trial. Once you can see which facts both readers agree on, the meeting has a chance to address where they actually differ.

## Is it worth doing for every meeting?

For a short item, one route is enough. If the book swap is posting this week's hours, the opening and closing times should be right there when people open the document. Two indexes would make people choose how to read before they can see what one sentence could tell them. Even a discussion may come down to confirming a single shift. Put that shift on the first line. An extra index needs a real problem to solve.

When rearranging a briefing, I'd also look for anyone who might end up taking extra steps. In a study published in 2026, Vered Vaknin-Nusbaum and Einat Nevo studied 166 third and fourth graders at two schools in northern Israel. Each pupil read two original passages that directly described how animals live and two adaptations that put that kind of information into a story. The four passages covered different animals. Pupils answered multiple-choice questions after each passage. Across the full sample, the difference in comprehension scores was not statistically significant. This comparison leaves open which format would score higher on average and whether the two formats are equally effective. When the researchers grouped students by their scores on the descriptive texts, the lower-scoring group did better with the story versions. The group the researchers called typical readers did better with the original descriptive versions. The story versions were also longer. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-026-10791-8)

I'd pay particular attention to how those groups were formed. The researchers used the scores on the descriptive texts to form the groups, then used those scores in the comparison itself. Someone who happened to do poorly on this set of descriptive texts could score closer to their usual level with a different set. That possibility makes me reluctant to use the group results to label readers. At work, the same person who knows the rota inside out today may need the full backstory when they take on something else tomorrow. I'd label each route by the job at hand and let people choose for themselves. [Original study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-026-10791-8)

Someone might argue that everyone needs to read in the same order so they start from the same premises. I agree that sometimes this matters more. Say the trial depends on a condition that's easy to overlook. The closer can take over the shift only if the handoff has been completed. That condition belongs beside the decision, whichever way a reader comes in. If the document is short enough that putting the condition at the top explains the whole situation, put it at the top. Save people the detour.

I'd decide whether to write two indexes by asking one question. Does someone need to catch up on the backstory before they can contribute, while someone else needs just one piece of evidence before they can offer a view? If you can't name those two jobs, keep the index you already have. If you can, see whether both routes can use the same main text. When two indexes point to separate passages that say the same thing, the next schedule change may cost you as much time in proofreading as the extra index saved in reading.

## How can I try it with a draft?

Use the material for your next discussion. Pick a draft that has some backstory and a real decision to make. Start with a page or a short section. Once readers can actually click through to the places you name, you can see whether the change helps.

1. Write down one decision the group needs to make and one condition readers have to know. At the book swap, the decision is whether to try an evening slot, and the condition is that someone can take over the shift at handoff and handle closing. Find the passages in your draft that support the discussion and give them descriptive headings. Mark guesses and items awaiting confirmation where they appear, so the index doesn't sound more certain than the text.
2. Give AI a draft you can use for the exercise and ask it to arrange the existing headings into two reading orders. One should let someone new explain how far things have got. The other should let someone making the decision find the evidence and the conditions still to be confirmed. Ask it to identify the passage each entry leads to and mark any passage it can't find. Then follow every entry yourself. Read the paragraph to see whether it actually contains the information the heading suggests.
3. Ask one person who needs the backstory and one person who'll help make the decision to try reading it. Have each choose a route and tell you where they stopped and what they'd be ready to say or do. Note where they had to go back to answer, and watch for anyone treating something unconfirmed as a fact. The aim is to see how these two readers use this draft and use their answers and backtracking to find places to revisit.
4. Fix one place where someone had to go back. If a reader kept looking for the rota, move that passage up in the decision index. If they reached it and still couldn't explain the condition for opening, clarify what's been confirmed in the main text. When something changes, update the shared passage, then follow each index again to check that it still takes readers to the passage they need.

At the meeting, you can invite someone who's already found the evidence to speak first. Ask them to point to the passage they read and start with one question they still have about it.
