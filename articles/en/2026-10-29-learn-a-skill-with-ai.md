# The Best AI Course Teaches Something Beyond AI

> 发布日期:2026-10-29 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-29-AI%20课最该教的，是%20AI%20之外的东西.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-29-learn-a-skill-with-ai.md)

---

When you spend time studying AI, what skill are you actually learning? My short answer: name the skill before you open the tool. If you want to explain a product clearly, practice explaining a product clearly. If you want to judge a research finding, practice judging a research finding. The model can help at any point, but “getting familiar with AI” is too vague to serve as the learning goal. This may sound like a dispute over wording. It changes how you choose tasks, judge results, and handle the next problem when no ready-made answer is waiting.

### Mistake one: treating tool fluency as a skill learned

A machine-generated summary of a recorded discussion about AI use contains a useful reminder: spend less time perfecting fixed tricks and more time using AI to learn something you could not do before. I would take that one step further: **you should be able to name the subject you are learning without mentioning AI.** “I can switch models” and “I have saved lots of prompts” describe operations. “I can write a product page that a stranger understands and wants to try” describes a skill. Operations matter, of course. They need a job to serve.

Why insist on the distinction? An interface can change and take its familiar buttons with it. Your judgment about a field may survive. Being able to write a product page means knowing what the reader is trying to solve, which promise needs evidence, and where someone might give up. AI can produce fluent copy. You still have to decide whether it has identified the right problem. Fluency with the tool gets you started faster; knowledge of the subject helps you decide where to go.

That is also why “just use it more” is only half an answer. You might generate a fresh page every day and learn the interface extremely well while avoiding the same difficulty each time: you do not know what a good page looks like. Eventually the output gets faster, but your choices about the audience, evidence, and final standard still depend on a hunch. It is rather like learning to cook while paying attention only to a new stove's menu. The stove can reduce effort; it cannot taste the food for you. Cooking and writing differ in how quickly they give feedback, so the kitchen is no evidence for a theory of writing. The shared mechanism is narrower: decide which judgment you mean to practice before optimizing the tool.

I would rewrite the learning goal. Replace “learn AI writing” with “learn to explain to a new reader why a product is worth using.” The first phrase makes the tool the main character. The second gives us something we can check. By all means, use AI to draft, revise, and format. Before each session, though, decide whether this round is for practicing explanation, checking evidence, or understanding the reader. A single task may involve all three, but choose the one that is most likely to trip you up this time. Otherwise a polished result can conceal the weak spot you came to work on.

This choice has a cost. If you concentrate on explanation, you may not get to design today. If you concentrate on evidence, the page may still look plain. At least you will know where to resume next time. When AI fills every gap at once, you receive a generally good page but have trouble telling which part you could have done yourself. The more specific the learning goal, the easier it is to look back and tell whether you entered a new field or merely found a new way to do familiar work.

### Mistake two: counting delivery speed as learning speed

A better-looking result does not automatically mean a more capable author. That is a disappointing sentence, perhaps, but it lets us evaluate AI fairly. In a [2023 working paper on professional writing](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf), Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang assigned short occupational writing tasks to 444 college-educated professionals. On the second task, the group offered access to ChatGPT spent about 37% less time than the control group, while quality scores from evaluators who did not know the group assignment rose by about 0.45 standard deviations. A standard deviation measures how spread out the scores are; this figure does not mean a 45% increase in quality. The experiment measured immediate performance on short writing assignments. It did not follow people's writing ability for years or test every kind of work.

That is a welcome result. For those tasks, producing better work more quickly is a real benefit. The paper also reports that, among participants assigned access to ChatGPT, 68% said they submitted its initial draft without editing it. The authors conclude that, in this experiment, much of the productivity gain came from the tool taking over part of the effort. That is no accusation of laziness. The experiment rewarded good, fast submissions, and participants responded sensibly. Trouble begins when we enter that submission score into the ledger as evidence that the human's own skill has grown.

Suppose you are writing a public page for a hypothetical reminder tool. AI gives you a clear headline, a list of benefits, and button copy. You can put that page in front of readers right away; you need not rewrite it merely to prove how hard you worked. The learning opportunity lies elsewhere. Can you say whom the headline addresses, what fact each promise rests on, and why a reader would click at that point? If you cannot, the page may still be good. You simply lack evidence for the separate claim, “I have learned to write product pages.” Keeping separate records for delivery and learning respects the finished work without claiming the tool's performance as your own.

I suggest spending some of the time saved on two very small sheets of paper. This is my practice proposal; the writing experiment did not test it as a way to learn. Before drafting, write down who the page is for, what readers already know, and the one action you hope they will take. After seeing the draft, write down which line might mislead them, where evidence is missing, what you changed, and why. If you cannot answer, ask AI to list information that may be missing, then check it yourself. This may make a page slower to finish. If delivery is the only urgent goal, slowing down may be the wrong tradeoff. If learning is also a goal, these notes show where the practice happened.

The two sheets protect you from hindsight as well. Once a finished page looks convincing, it is easy to feel that you knew how to write it all along. Keep your prediction from before the draft, and you can see what you got wrong and what the machine quietly supplied. On the next similar task, look at your own record before deciding which choices to reuse. You will be accumulating judgments that you can revise, instead of a collection of sentences that happened to work once.

### Mistake three: assuming more repetitions mean deeper learning

Doing the same task again and again can feel great. Your prompts become smoother, the results look more like what you enjoy, and the sense of progress is real. Familiar problems can also fool you. Have you learned a relationship that will help on a new problem, or memorized a reliable route to the old answer? Practice need not be mysterious. It needs a task that can expose an error and feedback specific enough to help you correct it.

The claim that hours of practice determine performance deserves a brake. In their [2019 replication of a study of violinists](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2019/10/03143050/Macnamara-Maitra-2019.pdf), Brooke Macnamara and Megha Maitra studied 39 student violinists. Accumulated solo practice was related to performance, but the researchers did not reproduce the original finding that the groups' skill levels fell into a complete ranking by accumulated hours. Participants also had to recall past practice, and the sample covered a particular group of music students. This gives us a reason not to treat “I spent a lot of time” as a sufficient explanation of improvement. It does not show that practice is unimportant or prescribe a number of hours for everybody else.

For the rest of us, a practical test is to change the problem. In a [2016 study of learning and transfer](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full), Gerdien van Eersel and colleagues compared rereading with retrieval practice plus feedback and with rereading followed by focused exposure to the key facts. Retrieval practice means closing the material, trying to answer a question yourself, and then checking the answer. In the second experiment, 54 psychology undergraduates answered questions that applied ideas in a different knowledge domain a week later. Their average proportions correct were 0.40, 0.64, and 0.53, respectively, in the order of the three methods just named. These were particular texts and questions, not a measurement of every learnable skill.

The middle result is easy to overlook. Students who saw the key information separately scored higher than those who only reread. The group that answered questions and received feedback scored higher still. This suggests that answering for yourself may have added something, but it does not prove that answering alone caused the difference. The researchers judged that differences in time spent practicing were unlikely to explain the results. Yet the groups also differed in how much time passed between repeated encounters with the same key information, and the researchers could not fully rule out an effect from that difference. My practice proposal is to find the important information, answer without looking, and then check your judgment on new material.

Return to the product page. The following check on a new page is my practice proposal based on the learning study; the study did not test whether it works the same way for product pages. Do not immediately ask AI to explain every edit. Close the answer and make three judgments of your own: What is the reader's most important problem? Which promise has the weakest support? Which paragraph could you remove to make the page clearer? Then use a new public description of a product with a different purpose and answer the same three questions. If you can still spot those things on new material, you have a little more evidence of learning. If you cannot, bring the place where you got stuck to AI and ask it to challenge your reasoning. This is no formal exam. It may be an inexpensive way to find a blind spot that repeated admiration of one finished page might hide.

Changing the problem does not mean making it impossibly hard. Check whether the same judgment carries across a modest difference. If you spotted an overbroad promise on the first page, can you catch one on another product's page? If the goal, audience, and material all change at once, failure tells you less about the original skill. Change one condition, notice where you stumble, and only then decide whether to move farther away. Learning needs variation, but you also need to know what varied.

### Mistake four: letting AI be the only judge

There is another trap: taking the model's praise as outside feedback. Given a clear standard, it can flag a winding headline or an unsupported claim. It can even play an opposing reader. But if it casually invents the standard and then gives its own work a high score, the process may feel wonderfully smooth while heading in the wrong direction. If your reason for writing is to help readers understand something and act, real readers' responses need a place in the judgment. Even one small reading session can reveal a more useful problem than a long string of “excellent” assessments.

In [a 2009 account of his time at MIPS](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/), Steve Blank describes a mistake of his own. He collected potential customers' requests for two ways of ordering bytes in a chip, meaning which byte of a value comes first when it is stored in memory, and recommended supporting both. It looked relatively easy on the hardware side; the software team pointed out that it would complicate the work that followed. Blank's later lesson was that collecting feature requests is insufficient. You also have to test whether the whole solution solves a customer's problem and whether someone will adopt it. This is one entrepreneur's retrospective account, not a controlled experiment about all products. I take from it a question about feedback: what, exactly, is this response answering?

The product page has the same issue. “Nice headline” is feedback about appearance. “I still don't know what this tool solves” is feedback about understanding. “I see its use but cannot find what to do next” is feedback about action. Do not combine all three into a single satisfaction score. The first may call for polish. The other two may require a structural change or a rethink of the promise itself. AI can simulate these questions and help you find weaknesses early. A page meant for people ultimately has to be judged by how people read and use it.

Of course, one confused reader is no reason to tear down the whole page. Look at the point where that person stopped. Was a word unfamiliar, or could they not connect the promise to their own need? One problem may need a sentence of explanation; the other may mean you chose the wrong thing to emphasize. Feedback is not a vote that says, “Someone disagreed, so I was wrong.” It helps locate a specific gap between the work and the world. Once you locate it, the choice of what to repair is still yours.

You can try the practice proposal tonight. Name one skill you genuinely want to learn. Use AI to make a small piece of work that another person could see. Before reading its suggested revisions, record where you expect the work to fail. Then repeat on new material and ask a suitable reader what they could not understand. If no reader is available yet, at least keep your prediction and your reasons for changing the work; do not let the model's self-rating be all your evidence. The next time you say “I'm learning AI,” ask a more precise question: **When I close the tool, what can I now do better myself?**

You can collect another prompt later. First, give this course a name!
