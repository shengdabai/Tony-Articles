# Give Your Personal Tool a Graduation Date

> Publication date: 2026-10-08 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-08-给个人工具设一个毕业日.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-08-give-your-tool-a-graduation-date.md)

---

An AI summary about personal AI tools raised an appealing possibility: a small app could exist for just one stage of a person's life. That possibility raises a question about learning tools. When should a tool let us try on our own? I think a personal tool built to help someone learn should have a graduation condition from day one. When it has done its job, the person should need it a little less. A smoother interface does not, by itself, make a more capable person.

## Wouldn't it be better to keep using it?

There are good reasons to keep a tool. A reliable one saves time and mental effort. If someone has to reexplain the same background to an AI, check the same format, and move the same results by hand every day, the person is doing the tool's work. Saved steps, accessible history, and connections to other people's work are what make a tool useful in daily life. For tasks that must be delivered accurately, deliberately removing help could even bring errors back.

A product builder could make an even stronger case. If people return every day, perhaps the product has solved a real problem. If they soon stop using it, perhaps it has solved nothing. Continued use also brings more chances to improve the product. Documents, habits, and working relationships accumulate around it. Retiring a tool every so often would force people to learn another interface. That could be a costly exercise in self-congratulation.

I agree with much of that. Navigation, bookkeeping, collaboration, and record keeping are usually not exercises in training human memory. There is no shame in letting a tool handle repetition. Calling every form of help a cause of laziness would make us give up useful help. The problem appears in a different situation: you intend to learn how to make a judgment, yet the tool also makes that judgment for you. Continued use no longer guarantees continued growth.

It is hard to see the difference in the finished work. A polished summary may be the expression of something you understood, or something the AI read and you copied. Working code may mean you understood the edge cases, or that a model happened to handle them correctly. The output can fool us. A better question is whether, when the next and different piece of material arrives, you can spot what matters and explain your choice.

By graduation, I do not mean deleting the app or making everyone perform every step by hand. I mean changing the roles: at some point, you make a particular judgment before the tool does; the tool can still provide material, flag omissions, and record feedback. If the only aim is reliable task completion, with no skill to develop, there is no reason to schedule a graduation. We need to name the goal before choosing the design.

## What should a small tool teach?

Suppose you want to learn to read English research reports, rather than simply obtain the conclusion of a particular report. You ask an AI to make a small page just for you. The original passage sits on the left. An explanation card on the right stays covered at first. Before it opens, you write who the study examined, what it found, and what it did not examine. Then the card opens and points to a limitation you missed. The next day, you work through an unfamiliar passage.

There may be no market for this page. It does not even need polish. Showing the passage, saving your attempt, and offering feedback that can be checked are enough to begin. But it preserves a sequence that a general chat box may easily lose: you must answer before receiving help. The learning goal lives in the interface, rather than only in a line of instructions. Think of the guidance lines on a practice driving course. They help a beginner judge position. Once you can judge distance yourself, they have done their job. Real roads contain more variables, so passing the practice course cannot establish that you can handle every road.

This page, and its answer-first sequence, are my design proposal, not a product or research result shown to work. The number of times you open the page would be a poor measure of its value. What matters is whether you can later read a new study and find its sample, finding, and limitations for yourself. If you can fill in this page's boxes but cannot recognize those elements elsewhere, you may only have learned the boxes. That failure is useful information too. The range of practice may be too narrow, or the hints may be appearing too early.

When making a tool, it is tempting to start with features: automatic summaries, translation, charts, a library of saved items. Those features seem convenient, but each addition should face one question: which step does it return to the learner? If every step becomes easier, the step removed may be the act of learning. My practical test is simple. Does the feature help you engage with the source material and discover your own error, or help you bypass the material and collect an attractive result?

An AI summary of an education interview offers a related idea: active thinking may matter for learning. That is a summary of an interviewee's view, not a proven outcome. I propose a narrower design for this page. When the goal is to form a judgment, the tool should preserve at least one chance for the person to judge first and receive feedback second. This is an author's proposal, not a research finding. Whether people will use that chance, and whether it improves their learning, still has to be tested in use.

## Does more AI mean less learning?

No general rule says so. AI can explain, ask questions, point out a gap, and adjust difficult material into a manageable exercise. Sometimes doing more of that work may help someone learn faster. The important question is which part it does. A recent [randomized study of coding skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) offers a warning. “Randomized” means researchers used chance to decide who could use AI and who could not, helping keep existing differences between the groups from driving the comparison. The 52 developers used Python regularly but were unfamiliar with a new library. Researchers tested their understanding soon after the task. The AI group averaged 50% on the quiz; the group coding by hand averaged 67%. The AI group finished about two minutes sooner on average, but that time difference was not statistically significant: the result was too uncertain to rule out chance differences in this sample, so the study cannot establish that AI made the task faster. It tested immediate understanding, not ability months later, and it does not establish the same result in every learning setting.

The ways people used the assistant are as interesting as the group averages. Some mostly asked it to generate or fix code. Others asked about concepts and then wrote code themselves; the latter did better in the researchers' descriptive groups. Those interaction patterns were not assigned in a second randomized experiment, so their different scores cannot prove that one pattern causes better learning. I take a design question from the observation: does the tool leave room for explanation, debugging, and an independent attempt? If it automatically fills every one of those spaces, a project may still get finished while understanding becomes harder to see.

Another [learning experiment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full) stops me from treating answer first as a magic formula. Psychology undergraduates read passages. Researchers compared trying to recall answers and then receiving feedback, rereading followed by separate statements of the key information, and simply rereading. In the second experiment's test on related new questions one week later, the three conditions scored 64%, 53%, and 40%, respectively. Recall with feedback did best, but highlighting key information for rereaders also helped substantially. The experiments used particular texts and transfer questions. They did not test an AI page or long-term professional skill.

The important information in the feedback was doing some of the work. A page that makes you guess without showing which sentence supports the conclusion turns practice into a guessing game. A page that reveals the answer and its reason first gives you no chance to find out what you failed to understand. I would preserve the gap between two actions: give an answer that could be wrong, then inspect evidence that could correct it. The gap need not be long, but it must be real.

In a [review of deliberate practice](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full), researchers K. Anders Ericsson and Kyle W. Harwell explain why quality matters. In its original sense, deliberate practice meant training in which a qualified teacher identifies a person's weakness, sets an individual goal, gives prompt feedback, and lets the learner revise and try again. Simply doing a job, or adding up hours, does not meet that definition. Their article argues over the boundaries of a research concept; it is not a recipe that makes anyone an expert. For a personal tool, I borrow a modest structure: choose a specific ability, get clear feedback, then practice the particular error again. Where no knowledgeable person checks the work, the AI's feedback also needs spot checks.

## How do you write a graduation condition?

Write it like a small test, not a motivational slogan. For the research-reading page, the condition might be this: given a passage you have not seen, leave the explanation card closed, identify the participants, main finding, and limitation, and point to the text supporting each answer. Only then open the card to compare. If you missed the limitation, practice finding limitations. If you misidentified the participants, practice reading the sample description. Changing one specific error at a time tells you more about your progress than rereading the entire report yet again.

There is a limit to that condition. One correct answer is not a lifetime pass. You can try again later with another passage, or bring back the aid when you meet a harder method. People forget, tasks grow harder, and standards rise. Graduation only means that, for a task of the present difficulty, the tool need not make that small judgment for you right now. It is neither a certificate of expertise nor a medal for refusing help.

Nor should the AI be the sole examiner. It may explain a passage fluently while missing an exception buried in the methods. It may favor an answer that sounds like its own. The page should keep links to the supporting sentences and let you challenge its verdict. For consequential material, ask someone who knows the field to check; if that is unavailable, treat the tool's comments as claims to verify. This design may feel slower, but it makes judgments traceable. Learning to challenge the feedback is itself part of graduation.

Where should a small tool begin? A developer cannot settle that by sitting alone and dreaming up features. In [Do Things that Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham argues that founders often have to find early users and help them personally before they understand what to build. This is a practitioner's account of early startups, not an experiment on learning tools. Borrowing its practical lesson, I would first work through several research-reading exercises by hand. Am I stuck because I cannot identify the sample, cannot follow the method, or hesitate to draw a conclusion? Each difficulty calls for a different button. Do the work once, and the tool has a better chance of knowing which step to support.

If reading reports is merely a step in an established workflow, and you already know how to judge them, the page can keep helping with formatting, citation alignment, and version tracking. Greater stability may make it better. But while you are still learning, a page that delivers conclusions every time invites a mistake: confusing fluency with the page for fluency with research. Both designs can be sensible once the goal is clear. They need different tests. For the first, ask whether delivery is reliable. For the second, also ask what the person can do when the aid is closed.

I would give a personal tool a small exit button. It would not uninstall anything. Every so often it would ask: would you like to try making this judgment first? If you say yes, it hides the explanation until you have written your answer. If you need help, it returns. Technology can stay beside us indefinitely. Good design also knows when to take half a step back.

In the AI tool you use repeatedly, which judgment would you like to make for yourself next time, before the tool makes it for you?
