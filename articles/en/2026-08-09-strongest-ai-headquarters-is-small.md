# The Strongest AI Headquarters Is Small

> 发布日期:2026-08-09 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-09-最强的AI司令部应该很小.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-09-strongest-ai-headquarters-is-small.md)

---

The first instinct, when people discover serious AI tools, is to build a palace.

One folder for prompts. Another for agents. Another for rules. Another for memories. Another for tools. Then a growing list of workflows, templates, shortcuts, plugins, commands, hooks, and "best practices" copied from someone else's setup.

It feels like progress because the system is getting heavier.

But heaviness is not strength.

The more I use AI as a real working environment, the more I suspect the strongest personal AI system has a surprisingly small headquarters. Not empty. Not primitive. Small.

The headquarters should contain the things that must remain stable across many projects: your values, your risk boundaries, your quality bar, your way of making decisions, your privacy rules, your habit of verification, your definition of finished work.

The experts should live closer to the work.

That distinction sounds like configuration advice. It is actually a philosophy of human agency in the AI era.

## A Bigger Headquarters Creates a Slower Commander

Every new AI user eventually meets the same temptation: "If this instruction helped once, maybe I should keep it forever."

So the global system prompt grows. The memory file grows. The tool list grows. A local success becomes a universal rule. A painful mistake becomes a permanent warning. A clever trick becomes a default behavior. Soon the assistant is no longer helping with today's work. It is dragging the emotional history of every previous project into the room.

This is not only a technical problem. It is a leadership problem.

A commander who carries every specialist's manual in his own head becomes slow. He cannot see the battlefield because he is busy remembering the bridge engineer's preference, the doctor's exception, the accountant's template, and the designer's old taste. These things may all be useful somewhere. They should not all be sovereign everywhere.

The same thing happens to an AI workbench.

When the global layer becomes too full, the system begins to confuse local context with identity. A rule written for a writing project starts influencing a coding task. A safety note written after one failure begins to block unrelated experiments. A formatting preference becomes a worldview. A tool installed for one rare workflow sits in every session, asking for attention even when it is irrelevant.

The result is a strange kind of friction: the system is powerful, but less responsive. It knows many things, but cannot easily tell which ones matter now.

That is why the useful question is not "How much can I add?"

The better question is: "What deserves to live at headquarters?"

## Occam Was Not Saying "Be Simple"

People often quote Occam's Razor as if it means "simpler is always better."

That is too simple.

The useful version is closer to this: do not multiply entities beyond necessity. The important word is not "simple." The important word is "necessity." The principle does not tell us to remove complexity that earns its place. It tells us to stop worshiping complexity that only makes us feel prepared.[^1]

This matters deeply for AI systems.

Some complexity is necessary. A legal review workflow needs different constraints from a personal journaling workflow. A publishing system needs privacy checks. A coding agent needs tests, file boundaries, rollback paths, and command discipline. A research assistant needs source standards. A customer-facing agent needs escalation rules.

But necessary complexity should usually be local.

Put the review standard inside the publishing project. Put the testing protocol inside the software repository. Put the source rules inside the research workflow. Put the customer policy inside the support system. Let each expert room carry the furniture it actually needs.

The headquarters should stay small because it has a different job. It is not supposed to know everything. It is supposed to decide what kind of knowing is needed.

This is the hidden difference between a rule and a constitution.

A rule says, "For this task, do this."

A constitution says, "Across tasks, never betray these principles."

Most AI setups become messy because they turn too many rules into constitutions.

## The Commander and the Expert Have Different Goals

An expert optimizes locally.

A commander optimizes globally.

The expert asks, "What is the best answer inside my domain?" The commander asks, "Which domain matters now, what risk are we accepting, who should decide, and when should we stop?"

Both roles matter. The mistake is to merge them.

AI makes this mistake tempting because the tool can imitate expertise in many fields. It can sound like a lawyer, editor, engineer, analyst, tutor, designer, marketer, and researcher inside the same conversation. That makes people feel as if one giant assistant should contain all roles at once.

But role density is not coordination.

The more expert voices you stuff into the center, the harder it becomes to hear the actual decision. The legal expert wants lower risk. The marketer wants sharper claims. The engineer wants maintainability. The designer wants elegance. The teacher wants clarity. The operator wants speed. None of them is wrong. But none of them alone owns the whole.

The human has to remain the commander.

Not because humans are always smarter. Often we are not. The point is responsibility. A model can generate options, expose tradeoffs, execute checks, and remember procedures. It cannot own your life direction, your reputation, your moral cost, your long-term promise to customers or readers, or the reason this work matters in the first place.

So a good AI headquarters should keep asking commander-level questions:

- What is the real objective?
- What must not be exposed?
- What counts as evidence?
- What would make us stop?
- Which expert should be invoked here?
- What decision must remain human?

That is enough. More than enough.

## Modern AI Tools Already Point This Way

The trend in serious AI tooling is not "put everything into one giant prompt."

It is scope.

Claude Code's public documentation describes memory across user and project scopes, including persistent instructions through `CLAUDE.md` files and auto-memory that can store discovered patterns.[^2] That matters because different memories have different rightful homes. A personal preference may belong globally. A repository build command belongs to the project. A one-time debugging clue belongs even deeper, close to the task that produced it.

Anthropic's Agent Skills point in the same direction. Skills are folders containing instructions, scripts, and resources that Claude loads when relevant, instead of forcing every specialized capability into the assistant at all times.[^3]

The Model Context Protocol points there too. MCP standardizes how AI applications connect to external data sources and tools.[^4] Its value is not that every tool should be present in every moment. Its value is that the system can reach the right outside capability when the work calls for it.

These are technical mechanisms, but they express a larger pattern:

Do not make the center carry everything.

Make the center good at routing, judging, and preserving the right lessons.

The personal version is simple. Your global AI setup should be more like an airport control tower than a warehouse. A warehouse stores everything. A control tower does not. It sees the situation, applies stable rules, routes traffic, prevents collisions, and knows when to stop movement.

That is a much better metaphor for a human-AI workbench.

## The Danger of Over-Training Your Assistant on Yesterday

There is another reason to keep the headquarters small: AI changes quickly.

A prompt that was brilliant six months ago may become unnecessary after a model upgrade. A workaround for an old failure may become a leash on a stronger model. A very detailed instruction may improve one narrow case while making ten ordinary cases worse. The system may still "work," so you do not notice the tax.

This is one of the quietest dangers in AI work.

Software usually complains when old configuration breaks. Tests fail. Dependencies warn. Commands error out.

AI instructions decay more politely. They rarely crash. They just bias the work. They make the assistant over-cautious, over-formal, over-explanatory, over-optimized for an old fear. You feel the result as a slight drag, but because the output is still fluent, you may blame yourself instead of the setup.

This is why the global layer should be treated like expensive real estate.

Every permanent instruction should pay rent.

Does it protect privacy? Keep it.

Does it define a real quality bar? Keep it.

Does it prevent a repeated serious failure? Keep it, but make it specific.

Does it merely express a mood from one past project? Move it out.

Does it belong to a particular workflow? Put it in that workflow.

Does it no longer change the result? Delete it.

The ability to remove is part of intelligence. A system that only accumulates becomes a museum of old anxieties.

## Small Headquarters, Thick Projects

Keeping headquarters small does not mean working casually.

It means moving complexity to the right level.

A serious project should be thick. It should have a clear goal, source standards, examples, tests, acceptance criteria, privacy boundaries, a record of decisions, and a way to recover from mistakes. The project is where detail belongs because the project has an object. It knows what it is trying to produce.

Headquarters is different. Headquarters has to survive many objects.

This is the structure I now trust most:

At the global level: values, privacy, verification, responsibility, and decision discipline.

At the project level: domain rules, examples, workflows, assets, tools, and acceptance tests.

At the task level: temporary context, current constraints, open questions, and stopping conditions.

At the review level: what failed, what changed, what should be promoted upward, and what should be deleted.

The last part is easy to miss. A self-evolving system does not only add knowledge. It promotes and demotes knowledge.

When a local rule proves useful across many projects, it can move upward. When a global rule starts causing noise, it should move downward or disappear. When an expert workflow becomes stable, it can become a reusable skill. When a skill is no longer used, it should be archived.

This is how a person stays strong while the tool layer changes.

Not by building the largest prompt.

By building the best metabolism.

## The Point Is Not to Make AI Smaller

The point is to make the human larger.

If AI makes you install more and more hidden complexity until you no longer understand your own system, it has made you dependent. If AI helps you separate principles from procedures, global judgment from local expertise, stable values from temporary tactics, then it has made you stronger.

That is the line I care about.

I do not want an AI setup that feels impressive in screenshots. I want one that makes me harder to fool, faster to recover, clearer about what matters, and more capable of turning experience into reusable structure.

The strongest AI headquarters is small because the headquarters is where responsibility lives.

Everything else can be summoned, tested, replaced, improved, or removed.

The human remains the commander. The machine brings experts to the table. The project gives those experts a place to work. The review decides what the system has earned the right to remember.

That is how AI makes people stronger rather than lazier:

not by doing our thinking for us,

but by forcing us to decide which parts of our thinking deserve to become infrastructure.
