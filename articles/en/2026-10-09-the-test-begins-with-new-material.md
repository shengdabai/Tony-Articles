# A Tutorial's Real Test Begins with New Material

> 发布日期：2026-10-09 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-09-教程的考试在换一份材料之后.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-09-the-test-begins-with-new-material.md)

---

If you finish a tutorial and produce the promised result, have you learned the skill? I think the answer is: not yet. The revealing test comes when the prepared example disappears and you try again with material of your own. A first success may contain a mixture of the instructor's cues, a carefully chosen sample, and luck. If we want AI to make people more capable, we need to know whose capability produced the result.

A summary of a bulk image editing tool raises this question. It describes a product that can perform a repetitive operation while a person may still need help applying it to their own material. The summary offered no verified measure of what users learned, so it cannot support a growth story. Its useful question is simpler. When a task moves from the maker's hands into the user's, how can we tell whether the ability moved with it?

## Isn't a finished result enough?

Try again with different material.

Consider making a product display image. A tutorial can guide you through one prepared example: open a template, insert an image, adjust its position, and export the result. An image appears on your screen. Next time, however, your own image may have different dimensions, the subject may sit in another part of the frame, or the template may have different layers. Layers are parts of a template you can adjust separately, such as the product image and the background. Do you know how to decide what to change, or do you only remember where the pointer went last time? Those outcomes look similar during a demonstration and feel very different when you work alone.

Someone might say that users simply want the image, not a lesson. Fair enough. If a service promises to do the work for them, a finished deliverable can be the right thing to inspect. If it promises to help them do the work themselves in the future, then we need to observe a second, independent attempt. That is the promise I am examining. A result made for someone is evidence that the result exists. It is not yet evidence that the person can reproduce it.

Even the same set of buttons can serve different purposes. In a [2016 interview](https://hbr.org/podcast/2016/12/the-jobs-to-be-done-theory-of-innovation), Clayton Christensen described an investigation of milkshake purchases. People who bought one alone in the morning were dealing with a commute; later buyers who came with children had a different social purpose. This was an account of a particular investigation, not a rule for every restaurant. The same distinction matters for display images. One person needs a single image to publish, another repeatedly handles changing products, and another needs to teach a teammate. An export click does not tell us which of those jobs was done.

Before asking whether someone can use a tool, then, define the task they must complete independently. Can they export a new image? Can they notice that the material does not fit the current template and choose another? Can they explain the procedure to the next person? Different goals call for different tests. Writing the goal as an observable action protects the maker from congratulating themselves too soon and tells the user what ability they are meant to gain.

The goal should also define what help is allowed. Consulting a short reference card is not the same as needing someone to guide every click. Knowing when to stop and ask for help can be a better outcome than quietly producing a wrong result. If we leave these conditions vague, almost any outcome can be declared a pass afterward. Draw the line in advance, and progress becomes visible.

## Why do demonstrations pass so easily?

They remove the hard choices in advance.

In a demo with material selected in advance, the instructor knows which step comes next, so the camera pauses at the right place. Menus arrive just when they are needed. It becomes easy to mistake following the sequence for understanding why that sequence was chosen. With new material, the difficult part may be recognizing what kind of situation is in front of you. The tutorial made that recognition once on your behalf.

A [2016 learning experiment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01977/full) helps make the distinction concrete. Psychology undergraduates studied passages through repeated reading, recall followed by answer feedback, or rereading followed by statements of key information. The researchers then asked questions that required applying the principles in a different knowledge domain. In the second experiment, 54 participants were included in the analysis. One week later, the average proportions correct were about 64%, 53%, and 40%, respectively. Recall with feedback led, but giving the rereading group focused access to key statements also narrowed the gap.

I do not take this to mean that every tutorial should become a quiz. The more useful point is that learners need to see the decisive information and have a chance to identify it themselves. The study measured conceptual application with specific texts and questions; it did not test image editing software. Its sample was limited, and time spent on the conditions differed. Predicting a product's conversion rate from these results would go too far. Still, the study gives us a reason to distinguish replaying a complete procedure from drawing attention to the clue that determines the next step.

Return to the display image. Suppose the subject in a new picture is close to the edge, unlike the centered subject in the sample. You need to check whether the template will crop the subject, then decide whether to change the template or adjust the frame. A lesson that only shows where to click on the old picture leaves you stuck again. A lesson that shows what to check, then gives you an unprompted attempt, at least gives you a chance to carry the judgment forward. This is an illustrative hypothetical, not a measured result from any particular tool.

The hidden difficulty can also appear in a moment that looks smooth. A user who gets stuck may say that they cannot find a button. Enlarging it might relieve the immediate frustration, while missing the possibility that they do not know when to use it. Asking what informed their last choice helps distinguish those two problems. If they cannot answer, the next tutorial might explain the decision cue first; only then can we tell whether the interface also needs work.

Cooking offers a familiar parallel. Following a recipe with ingredients already measured out and opening the fridge to decide the quantities are different tasks. Both can produce dinner, but the second requires you to recognize the material and make a choice. Software gives us a clearer record of clicks and outputs than cooking does. That similarity does not tell us that the two skills develop at the same rate; it simply exposes the decision that a prepared example can hide.

## Wouldn't more repetition fix it?

Practice helps. What you practice matters.

There is a tempting idea that if a user repeats the same sample image enough times, the skill will become secure. Repetition may make the motions less awkward. It may also make one image more familiar. Change its proportions, and the old routine may not carry over. This is the question of transfer: can you use what you learned when the conditions change, such as checking whether a different image will crop the product? The person teaching needs to inspect choices on a new task; the person learning needs to know whether the error lay in judgment, operation, or memory. Otherwise, more repetitions may only grow a feeling of familiarity.

A [2019 replication study by Macnamara and Maitra](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745/) is useful when we talk about practice. It recruited 39 violin students and compared skill groups with participants' retrospective estimates of accumulated practice. Practice and skill were related, yet the study did not reproduce the earlier claim that accumulated hours neatly tracked every group: the faculty-nominated best group had not accumulated significantly more practice than the other strong group. This was a small sample, and recalling years of practice is imperfect. It cannot tell us which image editing exercise will work best. It does, however, challenge the easy promise that enough repetition produces the same result for everyone.

Another distinction matters here. Practice can make a familiar task faster. Transfer asks whether you can still recognize the useful principle when the conditions change. The two can support each other, but speed on the first task cannot stand in for judgment on the second. Ask AI to produce a batch of display images, and perhaps all of them will be usable. If you still cannot explain why it cropped the edge of the product, the next image with different dimensions may leave you guessing again. I am in favor of using AI to remove mechanical work. I would spend some of the attention it frees on the places where a small change causes a mistake.

Feedback after practice should be more than a pass or fail. Name the cue the learner missed and the point at which it should have become visible. Then there is something specific to look for next time. Give the answer too soon, and the learner may learn to wait for a prompt; give it too late, and the error may have shaped the whole attempt. My proposed sequence is to let the person state their judgment first and show the key cue afterward. It is a practice suggestion informed by the study above, not a proven recipe for every real task.

A serious objection is that high-stakes work should be done by qualified people. Asking every user to become independent can waste time or create danger. Agreed. Work involving safety, law, or specialized judgment needs competent people and clear responsibility. A customer who pays for a result has no obligation to become an expert. My test applies when we claim to teach a person a task they can reasonably do themselves. If the product is a done-for-you service, assess the result, timing, and responsibility honestly. Do not call the customer's lack of learning a failure.

This is also why support requests are valuable. They are a kind of test away from the tutorial's prepared setting: people bring material the lesson did not anticipate, and the point where they stop exposes a hidden decision. But a support queue contains only the people who asked for help. Others may have finished without contacting anyone. Inferring everybody's learning from the number of requests would miss them. Turn a recurring difficulty into a question, then try new material with different users.

## When should the tool change?

When the same blockage recurs.

First, locate the problem. Someone who cannot tell what to do next may need a clearer decision cue. Someone who knows what to choose but repeats a tedious operation may benefit from automation. Someone who never wanted to do the task may need a service. If we treat all three situations as requests for another button, the interface becomes busier while the task remains unfinished. A longer feature list can mean more branches in which to get lost.

In a [2009 retrospective on his work at MIPS](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/), Steve Blank wrote about collecting conflicting requests from two kinds of customers concerning the order in which data was stored. Both orders went into the chip. The hardware team expected a modest circuit addition, while the software team warned of a long burden downstream. His story shows how one choice can move costs to the next group. It does not prove that customer requests should generally be rejected. Likewise, before adding a hint, a button, or an automatic step to a tutorial, ask who will do less judging and who will take on maintenance or misuse costs.

Sometimes the right change is to the explanation, sometimes to the interface, and sometimes to the promise itself. If most people choose correctly with new material but exporting is tedious, automate export. If people choose the wrong template, faster exporting solves nothing. If the material varies too much for the tool to make a reliable choice, preserve a route to human help. This is my design recommendation, not a formula jointly verified by the four sources. It gives the next revision a location we can inspect.

One failed attempt is a weak basis for a general rule. The material may be unusual, the explanation may be unclear, or the task may be outside the tool's intended range. If different people using different material keep stopping at the same point, the reason to change something becomes stronger. If every blockage is different, narrow the task definition first. That slower move can keep an exceptional case from becoming a new step everybody must take.

I propose a small experiment to test this approach. Choose one low-risk task you teach someone else and write down what learning would look like: perhaps they can finish with new material and can name the condition that should make them stop and ask for help. Show one demonstration, then let them try their own material without guidance. Record where they pause, what they consult, and whether they finish. Change just one part of the explanation or interface, then try another person with another sample. If the blockage moves later and they can explain the crucial choice, the revision has some initial value. If they only finish by following your prompts, the test is still open. This is a suggestion to test, not an experiment already completed.

A tutorial can be a map lent to someone for a while. A map can get a person through the route used in the demonstration. It has taught them a direction only when the junction looks different and they still know where they need to go. That case summary leaves us with a plain acceptance test: with different material, who can still complete the task?
