Keep has maintained three principles in its product development, and these principles still influence the company today. They are not only deeply embedded in the product team but also serve as a reference for company decisions. They are: **do more, not less; do heavy, not light; do focused, not scattered.**

Let's start with the first one: **do more, not less.** This means identifying the most desired actions of the majority of users as the core of what the product should do. The least desired actions are postponed. Making such decisions at critical moments ensures the product can update quickly enough.

Most Keep users are beginners in fitness, and the product can't solve 100% of the problems. Solving 60-70% of the issues is manageable, but if we insist on solving the remaining 30%, it would spread the team's focus too thin. Keep's product strategy was to concentrate efforts on the main user group and their core needs.

The second principle is: **do heavy, not light.** The product should be built with enough depth so that users can use it lightly. It's not about having many features that make the product heavy—it's about the depth of the product logic and content. Keep's approach was to build deep content and solid logic, not just a lot of features. This way, users can come in with a problem, search for it, and get an immediate answer. Whether they want to slim down or get bigger breasts, they can find the right course immediately. Problems are resolved right away, without waiting.

Keep's product is heavy in content, but users feel it's light. They come in with a question and get an answer right away.

The third principle is: **do focused, not scattered.** The product should clearly communicate what it does. It's a tool. From the beginning, Keep positioned itself as a "mobile fitness tool," emphasizing its tool-like nature. For example, the Keep community was designed so that users couldn't post any updates until they completed a workout. Over time, this helped users develop good exercise habits and raised the bar for posting, filtering out some spam and laying a solid foundation for future community openness.

Additionally, product and operations are inseparable. Without good operations, even the best product can't succeed. Regarding how Keep launched without spending money, Wang Ning shared two key experiences. One was new media operations. Wang Ning said this was the "inner power" of cold-starting a product. Keep's Weibo account had been running for about two years, with nearly 2.7 million followers. The key in operating Weibo was the speed of response—whether to hot events or user feedback. The other was the "Burying Bombs Plan," which was the "outer power" for cold-starting. This plan involved a series of pre-launch preparations, including a "Chief Beta Tester" campaign to identify key fitness influencers on Weibo as core seed users. These users responded positively, and through the beta version, they spread the product further, bringing in over 4,000 users. These 4,000 users loved Keep and became strong advocates once the product officially launched. In addition, the Keep team actively engaged in various communities, posting useful content in places like Baidu Tieba and Douban groups, and had Keep representatives in many QQ and WeChat groups. Once all these were in place, the product's launch was a natural and smooth process.
