# Choose Which Work to Save

> 发布日期:2026-10-18 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-18-省事也要会挑活.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-18-choose-which-work-to-save.md)

---

What do we actually mean when we say a tool “saves work”? Fewer clicks, fewer pages to read, and one less problem to think through all sound like the same benefit. They belong in different accounts. Some chores merely consume time. Others expose mistakes and gradually teach us to judge. If a tool sweeps both away, the user may feel relieved today and become more dependent tomorrow. My view is simple: a good tool takes over repetitive work that teaches little, while keeping useful feedback within reach.

Two AI-written summaries of outside material provide concrete cases to consider. One concerned the repeated task of adjusting temperature at night; the other concerned the handoff between finishing a document and making it useful to readers. Those summaries do not let me verify a product's effects or its business figures. The actions themselves, though, raise distinct questions: one is a repeated adjustment a person may want to stop making, and the other is a handoff where completed work can get stuck. Follow those actions, and “automate more” stops being an adequate design brief.

## Mistake one: counting every reduction in effort as progress

Consider writing. If AI revises a cover letter and a person types less, have they become lazy? Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania and other institutions ran randomized experiments: after learning writing principles, participants were assigned to practice groups by chance, rather than choosing a group, and then revised a different letter without AI. Assigning groups by chance reduces the influence of differences people brought into the study, making the practice methods easier to compare. Those who practiced with AI put in less effort during practice yet wrote better letters on the independent test. The follow-up was short and the task was cover letters, so the study does not establish lasting gains in writing generally or in other skills. The [research paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02880) makes a mildly uncomfortable point: effort is not itself a learning outcome.

That is no reason to hand AI every draft. The researchers also had a group that saw an AI-revised cover letter without editing it, then received one AI-generated suggestion for improvement. These participants did no further practice before the no-AI test, yet improved on it. A concrete revision may have made abstract advice visible: you can see how a sentence is shortened and how information is reordered, then try those moves yourself. Because the example and feedback came together, the result does not show that the example alone caused the improvement. The authors also caution that a finished answer in mathematics or programming may hide the reasoning that produced it. The cover-letter result cannot simply be carried over to those tasks.

A different study turns the question around. The [abstract of a 2026 paper by Grace Liu and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721) reports randomized controlled trials involving 1,222 participants across tasks including reasoning and reading comprehension. According to the abstract, AI assistance improved immediate performance, but participants later performed worse without AI and were more likely to give up. The supplied source contains the authors' abstract, not the paper's methods and full analysis. I therefore use it for a narrower point: a good answer now cannot be entered in the ledger as proof of independent ability later.

Put the findings side by side and I do not see a vote for “AI helps” or “AI harms.” In cover-letter writing, a specific revision can show a move the learner might repeat. In a reasoning task that requires persistence, an answer available on demand can remove the chance to work through difficulty. The tasks, forms of assistance, and observation periods differ; their effect sizes cannot be subtracted from each other. What we can take away is smaller and more useful: after saving effort, ask whether you have gained a clue you could use on your own next time.

Cooking offers a familiar parallel. Washing grease off a pan removes a repetitive chore. Tasting a dish before deciding whether to add salt connects a sensation to a decision. Automate the second act as well and dinner may be fine, but someone learning to cook has lost a moment of calibration. This analogy is an explanation, not evidence; even chopping and washing can be instructive for a trainee chef. It prompts the prior question: are you trying to get dinner on the table, or learn to cook it?

## Mistake two: treating complete takeover as the best help

Product makers often feel a pull toward completeness. If a tool can produce the finished result, why leave any middle step to the user? After a help document is written, software can categorize, format, publish, and even draft answers from it. That can save real work. But when a reader says, “I still don't understand this page,” the author needs at least some clue about where the reader got lost and what explanation needs changing. A polished page and an attractive report leave the author guessing if neither can be traced back to that trouble.

I would split “taking over a task” into two jobs: perform the repetitive operation, and make feedback visible when it can improve the next decision. The first can be thorough. The second cannot be reduced to a single score. Page visits tell you someone arrived; they do not tell you whether the person could not find the right entry, or read the page and still could not act. Signals that might change the writing include a search term, a place where readers stopped, or a specific question they asked. No metric can declare a page useful all by itself. The author still has to inspect the page in light of those signals.

The AI-written product summary about nighttime temperature prompted the same design question. A user may want to offload the burden of waking and adjusting a setting, rather than receive another daily scorecard. I do not have an independently checked product study, so I cannot say whether any particular device improves sleep. I can use the sequence of actions to ask: if a system detects a change in temperature, what concrete adjustment should it handle first? Afterward, what information should it leave so the user can investigate discomfort, change the limits, or turn automation off?

Someone could reasonably say that people buy tools to think less, not to enroll in another lesson. I agree with the first half. When a person is in a hurry and needs to finish a one-off task, making them review every step turns help into homework. Yet for a recurring task whose consequences remain their responsibility, hiding everything also has a cost. When conditions change, they may have no idea where the problem began. A tool can work quietly by default and surface enough reasoning when something fails, the user wants to change a setting, or a consequential decision needs approval. The stakes and the user's goal should determine how much explanation appears. They should not have to supervise every routine action.

There is a useful detail in Lira and colleagues' study: the example group saw a concrete rewrite and received AI feedback, and the researchers tested whether they could revise a new letter without AI. Liu and colleagues' abstract warns that independent performance can go down after assistance. I cannot rank all products using experiments on different tasks. Together, though, the studies suggest an acceptance question that is easy to omit: what can the person do when the tool is gone? A one-off service need not teach a transferable skill. A product that promises to help people learn, judge, or work over time should take that question seriously.

## Mistake three: finishing the feature list before finding the trouble

The promise of less work tempts product makers too. AI can assemble pages and features quickly. Soon the feature list grows faster than our understanding of the need. Every new feature adds an entry point for the user to learn and another failure for the maker to maintain. Between “I can build it” and “someone needs me to build it” lies awkward work: put an incomplete thing in front of a person and ask what they were doing before, and where they became stuck. A program cannot have that conversation on your behalf.

In [his account of Buffer's beginnings](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/), Joel Gascoigne describes first using very thin pages to test interest in queued posting. He then added a pricing choice before building a working product. He says the first paying customer arrived four days after launch. This 2011 account by one founder shows the sequence in which he sought demand signals; it is no statistical guarantee that copying the sequence will work. A pricing click is evidence that someone wants to look further. A payment is stronger evidence. Continued use poses yet another question.

In a [2013 essay](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham describes Stripe's founders helping willing early users install the product in person. He also describes Viaweb building merchants' websites for them by hand. The valuable part is not handwork for its own sake. By following the user's actions, the maker discovers where the job stops working and what the product has to change. Graham offers early startup cases from his experience, not a causal experiment that applies to every industry. Products with serious safety costs also cannot be placed in users' hands before they are safe enough to try.

This distinction also applies to the note about what happens after a document is finished. A handoff is more than moving a file from one app to another. A reader may fail to find the page, find it but not understand it, or understand it but still be unable to finish the task. Those are three different problems. Building one elaborate automation for all three before following real users through the handoff could automate a mistaken assumption. Building faster does not mean learning faster what was right. AI can compress production time; it cannot supply the evidence of why a user stopped.

There is a fair objection: close, manual contact is slow, and an opportunity can pass while you collect opinions. Speed matters. But early on, a wrong direction can make an entire finished system expensive to redo. Working through one handoff with a few users may reveal the next step sooner. Whether the idea can grow still depends on other people having the same trouble. A custom service needed by only one person may never make sense as a general product. Enthusiastic early users may also differ from later ones. Close contact yields clues about the problem, not proof of the market's size.

## Mistake four: measuring minutes saved but not ability returned

I propose two ledgers for a tool. The first concerns the task: which repetitive actions disappeared, did the result become more reliable, and can an error be reversed? The second concerns the person: when a similar but slightly different situation comes along, are they better able to tell what matters, or do they only know to wait for the same button? I do not expect every tool to become a teacher. Automatic temperature adjustment can be judged mainly on comfort and control. Writing assistance that promises to build independent writing skill, however, should accept a test without AI. Different promises give the ledgers different weights.

Costs belong in those ledgers too. Asking for confirmation every time may protect judgment or merely interrupt the work someone meant to do. Automatic execution can free attention or spread a small error widely. A fondness for “keeping humans involved” should not produce a stack of empty approvals; a fondness for full automation should not leave people with only a scorecard after the fact. For repeated actions with reversible outcomes, let the tool act while keeping undo and adjustment available. For rare decisions with serious consequences that a person must own, let the tool show its basis and uncertainties before the person decides. That is my design judgment, not a universal recipe established by the studies above.

Here is a test I suggest for today. Pick a task you often do with AI. Write down the repetitive action you most dislike, then write down the point at which your own judgment will matter next time. Give AI the first action. At the second point, ask it for two comparable approaches, the reasons behind each, and what remains uncertain. Once the task is done, take a similar piece of material and leave AI closed long enough to try the first step yourself. If you cannot begin, do not rush to call yourself lazy. Check what the tool gave you last time: an example you could learn from, or only a flawless finished result.

Product makers can run a related test. Find the step a user already knows how to do and the next step where they repeatedly get stuck. Support that handoff in the smallest useful way. Watch whether they continue, whether they are willing to pay, and whether they can explain what went wrong. Do not count praise as a purchase, or one purchase as proof of lasting value. Pass each gate before deciding how much more to automate.

So I pause for an extra moment at the word “convenient.” Are we saving the effort of scrubbing grease from the pan, or removing the taste that teaches us how much salt to add? I am happy to hand over the first. If the second bears on the kind of person you hope to become, taste it at least once before deciding what help to ask for.
