# AI Does Not Travel as Software

> 发布日期:2026-08-17 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-17-AI不是软件它要重新长一遍.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-17-ai-does-not-travel-as-software.md)

---

For a long time, people understood technology globalization through a simple picture.

Something appears in one market. Another market studies it. A local team copies the visible form, changes the language, adjusts the pricing, replaces a few integrations, and ships a domestic version. Sometimes the direction reverses. A model that grows in China gets explained in Silicon Valley. A consumer habit from Asia becomes a slide in an American pitch deck. A product pattern crosses the ocean and receives a new name.

That story was never completely true, but it was useful enough for the internet era.

AI is making it less useful.

Not because ideas no longer travel. Ideas travel faster than ever. Papers, model releases, product demos, open-source repositories, interface patterns, and founder narratives can circle the world in hours. The visible layer of AI travels almost too easily.

But the valuable layer does not travel as software.

It has to grow again inside a local system.

## The Surface Is Easy To Copy

The surface of an AI product is dangerously copyable.

A chat interface is copyable. A workflow builder is copyable. A support agent, a coding agent, a voice agent, a research agent, a meeting summarizer, an AI inbox, an AI spreadsheet, an AI tutor: from the outside, many products now rhyme with one another. The model can generate the prototype. The framework can connect the tools. The demo can be polished. The deck can describe a future that sounds inevitable.

This creates a strange illusion.

If the interface can be copied, we assume the business can be copied.

But most of the real product is not in the interface. It is in the things the interface must survive: payment habits, procurement cycles, data permissions, labor costs, regulation, delivery routes, local trust, customer language, supply chains, and the unglamorous pattern by which people already get work done.

The screen is the skin.

The business is the body.

AI makes the skin cheaper. It does not make the body portable.

This is why I find some "copy the foreign product" conversations less convincing than before. In the old software world, a good interface plus a large market could sometimes carry a company surprisingly far. In the AI world, the interface may be the least defensible part. Everyone can generate a smoother surface. What matters is whether the product has entered a real local metabolism.

Does it know who pays?

Does it know who refuses to pay?

Does it know what has to be approved, signed, delivered, repaired, explained, remembered, or made reversible?

Does it know what people actually do when the official process breaks?

If not, it has not entered the market. It has only entered the browser.

## A Company Grows From A Crack In The Ground

One useful example is Weee.

At first glance, it is easy to misunderstand it as "Chinese e-commerce moved to America." But that frame misses the point. Weee describes itself as a large online Asian grocery service in the United States, and its own site now presents storefronts across multiple communities, including Asian, Mexican, Vietnamese, Thai, Brazilian, and others.[^1]

That is not just category expansion. It is a clue about soil.

The United States is not one unified consumer surface. It contains many communities with different food memories, family habits, languages, neighborhoods, and expectations. The U.S. Census Bureau reported that the foreign-born population reached 46.2 million people in 2022, or 13.9 percent of the total population.[^2] That number does not automatically create a business, but it does reveal why a "minority grocery" need can be large and fragmented at the same time.

The mainstream supermarket may be nearby but not culturally complete. The ethnic supermarket may be more relevant but physically far away. A one-hour delivery promise borrowed from China may not be the right answer. Next-day delivery, better assortment, community-specific purchasing, and local logistics may fit the shape of the problem better.

So the company is not simply moving a Chinese playbook into an American market. It is growing out of an American crack: scattered communities, long driving distances, specialized ingredients, local food regulation, cold-chain logistics, and a consumer base whose needs are obvious to insiders and easy to underestimate from far away.

AI can help such a company expand. It can help translate categories, draft product descriptions, design storefronts, predict demand, support purchasing, and reduce the cost of serving another community. But AI is not replacing the soil. It is helping the roots spread through it.

That distinction matters.

If you only copy the interface, you copy the leaf.

If you understand the local crack, you can grow a root.

## The U.S. And China Are Solving Different Bottlenecks

This also explains why the U.S. and China AI paths are becoming harder to copy from each other.

Both sides can read the same papers. Both sides can use strong models. Both sides can study the same demos. But they are not always trying to remove the same bottleneck.

In the United States, many companies are already accustomed to paying for software. Workflows are often more digitized, job categories are more standardized, and capital markets are comfortable turning a narrow pain point into a venture-scale vertical software story. So it is natural for many AI products there to become deeper software: agents for lawyers, doctors, accountants, sales teams, recruiters, analysts, and engineers.

In China, many valuable problems sit closer to physical operations, manufacturing capacity, dense supply chains, offline service, price-sensitive customers, and teams that historically solved problems by adding people rather than buying software. That does not make the market worse. It makes the translation different. The opportunity may not be "sell a better SaaS tool." It may be "deliver a cheaper, faster, more reliable result inside a messy operational process."

The same model capability lands differently because the receiving environment is different.

A stronger agent in one market becomes a paid software seat.

In another market, it becomes a service layer, a hardware bundle, a workflow hidden inside an existing business, or a three-person team delivering a result that used to require twenty people.

Neither route is morally superior. They are answers to different terrain.

The 2026 Stanford AI Index points to this broader divergence in public sentiment and adoption patterns around AI across countries, including the United States and China.[^3] The more important point for builders is not which side is "ahead" in the abstract. The more useful question is: ahead at solving which bottleneck, under which constraints, for which paying customer?

Once you ask that question, copying becomes less magical.

You can copy a feature.

You cannot copy a payment habit.

You can copy a demo.

You cannot copy a supply chain.

You can copy a prompt.

You cannot copy the trust pattern that lets someone rely on the output.

## The Dirt Is Becoming The Moat

AI lowers the cost of visible execution. That means visible execution becomes less special.

This is the uncomfortable part.

Many people still want their moat to be the thing that looks impressive in a demo. The page loads. The agent acts. The report appears. The dashboard updates. The code runs. The voice sounds natural. The flow looks smooth.

All of that matters. But it is also exactly the part that competitors can increasingly reproduce.

The harder part is the dirt.

By dirt, I mean everything that makes a real situation stubborn: inconsistent records, half-digital workflows, local exceptions, emotional trust, strange incentives, unofficial approvals, fragmented data, legacy habits, and the one person in the corner who knows why the official rule is not the real rule.

This dirt used to look like a disadvantage.

Founders wanted clean markets. Investors wanted scalable stories. Product people wanted elegant abstractions. Engineers wanted well-defined systems. Everyone wanted the mess to go away so the software could scale.

AI changes the calculation.

When generation is cheap, clean abstractions get crowded quickly. The messy edge becomes more valuable because fewer people are willing to learn it deeply. The person who understands the strange workflow, the hidden buyer, the real failure mode, the undocumented exception, or the customer who cannot describe the problem in software language may now have a better starting point than the person with a perfect clone of a hot interface.

The dirt is not something to escape.

It is where the model gets anchored to reality.

Of course, dirt alone is not enough. Local knowledge without engineering becomes custom labor forever. Engineering without local knowledge becomes elegant irrelevance. The real opportunity is to connect the two: take a specific, stubborn, local problem and turn its repeated patterns into a system that improves.

That is where AI becomes more than a tool.

It becomes a way to package local understanding.

## What This Means For A Small Builder

For an individual, creator, consultant, teacher, operator, or small team, this is a very practical shift.

Do not begin with "What AI product is hot overseas?"

Begin with "What do I know because I have lived close to a problem?"

What do customers ask you again and again?

Where does a process break in a way outsiders would not predict?

Which judgment do you make so often that you no longer notice it is judgment?

Which messy task looks too small for a venture-backed company but is painful enough that a real person would pay for relief?

Which local constraint makes the imported best practice fail?

That is the starting point.

Then use AI to engineer the pattern. Turn repeated explanations into a guide. Turn scattered cases into a dataset. Turn private judgment into a checklist. Turn a delivery habit into a workflow. Turn a workflow into a tool. Turn the tool into a service. Turn the service into a learning system.

This is not as glamorous as copying the newest product demo.

It is better.

The person copying a demo is competing against everyone who saw the same demo.

The person building from local truth is competing from a place fewer people can see.

## AI Arrives Everywhere, But Not All At Once

AI will travel everywhere.

But it will not arrive everywhere in the same form.

In one place, it will look like software. In another, like service. In another, like hardware. In another, like a training system. In another, like a hidden workflow inside a company that never appears in an AI startup database.

That should make us less anxious and more practical.

The goal is not to guess which country, company, model, or product pattern will become the one correct template. There may not be one. The goal is to notice where a powerful general capability touches a specific local constraint and creates a new possibility.

This is also a better way to think about becoming stronger with AI.

Do not only borrow distant answers.

Use distant answers to see your local problem more clearly.

The future does not become useful when it is copied. It becomes useful when it is rebuilt inside your own constraints.

AI gives us a strange new kind of leverage. It lets one person cross time zones of knowledge, borrow patterns from other industries, read markets they have never visited, and test ideas faster than before. But the final mile still belongs to contact with reality.

The model can travel.

The product has to take root.

And perhaps the real opportunity now belongs to people who stop asking, "What can I copy?" and start asking, "What is true here that outsiders cannot see yet?"
