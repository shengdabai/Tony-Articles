It's unclear when people began to resent motivational advice. Young people with rebellious spirits especially detest it, and I'm no exception. I hate it, to be precise, toxic motivational advice.

But how do we determine whether motivational advice is toxic? This reminds me of a video by music critic Ding Taisheng. Is music really good or bad?

I agree with the viewpoint in the video — music does have good and bad. Chicken soup should also be discussed in categories.

Before discussing a concept, we need to first clarify what it is. How do we define motivational advice?

> Chicken soup refers to texts that can warm the heart and offer inspiration and encouragement. The key of motivational advice writing is to make readers feel happy and satisfied, while also providing practical advice and techniques to help them face life's challenges positively.
>
> Chicken soup is a kind of positive energy article. Chicken soup articles are usually used to motivate, comfort, and inspire readers, helping them maintain a positive mindset when facing difficulties and setbacks, and pursue their ideals and happiness.

The AI's two definitions seem understandable, but they didn't make me instantly grasp the concept. I'd like to understand "motivational advice" as — **a source of concentrated energy**.

I was taught this idea as a child: only when sick could one drink motivational advice, as it could nourish the body. This absurd idea sometimes makes me wish I could get sick just to taste this deliciousness. As I grew older, having tried various things, I no longer had such a strong interest in motivational advice, to the point where it felt greasy.

Chicken soup is a source of energy, but once it becomes greasy, no amount of explanation will make it palatable. That's exactly what toxic motivational advice is.

Let me show you three types of "motivational advice" and see how you feel:

  * **Stories of Celebrities** : You can draw inspiration from the experiences and insights of celebrities, showing how they overcame difficulties and achieved their goals, offering readers guidance and role models.

    * Thomas Edison failed thousands of times before inventing the light bulb, but he never gave up. He said, "I have not failed. I've just found a thousand ways that don't work."

    * Before founding Alibaba, Jack Ma was rejected by Harvard ten times and by dozens of companies. But he never gave up. He said, "Today is cruel, tomorrow is crueler, and the day after tomorrow is beautiful. But most people die in the night."

  * **Famous Quotes** : You can quote famous sayings from celebrities or ordinary people, using concise and powerful language to express wisdom or attitudes, offering readers inspiration and emotional impact.

    * "Don't let the distance you've traveled make you forget why you started." — *The Little Prince*

    * "Life is not as good as you imagine, but it's also not as bad as you imagine. I think human fragility and strength both exceed our own imagination." — Zhang Ailing

  * **Life Stories** : You can extract touching or interesting details from your own or others' life stories, describing emotions or situations with vivid and realistic language, creating resonance and emotional impact with readers.

    * A girl lost her father just before the Gaokao (college entrance exam). She didn't give up on her studies and used the pen her father left behind to write down her college choices. She said, "This pen is the best gift my father gave me. It made me feel his love and expectations."

    * A boy chose to go teach in the west after university. He taught in a poor mountain area for three years, changing the lives of many children. He said, "I didn't come here for honor or reward. I just wanted these kids to know that someone cares about them and is waiting for them."

Write or say your true feelings? Whether positive or negative, record your emotions and experiences — this is important. This is likely your instinct about "motivational advice." Your instinct isn't random. Look at the image below:

Now let's look at the characteristics of motivational advice and toxic motivational advice:

  * **Sincerity** : The content and language of the article should be sincere. Don't exaggerate or fabricate facts. Avoid excessive rhetoric or clichés. Don't try too hard to please or lecture readers. Instead, express your thoughts and feelings genuinely, so readers can feel the author's sincerity and sincerity.

  * **Interesting** : The content and language of the article should be interesting. Don't be too dry or dull. Avoid too many professional terms or abstract concepts. Don't use too much theory or dogma. Instead, use vivid and interesting stories and details to let readers feel the author's humor and wisdom.

  * **Useful** : The content and language of the article should be useful. Don't be too empty or irrelevant. Avoid too much filler or repetition. Don't use too much complaining or blaming. Instead, offer practical and enlightening advice and methods, so readers can feel the author's care and help.

***

  * **Unrealistic** : Some motivational advice articles may be overly idealistic or beautify reality, ignoring the difficulties and challenges people face, giving a sense of unrealistic fantasy and expectation, making it feel inauthentic or untrustworthy.

  * **Disrespectful of Individuality** : Some motivational advice articles may emphasize or impose a standard or model of success or happiness, ignoring people's individuality and differences, giving a sense of disrespect or misunderstanding, making people feel uncomfortable or out of place.

  * **Inappropriate Timing** : Some motivational advice articles may overly lighten or optimistically view people's pain and melancholy, ignoring their emotions and needs, giving a sense of inappropriateness or unsuitability, making people feel unempathetic or indifferent.

After calm reflection, besides the issues with the article itself, why do we label motivational advice as toxic, regardless of whether it's good or bad?

I enjoy doing one thing: continuously sharing and recommending good content, especially recommending good books. I guess you can imagine the feedback I get after recommending something: no feedback at all! Even when I occasionally receive feedback, it's usually just one sentence: "I understand the principles, but I just can't do it."

This reminds me of a widely spread line from the movie *After We've Gone*: "You've heard many truths, yet you still can't live a good life."

Are truths really useless? Have we truly understood them? More importantly, have we seriously practiced them?

Is this line a toxic motivational advice? It's stating a fact, but most people don't want to listen. Hearing it is all — it becomes an earful, and then it's forgotten. Hearing it doesn't mean understanding it. Do we understand it, and are we willing to keep believing in it and take action?

Li Xiaolai, in his book *The Road to Financial Freedom*, mentions a viewpoint: doing the right thing itself isn't valuable; doing the right and unique thing has enough value.

> Correctness itself has no value.
>
> Correctness and uniqueness have value.
>
> Judging correctness cannot rely on public opinion.
>
> Correctness and uniqueness do not guarantee you can verify or gain its value.

Most people tend to think in one dimension. Whether something is correct has objectivity, but the key lies in how the person viewing it perceives it. A thinking model from the book *The Most Important Thing* expresses this: **Second-level thinking** — this is correctness and uniqueness. The market is made up of the general public, and their behavior is the standard market behavior. If you want to achieve above-average returns in the market, you must be different from the consensus (first-level thinking), with insight, analysis, and execution different from the norm. But being unique alone is not enough; you must also be correct.

Only when we truly understand second-level thinking does motivational advice truly take effect. Merely being correct is not enough; uniqueness is extremely important.

For example, in Li Xiaolai's book *Making Time Your Friend*, the title itself is the core — making friends with time. It's a correct view, but what's unique about it? Because most books tell you to manage your time, and a popular niche in the book market has emerged: **books on time management**.

Can time really be managed? Who owns time? It belongs to no one. What we should do is not manage time, but focus on attention, which is more important than time, and improve our attention level.

Li Xiaolai has always practiced this concept. This year, he is about to publish a new book titled *Really Making Time Your Friend*. Look, this is the result of absorbing the essence of motivational advice, practicing it day after day, and producing a lot of output — courses, books, projects, investments, planting trees. The teacher has always been doing this.

Perhaps you've been deceived by your own mistaken belief in toxic motivational advice, dismissing valuable correct ideas as irrelevant, and thus missing out on so much beauty...

Finally, I want to say that there isn't that much malice in this world. Even if you and I are destined to meet bad people and bad things, remember that we still have different choices. In the Auschwitz Nazi concentration camp, there was a Jewish man who, when facing death, didn't choose panic and sorrow. He chose to smile. Even in death, we have choices — and certainly, with motivational advice.

I don't deny that some motivational advice has impure motives or ulterior intentions. Use our judgment, analyze carefully, and if it's truly toxic, then discard it. If not, then take a sip — after all, motivational advice is very nutritious.
