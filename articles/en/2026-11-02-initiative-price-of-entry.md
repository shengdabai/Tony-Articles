# Initiative Shouldn't Be the Price of Entry

> Draft date: 2026-11-02 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-02-主动性不该是入场券.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-02-initiative-price-of-entry.md)

---

An AI tool that asks you to do less can help you take more initiative. The condition matters: it removes the hassle of getting started while gradually involving you in deciding what to do, why to do it, and what would count as enough. Removing a few operations is very different from removing someone's say.

If the tools are already so convenient and you still haven't put them to use, should you blame a lack of initiative? I'm reluctant to jump to that conclusion. For AI products intended to help ordinary people learn and work, I care more about another test: can the product help someone begin before they can clearly express a need, while leaving room for them to develop ideas of their own? Initiative can emerge through use. It need not be an entrance requirement.

## What is actually stopping you from starting?

A summary of an AI product discussion proposed moving AI from waiting for questions to asking questions and preparing the next step. It identified unfamiliar tools and difficulty writing prompts, meaning instructions to AI, as important barriers. That direction interests me, but I want to take the question back a step: when we see someone doing nothing, how much do we actually know?

Perhaps less than we think. They may not know what the tool can do. Or they may know, but be unable to find the materials, specify what they want, or predict what will happen if they hand over the task. There is another simple possibility: the task doesn't matter to them. Calling all these states a lack of initiative makes it easy to offer the wrong help.

Suppose you want to turn a public document into a short summary. You've opened the AI chat, but haven't typed anything. You have to decide which materials to provide, who the summary is for, and what a satisfactory result would look like. An experienced user might make those decisions almost automatically. For a newcomer, the empty box presents them all at once.

A reminder to explain your requirements clearly offers little help at that point. Explaining them is precisely where you're stuck. If the AI instead offers a few common purposes, lets you choose one, and identifies the material still needed, part of the task becomes actionable. You haven't suddenly become more industrious. The next step has become recognizable.

There is a concrete precedent in [Paul Graham's “Do Things that Don't Scale”](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html). Writing in 2013 about Stripe's early user recruitment, he described how the founders would help people install it on the spot once they agreed to try it, rather than merely send a link. This is an investor's observation and startup advice, not a controlled experiment estimating the approach's success rate.

The installation is what interests me. It brought interested people into actual use without requiring them to resolve every integration difficulty first. For your summary, the AI could check whether the materials are complete and prepare a starting point you can change. If a product leaves every cost of getting started to the user, then looks for initiative among the few who manage to begin, it can easily mistake difficulties it has left unresolved for the user's personality.

But this creates a problem. If the AI supplies the starting point, the direction, and the standard, and you merely agree, how does the product know you need the summary? A yes can mean several things: approval, casual experimentation, or even reluctance to reject an assistant that has already put in so much work.

In [his 2009 discussion of customer development](https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/), Steve Blank emphasized testing whether a product solves a customer's problem and is compelling enough to make them use or buy it, rather than stopping at feature requests. His account draws on his startup experience. It helps us examine the basis of a judgment; it does not supply a fixed formula for every market.

After offering help, then, a product still needs places where willingness can become visible. Once the summary exists, will you use it for the task you originally had in mind? Will you point out what doesn't work? Those subsequent actions tell us more about the need in question than whether you typed the first message. A single action can still depend on time and other circumstances, so it needs to be understood within the particular task.

Two questions need separating: how to help you begin, and how to tell whether you want to continue. A product can answer the first by doing more work itself. For the second, it must leave room for dissatisfaction, a change of direction, or an end to the task. When an interface only lets you keep clicking forward, more compliance can mean less information about willingness.

Help can also change the value of the task itself. Once preparing the materials takes less effort, you may find uses for the summary that previously seemed not worth the trouble. In that case, the new purpose develops through use. It shouldn't be rewritten as proof that the product had already divined a hidden need. Graham's essay also notes that users' needs can change in response to the arrival of a product.

This adds a requirement for proactive service: goals must be allowed to change. You choose a starting point, then discover a more suitable purpose as you work; the AI needs to accommodate that change. If the system treats an initial choice as a permanent preference, greater activity can take it further off course. Helping you make fewer decisions at the beginning should create the conditions for more fitting decisions later.

## Do you want a result, or an ability you can develop?

You may say: I just want the summary. Why must I be developed?

Fair enough. A tool has no right to set your growth goals. If you're delegating a task, asking it to carry more of the work is perfectly reasonable. You don't need to prove your initiative by writing an elegant specification and then performing earnest learning for the AI. Setting a clear delivery standard and checking whether the result is usable already involves consequential judgment.

The kind of help needed changes only if you also want to become better at reading material, identifying the main points, or expressing a judgment. Delivering a finished summary then fulfills only part of the goal. You receive usable text, but may never make the decisions about what to include or leave out. Those decisions are precisely what you wanted to practice.

I find it useful to distinguish where the effort is being saved within the same task. Finding files, adjusting formatting, and repeatedly moving material around can take as little of your attention as possible. Deciding what the author's most important reason is, or where the evidence is insufficient, deserves some thought of your own. The dividing line follows your goal, not how strenuous an operation looks.

A [2026 study by Zara Contractor and Germán Reyes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849) involved undergraduates learning an unfamiliar topic and writing an essay, with or without AI, in a proctored, timed assignment. They then completed unaided assessments, with another assessment a week later. The authors' abstract reports higher test scores in the group given AI access, with a smaller gain persisting after a week. Gains remained among students who used AI as a tutor and faded among those who had it write for them.

What is available for verification here is the authors' abstract, not the full methodological detail. In particular, it is insufficient to attribute the entire difference between those specific uses causally to the way AI was used. The assignment also does not establish what long-term use of every AI product would do. It does remind me that fewer operations need not mean less learning: we need to ask where the saved time goes, and who does the essential thinking.

Return to your summary. If the AI has already organized the material, you can first choose what you consider its most important point, then ask it to find passages that support and challenge that judgment. The tool handles retrieval; you still make a choice. Accepting a finished result is also legitimate. The two processes simply shouldn't be recorded as equivalent practice.

In [their 2019 examination of deliberate practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6824411/), Ericsson and Harwell emphasize explicit improvement goals, informative feedback, and repeated attempts revised after reflection. Deliberate practice has a strict meaning here, including individualized training guided by a qualified teacher. A casual exercise with AI does not automatically meet those conditions. Nor does this conceptual discussion establish that practice duration explains all differences in ability.

It does help make the target of practice concrete. If you want to become better at starting tasks, practice turning a vague wish into an actionable decision. If you want to become better at reading, practice identifying what matters and finding evidence for it. Repeated confirmation practices confirmation. Finishing the workflow does not mean you've practiced the ability you hoped to develop.

Help can work like a library catalog. The catalog makes things easier to find; the reader still decides which book to take and which chapter to open. With AI, organized materials and clear options support the search, while deciding what deserves attention remains your choice. But AI also generates and ranks the options, potentially narrowing the range. Having buttons to choose from isn't enough; you also need to be able to ask for something outside them.

That places a limit on cultivating initiative. Turning every routine task into an exercise gives you another burden. When you need the result promptly, let AI do more. When you want to practice a particular ability, keep that step for yourself. Both modes should be easy to choose, and easy to change your mind about.

## What changes when you start the second time?

Will someone who receives help become more willing to initiate things later? That interests me, but the available evidence doesn't justify declaring an answer.

[Anthropic's 2026 report on learning curves](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report) analyzed Claude usage from February of that year. Users who had registered at least six months earlier and were still using the service collaborated with AI more iteratively, and their conversations were more often judged successful by the model. This is a comparison of different users observed at that time: earlier users may already have been better suited to the tasks, and people who had stopped using Claude were not observed. The report did not establish why they stopped. It therefore does not establish that time on the platform itself caused improvement. Conversation success is also different from completion of a real-world task.

The reminder I take from this is simple: you can't require newcomers to behave from the outset like the experienced users who remain today. Clear instructions from an experienced user may depend on experience, or on the kinds of tasks they repeatedly do. Making that outcome everyone's entrance requirement skips exactly the process a product could help with.

For the summary, I'd rather look at how the next task begins. Can you find the material more easily? Can you bring a requirement of your own? Can you recognize that the AI's default purpose doesn't fit? These changes need not involve more typing. Deliberately having the tool do more, or canceling a task without value, can also indicate a clearer sense of what you want.

Conversely, more confirmations may simply mean that the system asks more questions. To distinguish the two, you must be allowed to change the default arrangement. If AI always suggests the same direction and you can only accept or leave, what the product can observe is constrained by its own options. Sometimes users show no ideas of their own because there is nowhere to put them.

You can take a small task you've been putting off and try the way of observing it that I propose here. This approach has not been tested in a study. I'd keep the record minimal, enough for a later comparison. Life doesn't need to become a scorecard.

1. Before starting, identify the exact step that's blocking you. If the material is missing, record that. If you don't understand the standard, record that. If the task isn't worth doing, record that too. Hold off on labeling yourself proactive or passive. Tell the AI which difficulty you want it to address and whether you want a result, or also want to practice a particular judgment.

2. Have the AI help you past that step, then put the result to actual use. For the summary, once you've chosen its purpose, use it for the intended task. Record a change you made, a suggestion you rejected, or a reason you decided not to continue. It's fine if you change nothing. What matters is whether your reason comes from your understanding of the task.

3. When a similar task comes along, see what help you need to begin. Keep the goal and tool conditions similar, and try to change only the step that blocked you last time. If you still need the same support, keep it. If you've developed a standard of your own, have the AI work to it. Don't deliberately withdraw necessary help as a test of initiative.

This way of observing has no universal score, and two attempts cannot establish long-term growth. You can use it to distinguish support that gets something started from arrangements that leave you merely following along. Even discovering that you don't need the task is more useful than agreeing repeatedly while tasks pile up.

Product designers should preserve those distinctions in their records too. Needing help to begin does not mean needing prompts forever. Rejecting a suggestion does not mean having no need. Completing a task does not mean learning the judgment it involved. If usage frequency alone becomes the measure of initiative, more reminders can be mistaken for greater ability.

For yourself, a fairer question is: the last time I couldn't get started, what exactly was missing, and which decision did the product leave to me? That question can lead to a concrete adjustment. Blaming yourself for laziness rarely offers such a clear next step.

You can start that summary now.
