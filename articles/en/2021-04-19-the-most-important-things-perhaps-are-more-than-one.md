Howard Marks, the author of *The Most Important Thing*, is a master who thrives in economic crises and achieves massive investment returns. He has earned a well-known nickname: "The Fierce Wall Street Vulture." His influence in the investment world is so great that even Warren Buffett, the so-called "Oracle of Omaha," counts him as a disciple. Since the 1990s, Howard has been writing annual investment memos for global investors. Buffett once said, "The first thing I do when I see an email with Howard Marks' investment memo is to read it immediately."

The core idea of the book can be summarized as: maintain independent thinking, be an inverse thinker, not a trend follower. Maintaining independent thinking is something we all need to do. Starting from *Beyond Feelings*, I began to have a shallow understanding of the logic world, and I experienced the charm of logical thinking. With more independent thinking, many problems in life can be resolved. Honestly, before reading this book, I just thought logic was important, but I hadn't systematically studied it. This made me recall why universities, as centers of higher education, couldn't provide logic education? How beneficial it would be to have such courses. Carrying this question, I read about Charlie Munger. His reading and thinking content shocked ordinary people. He once said, "What the university couldn't provide, I'll make up for myself." These words suddenly awakened me. Yes, why do we always expect or rely on teachers, rather than exploring and finding things on our own? What did Charlie Munger find? What couldn't the university teach him? This is the well-known concept of the "multiples of thinking models" that everyone now knows. Munger believes there are 100 thinking models, and with them, we can have a comprehensive understanding of the world. Many people might be scared by this number, but in reality, having just 10 thinking models can help us become experts in a field, allowing us to think about problems within that domain.

In today's internet age, what excuse do we have not to learn, given the abundance of resources and information? For example, if you want to have a basic understanding of a field, you can find a review, look up related papers, and after reading a few, you'll have a basic grasp. What comes next is what we need to do: independent thinking. In this era of information overload, there's too much information that can distract us, leading us to get lost in the data stream. Li Xiaolai once mentioned that we should read less from self-media articles. Although I myself write self-media articles, I must say that there's not much worth reading. A lot of the information in these articles doesn't hold up in the past or future, and it's hard to justify. Read books diligently, think carefully, and form your own judgment. This is what I've felt most during my daily reading. Use logic well, and let independent thinking guide our lives. When things are unclear, ask yourself more questions—perhaps the answers will reveal themselves.

Being an inverse thinker, not a trend follower, ultimately still requires the ability to think independently. The second-level thinking in the book also deeply inspired me. When we think about problems, we often tend to be one-sided and overly focused on conclusions, which is irrational. First-level thinkers have the same views on the same things and usually reach the same conclusions, making it hard to achieve outstanding results and difficult to beat the market. Second-level thinking is profound and complex. To achieve outstanding investment results, our view of value shouldn't be conventional, and it must be correct. Second-level thinking allows us to see more. For example, when looking at an industry, we need to not only understand its history but also all the companies in that industry globally, and the supply and demand chain. This perspective and understanding are more comprehensive, and the strategic angle is more macro. We need to think with a diversified perspective. A single dimension can easily lead us into thinking traps, and we might make mistakes without realizing it. Being an inverse thinker, using independent thinking for investment analysis, isn't about following trends but making personal value judgments. Analyze based on your environment and situation, reduce investment risks, set reasonable expectations, and think before investing. I hope we can all gain energy from this book, a classic investment work that remains fresh with each reading, and keep reading!

When I first saw the title of the book, *The Most Important Thing*, I thought it might be a bit clickbaity. Is it really that important? It seemed like every single thing was important, and after reading, it really was. Using the term "most important" doesn't seem excessive. This made me think of the many logical fallacies mentioned in my logic class. Sometimes, we're really unreliable. For example, our understanding of dollar-cost averaging can be unreliable. When we know that the best and simplest investment strategy is dollar-cost averaging, we might unconsciously fall into a trap, thinking that everything else is irrelevant, and that we just need to focus on dollar-cost averaging. What other things matter? This is a thought I've had before, which shows how shallow my understanding is. If we only know about dollar-cost averaging and don't have a deeper understanding or thinking about investing, we'll still be swayed by market analysis and fall into biases. In fact, many decisions in the investment process are related to logic. If we can't better understand our thinking process and the structure of logic, we might get lost while reading. We're easily blinded by various forms of bias, and there are plenty of logical pitfalls waiting for us. Some say that reading one book gives you one thinking model. I think this book provides more than one thinking model, which is something we can apply in our thinking and learning in the investment field. The value of a model isn't in being universal or solving everything—it's dynamic and context-dependent. Choose the right model for the situation, and it still has boundaries and limitations. The worst is using the wrong model and sticking to our wrong views. Stay open, constantly reflect, and see through the essence.

The greatest value of a shared reading activity is in discussion and exchange, sharing our views, and learning from each other. The core of it, however, is our second-level thinking. I didn't truly realize this before. First-level thinking is the most common: people have the same views on the same things and usually reach the same conclusions, making it hard to achieve outstanding results or beat the market. Second-level thinking is profound and complex. To achieve outstanding investment results, our view of value shouldn't be conventional, and it must be correct. Second-level thinking allows us to see more. For example, when looking at an industry, we need to not only understand its history but also all the companies in that industry globally, and the supply and demand chain. This perspective and understanding are more comprehensive, and the strategic angle is more macro. Logic helps us form second-level thinking by helping us build logical concepts, understand professional terms in the logic system, and deepen our understanding through real-life and related examples. This allows us to break free from first-level thinking, understand the process and results of thinking, and more objectively and comprehensively understand ourselves. At the same time, it also points out many logical fallacies, helping us gradually develop second-level thinking, stand on it, and see more. Facing various traps in the investment market, a logical perspective and independent thinking are essential. It's a great honor to be part of the practice group and to upgrade our cognitive system with everyone!
