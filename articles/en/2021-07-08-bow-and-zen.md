# Bow and Zen

> Published: 2021-07-08 · [中文](../zh/2021-07-08-%E5%BC%93%E4%B8%8E%E7%A6%85.md) | [English](../en/2021-07-08-bow-and-zen.md)

---

*The Bow and Zen* is a book that many people have mentioned, but perhaps fate had not yet brought me to it, so I never read it. This year, a book friend shared *The Real Practice Diary of a Enlightened One*, which sparked my interest to read it. I went to the library to look for it, but didn't find it. However, I stumbled upon *The Record of Learning the Bow and Realizing Zen* by accident. I thought it might be similar to *The Bow and Zen* and *The Real Practice Diary of an Enlightened One*, so I borrowed it to read. Coincidentally, both *The Record of Learning the Bow and Realizing Zen* and *The Bow and Zen* are translations of the same original book written by Herrigel in 1948. *The Record of Learning the Bow and Realizing Zen* was translated by Yu Juezhong in 1989 and officially published in 1993, making it the earliest Chinese translation. It seems this was fate. This book is truly a classic that becomes more meaningful with each reading, a remarkable blend of Eastern and Western cultures. It's not thick, the language is simple and accessible. If you read it with a calm mind, you can finish it in a day. When you close the book, you may feel a sudden sense of clarity. But a couple of days later, you might feel like you haven't fully grasped its meaning, and you want to read it again. In this way, I read it three times.

This book is the record of Herrigel, a German philosopher, who studied with the archery master Abo Kenjō in Japan for six years, and it documents his journey of realizing Zen. From learning Zen to realizing it; from learning archery to realizing Zen; step by step, gradually. He repeatedly experienced deep insights, and repeatedly fell into despair. The master only revealed the path when he reached the point of despair. Only then did Herrigel gradually realize the bow, the arrow, the hand, the heart, and everything else... He moved from complexity to simplicity, and then from simplicity, he saw the complexity again. He knew that there was something more important deep within things. The surface appearances often create illusions, and superficial understanding only thickens the illusion. Only through vivid experience and verification can one break through the illusion and see the truth clearly. From then on, he held on tightly, neither letting go nor holding on.

This book vividly portrays the Zen state of "bow and Zen are one, the archer and the target are one, one arrow, one lifetime." It also made me unconsciously think of the Zen flavor of "tea and Zen are one, one tea, one meeting, one meeting, one lifetime." How to reach such a Zen state, however, is something people often fail to grasp, and they often remain outside its door. Herrigel, through his personal journey of learning archery and realizing Zen, genuinely describes an effective teaching method to reach this state, as well as how to overcome various obstacles during the process of realization and enter the state of enlightenment.

Someone asked: What is Zen? The master said: When hungry, eat; when tired, sleep. Zen is in our daily lives. Life is full of Zen moments. Seeing mountains as mountains, then seeing mountains not as mountains, and then seeing mountains as mountains again. This is the three stages of Zen practice and realization.
