# Innovation in Motion

> Published: 2021-06-08 · [中文](../zh/2021-06-08-%E5%88%9B%E6%96%B0%E8%BF%9B%E8%A1%8C%E6%97%B6.md) | [English](../en/2021-06-08-innovation-in-motion.md)

---

In *The Innovator's Dilemma*, Professor Christensen lists a series of examples. For instance, drones for manned fighter jets, and online banking for physical banks. We are actually familiar with many other examples, such as Sony's "Walkman" revolutionizing the concept of audio devices; or Southwest Airlines in the U.S., with its low-cost model and service reduction, which has had a significant impact on the industry, and so on.

These examples happen every day around us, which is why Christensen's theory resonates with people. Christensen once wrote: Why do successful companies, which are not lacking in innovative talent, ideas, or funding, still fail? Why can successful companies never return to their former glory? Don't think that successful companies will always remain successful. Often, they are afraid that their existing successful markets will be eroded, their existing business models, and their habitual ways of organizing operations will be affected. Therefore, they can only cautiously push out new products along the original path, but these products often fail to meet users' needs. It is precisely for this reason that small companies get the opportunity. The real threat to successful companies comes from new entrants without the burden of past success. Their products are not necessarily better in quality or performance than the original ones, but they are cheaper and have lower performance.

Christensen proposed the "task-to-be-done" theory. He said, when people have a certain need, that is a "task-to-be-done." For example, if you want to have lunch at noon, the act of having lunch is a "task-to-be-done." You use an online food ordering app because it can help you complete this task. If the meal you ordered is good, you'll use it again next time; if it's not, you'll switch to another app. Therefore, what people care about is not the product itself, but what the product can help them accomplish, and how well that task is completed. If you can think about your product from this perspective, other competitors will find it hard to disrupt you. But how exactly should you do this?

First, you should focus on what the user is trying to do, rather than just the product itself. Just like that classic saying, people don't buy a drill; they want to make a hole in the wall. But in reality, when people think about products, they tend to focus on features or functions rather than on what the customer wants to achieve. Therefore, Christensen said that the most important thing when making a product is to ask the customer what they want to use the product for. Only in this way can you continuously innovate, and your product will have a competitive edge.

Second, you should identify those truly unmet needs. Christensen said that sometimes, we can find opportunities in things people are unwilling to do. For example, in the U.S., even a simple throat inflammation check requires an appointment, waiting in the clinic, and then a prescription after diagnosis. The process is very cumbersome, and many people get impatient while waiting in the hospital. Walgreens, the largest retail pharmacy in the U.S., seized this opportunity by setting up small clinic services in its retail stores. Common illnesses don't require appointments or long waits, and patients can get prescriptions directly. The service is also very attentive.

Third, besides finding user needs, you should also create an exceptional user experience that your competitors can't easily copy. For example, the toy brand "Barbie" generates over $500 million in annual sales. The reason this brand has remained popular for so long is that it's not selling dolls, but an experience. "Barbie" sets up doll hospitals in its flagship stores, where broken toys can be repaired. Some stores even have restaurants where parents can take their children to eat, or even hold birthday parties. Going to a "Barbie" toy store becomes a special day, and the doll becomes a catalyst for family gatherings. Additionally, the packaging box of "Barbie" has a ribbon that adds 27 seconds to the unboxing process, increasing the cost by two cents. Why add a ribbon? The founder of "Barbie" thought that if children could wait a few seconds while unboxing, taking the ribbon out first and then pulling the doll out from the cotton paper under the lid, it would give them a sense of novelty. This experience is completely different from simply picking up a toy from a store shelf. Therefore, if you can create a unique user experience, you don't have to worry about threats from competitors.

Finally, Christensen said that the real challenge is building a company where all its metrics revolve around satisfying the user. But if you can focus on this goal and identify the tasks people are eager to complete, you can better predict innovation, and the returns from innovation will be higher.
