I used to be an English teacher at New Oriental in Hangzhou. I had the privilege of teaching over 2,000 students and spent more than 3,000 hours in the classroom. At my busiest, I taught 12 hours a day for a month. Now, all that is in the past. I now prefer to call myself *The Linguist*, which is not an exaggeration, but rather a way to tell every companion: *Everyone is the linguist!* Everyone can become a language expert, and we've all walked the path of language exploration. This idea comes from Canadian scholar Steve Kaufmann, who wrote a book titled *The Linguist* many years ago. He shared his journey of learning over 30 languages, which greatly inspired me. We have no reason to refuse learning a language if we don’t master it well—it may be due to a lack of clear goals or not finding a compelling reason to use it. I firmly believe that we can master a language if we are confident. Our brains are incredibly flexible, allowing us to change how we express ourselves. Different languages are just different ways of expression, and our brain's adaptability ensures we can master any foreign language. So, don’t give yourself excuses. Essentially, we are not learning a language—we are using it. Never see yourself as a beginner; just use it!

Since I've been in English education, countless people have asked how to learn English well, what methods to use, and various details. Looking back, I was also often asked in school why English is so good and what learning methods to use. My off-the-cuff response was, “English is really simple!” Now that I think about it, that was irresponsible and could mislead others. Does this mean English is hard? Does it require a huge investment of time and effort? My conclusion is that language learning is simple, but it requires repetition, repetition, and more repetition. Whether it's language learning or understanding any knowledge, the most fundamental and simple method is repetition.

The Learning Pyramid theory is a well-known modern learning theory, first discovered and proposed by Edgar Dale, a famous learning expert in the U.S., in 1946, as a research outcome of the National Training Laboratory in Maine, USA. It uses numbers to show how much learners can remember two weeks later depending on the learning method. Learning methods with less than 30% retention are individual or passive learning, while those with over 50% retention involve team learning, active learning, and participatory learning.

This is where the phrase “Teaching is the best way to learn” comes from. The difference in learning efficiency lies in whether we actively seek out information, which is driven by intrinsic motivation. To understand why you're learning English, find a significant reason, and then language learning can be sustained. The reason is a harsh reality—most people don’t have a real need to learn English.

Li Xiaolai once said, “Often, we can’t become excellent because we haven’t developed excellent habits. Further, why haven’t we developed good habits? Because we don’t see the things we do as a necessity.” At the start of the new year, many platforms love it when users write down their New Year wishes. On the Dedao app, there’s a time capsule where you fill in your wishes and send them a year later. You can imagine the result: I didn’t complete one of my wishes because I suddenly got sick and couldn’t take the BEC Advanced exam. It was really frustrating, and I spent a day lying in a hotel. Other wishes I did complete, simply because I quit my job and fully prepared for going abroad, which became my necessity. I had to complete it. Often, it’s not that things are hard to achieve or impossible, but that we lack a reason to keep going. From an economic perspective, such things have little impact on supply and demand. So, what makes something a necessity? I think of it as something you must do, whether you like it or not. If it becomes something you enjoy and can’t live without, then it’s a necessity.

Many people say English is important because others think it is, but you don’t feel its importance. From my own experience, I never spent much time on English learning, and it was relatively easy compared to other subjects. I only started serious learning in university, where I majored in civil engineering. Going to Germany for further study was a great choice, so I began my long journey learning German. After learning German, I realized how wonderful English is—it’s so beautiful, with fewer rules and clearer grammar structures than German. Of course, German is easy to read, with pronunciation more standardized than English, so it’s easy to read, but you just don’t know what it means. Through comparative language learning, I discovered more motivation to learn languages, opening up my perspective and allowing me to see the world through different expressions. For example, what did I do while learning German? I often used a dictionary, and my first choice was a Chinese-German dictionary. But people who’ve used English-Chinese dictionaries know that some translations are inaccurate. The Chinese meanings are the same, and the example sentences are even the same, just with different word order, often leading to confusion. For instance, *important* and *significant* both mean “important,” but their scope and degree are entirely different. You can say *significant small*, but can you say *important small*? While using a Chinese-German dictionary, I often encountered vague definitions and unclear concepts. At that point, an English-German dictionary saved me, helping me understand the differences between English and German, and thus grasp their true meanings.

Where is the necessity in language learning? Some people think it’s for going abroad. After living in the West for many years, many Chinese people still speak their native dialect, and a few simple words in a foreign language are enough. After all, there are many Chinese communities everywhere, and Chinatown is common. Within their cultural communities, they have little contact with the outside world. Plus, modern speech-to-text translation is highly accurate and convenient. So, we need to think clearly: what is our real necessity, or what is the inevitable cause? I’m currently studying for a master’s degree in education at the University of Manchester. Honestly, I can understand things just by using software translation. So why do I miss out on so much useful information, or maybe I’m even studying translation? Using English to its fullest—reading, looking up literature, writing exercises, communicating with professors and classmates—can be applied in all aspects. What if I’m afraid of making mistakes? Why am I afraid when writing in Chinese? I’m not. Why am I afraid when speaking English? Is it because I overestimate English? Li Xiaolai once said that high school students often score better on the TOEFL than college students, even though college students have studied longer and should have higher language proficiency. In reality, many high school students who score high on the TOEFL and apply to universities in the U.S. are surrounded by role models. They don’t think English is hard; they just need more practice. In contrast, college students often say English is hard, and passing the CET-4 or CET-6 makes them “language experts.” Taking an English elective feels like listening to a foreign language. Is it really that hard? If you look at it from a long-term perspective and use English actively, it’s not that hard, but it doesn’t mean you can learn it by just lying around.

Necessity is an economic concept. For us, how to apply necessity in life is to assign it a significant meaning, make it appear every day, spend time refining and practicing it, and believe in the compounding effect of time. Necessity will eventually shape our excellence. Let’s encourage each other!

Think clearly: why are you learning English? Even if it’s to improve a specific skill for an exam, that’s a specific goal. But the question is: after you no longer need to take English exams, why would you still learn English? Don’t use vague reasons to comfort yourself. Starting without clear goals will lead to blind learning, which brings no real improvement. The key is to understand that our goal is to use English, not to learn it. For example, Tony improved his vocabulary and grammar while learning German, but he still stammered when speaking. He studied, but never applied it. The world in the textbook is different from real life. No one in Yiwu will speak to you in the way the textbook shows. We’ve also seen people who have lived abroad for years but still can’t speak a foreign language well because they never intended to change completely or use the language. After all, Chinese and translation software have already solved all the problems. Using a language isn’t that hard—just use it. There are so many methods and secrets, but how many have you actually used?

From my perspective, I don’t explain language points. Many short video platforms and content areas focus on knowledge transmission, but I don’t think these fragmented knowledge points will help us much. Why? I’m sure many of you have heard of the *New Concept English* series. Professor Alexander is very impressive, and the content is rich, but the problem is that it has four volumes. Because of the four volumes, how many people have actually completed the series? Why can’t people finish it? It’s because they lack clear goals. Why would you set finishing *New Concept English* as a goal, and then what?

Professor Qian Yingyi once discussed the biggest problem with education in China: there is a systemic bias in our understanding and practice of education. This bias is that we equate education with knowledge and limit it to knowledge. Teachers’ job is to teach knowledge, students’ duty is to learn knowledge, and the college entrance exam tests knowledge. So knowledge has become almost the entire content of education. What else is education besides knowledge? Education is like a pair of glasses that help us see the world more clearly, but don’t confuse education with the goal itself. Education must go beyond knowledge to help us innovate and grow. Einstein once said, “The value of a university education is not in remembering many facts, but in training the brain to think.”

I hope more people can start their language learning journey. We use foreign languages, not just focus on knowledge points. To my followers, I won’t explain a single word, obsess over a pronunciation, or practice a grammar repeatedly. I truly hope to help more people start using language. Start by reading an English book, and wherever you have problems, start there. If you want to become a learning expert or a language expert, face the difficulties head-on and overcome them. For example, if you're preparing for an overseas application, how to choose a major is simple: just go online, check the official website, gather information, and look up words you don’t know. You’ll definitely gain something from it.

Finally, here are the basic elements I think are essential for using English. You can adjust them according to your own situation:

1. A book you must read and read repeatedly:

   Li Xiaolai’s *Everyone Can Use English*

   Online open-source reading: http://lixiaolai.com/#/everyone-can-use-english/

2. Phonetics

   Phonetics is the foundation of spoken English. It’s recommended to learn the widely used IPA International Phonetic Alphabet.

   Video Resource 1: Bilibili - English Rabbit

   Video Resource 2: Youku - BBC Phonetics Teaching

   After learning, it’s just a matter of practice. Mastering the basics won’t take much time.

3. Dictionary

   The biggest difference between an English teacher and a student may be just one thing: an English teacher must use a dictionary.

   How to choose? Whether to go for a physical or digital version, refer to Li Xiaolai’s book.

4. Feedback

   Mirrors, recordings, video recordings...

   Various forms of feedback. The best feedback is from yourself.

5. Accuracy over fluency or native-like

   Don’t pursue fluency or native-like English. It’s just an extra flourish. The foundation is accuracy. Most of the time, we fail to be accurate, so start from the basics.

6. Start from what interests you, what’s useful, or what’s urgent

   There are many materials, but choose what suits you best. More importantly, it should be something you can use consistently.

7. Listening, speaking, reading, and writing are skills. Vocabulary and grammar form a system. Cognitive strategies are important. Focus on improving metacognition.

   It’s said that seven is the memory limit. I’ve listed these seven points to help you use English. Again, emphasize that it’s about using English, not learning it. Water dripping on stone eventually wears it away. Every little progress is progress. Never just think without acting. Take action and work hard!
