Chinese martial arts are an essential part of traditional Chinese culture. In the early years of the Republic of China, it was briefly called "guoshu" (later officially adopted by the Central Guoshu Academy, and still used in Taiwan today), regarded as the essence of Chinese culture, hence also called "guicui" (national treasure). Due to historical development and regional distribution, various schools and styles have emerged. The main content of Chinese martial arts includes combat techniques, fighting methods, defensive strategies, and weapon usage. These can be divided into two categories: theory and practice. The practical aspect mainly refers to physical exercise and unique Chinese martial arts qigong and health preservation functions. The theoretical aspect comes from the accumulated experience and records of previous generations' martial arts and boxing manuals. Therefore, it reflects the Chinese nation's understanding of combat techniques and strategies. Combined with accumulated experience, it aims at self-reliance, self-strengthening, and physical health, and during the practice of routines, it displays the graceful movements of the body. Chinese martial arts often carry cultural characteristics of ideological refinement and humanistic philosophy, and have a profound influence on contemporary Chinese popular culture.

The origin of Chinese martial arts can be traced back to the primitive society. At that time, humans used tools like sticks and clubs to fight wild animals, gradually accumulating some defensive and offensive experience. The practice of field hunting in the Shang Dynasty was considered an important means of martial arts training.

During the Shang and Zhou Dynasties, "wuwu" (military dance) was used to train soldiers and boost morale. Therefore, it was believed that martial arts could be practiced in the form of dance. In the Zhou Dynasty, "xu" (a type of school) included archery, chariot driving, and dance in its educational content. During the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, various feudal states placed great emphasis on the application of combat techniques on the battlefield. Duke Huan of Qi held "jueshi" (duel competitions) in spring and autumn to select heroes from across the country. During this period, the production and development of swords reached an unprecedented level. In the Qin and Han Dynasties, wrestling and fencing were popular, and there was a custom of entertainment and dancing. For example, at the Hongmen Banquet, Xiang Zhuang danced with a sword, a form that was closer to today's martial arts routines. The use of spears in the Han Dynasty reached its peak, and various spear techniques began to emerge. It is said that Hua Tuo first created the "Wu Qin Xi" (Five Animal Frolics), which is considered the beginning of Chinese martial arts.

People generally believe that martial arts originated from the need for combat, but the book *Chinese Martial Arts: History and Culture* suggests that although some aspects of martial arts came from military needs, most actually originated from private duels among the people. The requirements for martial arts used in military purposes differ from those in civilian contexts.

In the TV program *Zheng He's Voyage to the West*, *National Geographic Magazine* suggests that during the Northern and Southern Dynasties, the founder of Buddhist Chan Zen, Bodhidharma, might have brought Indian martial arts to the Shaolin Temple in Songshan, Henan, in the middle of China.

The early development of Chinese martial arts mostly lacked written records and could only be passed down orally from teacher to student.

The number of martial arts schools in China is also rare in the world of martial arts. According to statistics, there are currently about 300 types of boxing styles in China with clear history, orderly lineage, unique style, and a complete system. In *A History of Chinese Sports* by Guo Xifeng, it is mentioned that the numerous schools of Chinese martial arts are due to differences in geography, climate, and people between the north and south. Northerners are tall, and the harsh northern climate leads to northern martial arts being powerful and open. The south has more water, and southerners are shorter, so their martial arts are more delicate. There is a saying: "Southern fists, northern legs." Of course, this is not absolute. For example, the Baguazhang (Eight Trigrams Palm), which originated in the north, rarely uses leg attacks [source requested], while the Wing Chun (Ying Chun) from the south has many leg techniques.

Chinese martial arts have many schools, but there is no unified naming method. Some are named after regions (such as the Henan School of Yinxiquan), some after mountains and rivers (such as the Wudang School), and some after the surname of the master (such as Yang Style Tai Chi). In the past, these schools were roughly divided into internal and external martial arts based on whether they focused on internal or external energy cultivation.

After 1949, when the Communist Party established the People's Republic of China, the traditional transmission and martial arts schools were halted. Only some schools' martial arts were compiled into fitness and performance routines and taught in primary and secondary schools.

After the reform and opening up, martial arts were generally divided into routines and combat fighting. With the development of combat sports, Chinese martial arts have now shifted toward more practical free fighting.
