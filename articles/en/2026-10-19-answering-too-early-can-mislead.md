# Answering Too Early Can Mislead

> 发布日期:2026-10-19 · [中文](../zh/2026-10-19-回答得太早，也会误事.md) | [English](../en/2026-10-19-answering-too-early-can-mislead.md)

---

How much must you figure out before you begin? My answer is: resolve only the questions you cannot take the next step without answering. Write down the other questions and return to them when you have material from doing the work. That may sound like an argument for thinking less. I care about when we think. If a question can only be answered by what someone does with a product, by the mistakes we make, or by the consequences of an action, an elegant answer prepared at a desk can turn a guess into something that feels like knowledge.

This matters especially when we work with AI. It can research, compare, and produce at remarkable speed, so we can answer far more questions before starting. Yet the ability to produce an answer does not mean the question is ready for one. Get the order wrong, and a capable tool helps us prepare an untested idea with increasing diligence.

## Some questions cannot be answered yet

An AI summary of a creator interview separates startup questions into two kinds. Some are necessary to begin; others, such as equipment and software details, can be handled while doing the work. That distinction comes from the interviewee's experience. I propose taking it one step further: the order of questions depends on where their answers come from. Look up a fact when a fact is missing. Put a piece of work into the world when an answer requires actual use. Investigate risk before acting when the cost of a mistake could be high.

Take creative work. You need to know who the first piece is for, what those readers are trying to do, and where they can encounter it. You do not need to identify the best platform in the world or choose a direction for life. A place where the first reader can find the work and reply is enough to begin. Font size, recording gear, and automated publishing may matter later. Before there is a piece to use, however, many such choices are arguments with our imagination. Once a version exists, the questions become specific: Which passage is hard to read on a phone? Which sentence is hard to hear? At what exact point does publishing get stuck?

Preparation feels like progress, which makes this distinction easy to miss. Comparing equipment is more comfortable than showing someone an unfinished article. Asking AI for ten growth strategies is easier than putting the first piece in front of a reader. Preparation has its uses, but a comparison table records options, not reader behavior. It can reduce known risks. It cannot tell you whether anyone wants to keep reading.

I try to state the question in a form that is harder to dodge: Do I need a public fact, a finished piece of work, or an instance of actual use to answer this? Each shortage calls for a different move. Check the fact. Make the piece. Observe its use. Mix them together, and research becomes an attractive reason to delay delivery.

## The first version should bring back evidence

Buffer product manager Tom Redman tells an unglamorous origin story in his [2019 account of building Insights](https://buffer.com/resources/buffer-analyze-journey/). He began with about 20 to 30 calls with prospective customers, along with emails and survey responses. The early product that followed could display a few charts and create simple reports, while many features were still missing. About seven months into development, the team received its first $25 from a paying customer. The sequence matters. Conversations helped the team understand a problem; a rough paid version let them learn whether somebody would bear a cost for that particular answer.

At that point, the product worked only for existing paying Buffer customers. Redman explained its limitations individually, offered an early discount, and personally helped some users get started. Self-service signup came later, after the number of users grew. This is Buffer's own account, and it benefited from an existing brand and customer base. It is no forecast of what an ordinary creator will achieve. What it does show is the difference between two questions that often get merged. You can ask people about a difficulty before building. You can learn whether they will use and pay for this version only after a version exists.

In [his 2013 essay on early startup work](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham also describes founders recruiting users personally and doing manually what they hope to automate later. His essay offers observations and advice, not a controlled result that applies to every project. I take from it an order of operations: doing the work by hand can show you which parts are worth turning into a system. The point of the manual stage is to locate a problem that was previously vague, not to perform a ritual of hard work.

There is a practical dividing line here. Someone saying they want better reports is a lead. Which page they open, where they give up, and whether they will keep paying for a change are material you can use to design the next version. Collect only compliments, and your first version may appear right about everything. Record what people do, and its gaps become visible. Both kinds of feedback come from users, but they carry different weight.

Asking several people what they want to eat can help you plan a menu. Watching what they finish after you serve it answers a different question. Even an empty plate could reflect politeness rather than a favorite dish. A click, a payment, or silence around a piece of work can also have several explanations. Do not worship a single number or treat the first response as a verdict. Let it point to the next thing you need to test.

## Saved time does not become a better decision by itself

A reasonable objection is that AI can make the comparison, draft, and process quickly, so why not prepare everything before going out? I am happy to use it for those jobs. Saving time and improving the work are separate accounts. A [field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11436) found that, in the latter half of a six-month trial, workers who were given access to and used an integrated office AI tool spent about two fewer hours a week on email. The researchers did not detect a change in the quantity or composition of tasks from giving individuals the tool. These results come from the authors' abstract and concern work inside office applications; they cannot be transplanted directly to an independent creator's day.

What the finding brings into focus for me is a daily question: Who decides where the freed time goes? The tool will not assign that time to a customer conversation or automatically turn it into a more revealing trial. If the schedule has no place for real feedback, the time may be absorbed by the next batch of email or a more polished slide. The pace improves while the unresolved question stays put.

The same applies to an AI workbench. Ask for ten plans or ten titles and you have candidates. What you may actually need to learn is whether readers understand the piece, whether anyone will use a process twice, or whether a change reduces mistakes. Those answers are not hiding in the number of candidates. A machine can help make samples and organize observation notes. It cannot claim a solution has been validated using a record of use that does not yet exist.

I am not arguing against homework. Research rules out known errors and can lower the cost of the first attempt. But it reaches a boundary: sometimes the next piece of information can only come from action. Further searching may add information without reducing the uncertainty that matters. That is my cue to stop researching this question and arrange a test, rather than expand the search.

## Borrow an answer too early, and you may borrow away the practice

A subtler premature answer appears after the work has begun. At the first difficulty, we may ask AI to complete both the task and the judgment behind it. The obstacle disappears today. The next time we have to recognize a similar problem ourselves, our ability may be no stronger. A narrow but useful study puts some evidence behind this concern.

In a [randomized trial Anthropic published in 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills), 52 mostly junior developers learned a Python library they did not know. Randomized means chance determined who could use AI and who coded by hand, so the researchers could compare the groups. The AI-assisted group averaged 50% on an immediate quiz, compared with 67% for the group that coded by hand. The average difference in task completion time was not statistically significant: the observed difference was too uncertain to conclude that AI reliably changed completion time in this study. The quiz examined understanding shortly after the task, including code reading, debugging, and concepts. It did not measure long-term skill or every occupation. The lesson I take is modest: delivering one thing and becoming able to handle the next thing are outcomes we should count separately.

The researchers also recorded different ways participants used the assistant. Some delegated the coding; others asked about concepts and then wrote code themselves. The higher-scoring patterns are observations, not proof that one interaction style caused the scores. Still, they suggest a sensible practice when a skill matters to you: state your own guess, ask AI to find a flaw, then try the idea on fresh material yourself. A polished explanation from a machine can leave you with the feeling of understanding without that last attempt.

The distinction carries over to writing. AI can rewrite an opening paragraph, but if you cannot see why the original made a reader lose the thread, the next article may hit the same obstacle. It can list reasons a reader might object, but it does not know which sentence your actual readers cannot follow. Compare the versions with what readers actually did, and you may gain a judgment you can use next time. No one needs to type every word by hand. What matters is keeping the practice that lets you spot an error later.

## When must you stop before acting?

There is a serious objection to this sequence: acting early can expose other people to risk. Yes. When you handle personal data, take money, give health advice, or change files that cannot be restored, learning as you go is no excuse. Someone else may bear the error. Even a first version needs a clear scope, checked facts, and a way to reverse what can be reversed. If a trial has irreversible consequences, gather evidence before taking that step. Speed is not courage in this setting.

My test is plain: Who pays if I am wrong, and can that cost be undone? If the answer involves another person's privacy, money, or safety, narrow the attempt until it is safe enough to run. Publishing your own explanation and asking a reader what they could not understand usually has a much lower correction cost. It can go out earlier. Both situations require thought; evidence simply needs to arrive at different points in the process.

Another objection is fair too. Experienced people may already have the answer in their records. Then read the records. Past complaints, your own unsuccessful drafts, and public research are evidence left by prior action. There is no prize for ignoring them in order to look brave. The error I want to avoid is placing unsupported speculation ahead of evidence that already exists.

If you are doing something for the first time, distinguishing these sources can be hard. The following small experiment is my proposal, not a method the cited studies have tested. It needs no new app and no perfect process map:

1. Write down the decision in front of you and three questions keeping you from the next step. Mark each one as needing a fact check, a first version, or actual use before an answer is possible.
2. Check facts that would make the action clearly risky if left unresolved. Turn the other unknowns into a version small enough to deliver. Name who will use it and what action you intend to observe.
3. Put that version into a real setting. Record what people did and what they did not do. Do not count praise as demand, or decide that one silence has settled the whole question.
4. Return to your original questions. Which have evidence, which have merely been reworded, and which are now worth researching with AI? Choose one consequential change for the next attempt.

Suppose you want to make an introduction to AI for readers without a technical background. Before publishing, you need to check that you can use the materials you cite and choose which readers to approach first. You can do that now. You will only learn which explanation stops them or whether they try an exercise after they receive the guide. Deliver a short section, observe where a real reader pauses, and then decide whether to explain a term, change an example, or rebuild the section. This example illustrates the order of gathering evidence; one short section cannot guarantee what will happen with the whole guide.

Tonight, look at something you have postponed and ask a precise question: Can the answer I am waiting for really be found before I begin? If it can, check it. If it can only appear through use, arrange the first delivery you can observe. Do not let a question that is not yet answerable occupy the starting line forever.

Let the question speak when there is evidence.
