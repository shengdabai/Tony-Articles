# Save Your Patience for the Problem

> 发布日期：2026-11-15 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-15-把耐心留给问题.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-15-patience-for-the-problem.md)

---

When Viaweb approached merchants in its early days, some declined to build online stores with its software themselves but were willing to let the founders build stores for them. Paul Graham describes this in a [firsthand essay](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html). The same task could move forward once the way of doing it changed.

I think the most useful object of persistence is a problem worth solving. Tools, processes, and ways of presenting a solution can change, but each change should continue to address that problem. Otherwise, after all the activity, we may struggle to explain what we have actually ruled out.

This speaks to a practical question: what does persistence look like when you are working on an AI project? Generating something every day does not necessarily mean progress. Replacing an approach that does not work does not necessarily mean giving up. The distinction is whether your next attempt can build on what the previous one taught you.

## Are you staying with the problem or with your current approach?

Among my notes is a summary of someone else's reflections on building websites. It describes repeatedly switching projects after seeing other people's successes, while also trying to arrange the entire sequence of work before getting down to it. There was plenty of preparation and little completion. What catches my attention is the underlying difficulty: changing tools can feel like a fresh start, while revising an existing approach can feel like admitting defeat.

What interests me more is what survives each fresh start. A few files can certainly be useful. But if novelty alone decides what happens next, those files may not help us make even one fewer guess. The effort happened; the understanding failed to carry over.

You might reasonably object that persistence means sticking with a method long enough to see whether it works. Changing direction at the first obstacle makes that difficult. Yet keeping a method indefinitely can also amount to protecting an initial guess. More time has passed, but the guess has not necessarily been tested.

Viaweb helps make the distinction concrete. A merchant's refusal to operate the software could have several explanations. Perhaps opening an online store did not matter enough, or perhaps this way of delivering the service failed to make starting worthwhile. The refusal alone does not distinguish them. Building the store for the merchant preserved the task while changing who operated the software, producing information the original invitation had not supplied.

Graham recalls discovering missing features while making merchants' websites, adding those features, and then returning to the work. This is a founder's selected firsthand account. It shows how he discovered problems, without establishing a general success rate for the approach. The useful detail is how a need became specific: a feature was no longer merely something a founder thought should exist. Its absence was obstructing an actual task.

So writing the problem as finishing a particular tool is too restrictive. It makes the tool the answer from the outset. For the Viaweb example, a more useful question is how to help merchants who want online stores get those stores built. Operating the software themselves, getting assistance, or changing the software can all remain possible approaches.

Even that question has limits. Getting a store built does not mean it will earn money, or that every merchant needs the same product. Defining a question clearly does not mean packing every distant ambition into it. Acknowledging what the current task can answer keeps us from inflating what we have learned.

There is another objection: if you picked the wrong problem, wouldn't holding it steady make it easier to trap yourself? Yes. A merchant might accept help simply because someone else is doing the work. That does not establish an intention to keep running the store, let alone a willingness to pay. Graham's account does not eliminate those explanations. Acceptance of help cannot quietly become proof of a viable market.

By holding a question steady, I mean doing so temporarily, during an attempt whose costs you can bear. When that attempt ends, examine the question itself. Whether the task still matters, whether users are willing to keep investing effort, and whether feedback repeatedly points elsewhere can all justify changing it. Keep the grounds for the change, rather than granting your initial choice permanent immunity.

Following a recipe and getting a meal properly cooked are related but different things. A change in heat or cookware may require a change in timing. Refusing to adjust out of loyalty to the recipe will not cook the food any better. The problem is like the state the meal needs to reach; the method is like the heat and timing. Judge an adjustment by whether it moves the result toward that state.

Making something for other people is trickier than cooking. Whether food is cooked is relatively easy to judge. Whether someone needs your work often requires seeing them use it. So holding a problem steady also requires saying what would count as an answer. With an objective but no way to assess it, persistence can become an exercise in explaining ourselves to ourselves.

Look at the piece of work you keep revising. Can you identify an uncertainty that the last change reduced? If all you can say is that there are more pages or a more complete process, go one step further: who can now complete which task more easily because of those changes?

## Change the approach while carrying the learning forward

Holding a problem steady does not require freezing every action. It requires knowing, before a change, what you are hoping to observe. Without that, it is easy to invent a meaning after the result arrives. Someone likes it, so the direction must be right. Nobody uses it, so the market must not be ready. Whatever happens, the original judgment remains untouched.

For Viaweb, inviting merchants to use the software and building stores for them addressed overlapping questions, but not identical ones. The second action could reveal obstacles in the store-building process. It could not also establish that merchants were willing to operate the software independently. Carrying learning forward includes being clear about what remains unknown.

I would keep a plain record of each attempt: what was uncertain beforehand, what changed, what was actually observed, and what was not observed. Those few lines can be harder to evade than a polished review. They require putting the result beside the original question, rather than selecting only the most flattering part.

There is no need to pretend this is a controlled experiment. In real projects, users, timing, and the work itself often change together. One event following another does not establish causation. If several things changed, record that several things changed and that you cannot yet tell which mattered. Preserving uncertainty is more useful than saving a convenient explanation as a lesson.

Sometimes a question can also be addressed by making one change and using the result repeatedly. In his [account of CD Baby's shipping email](https://sive.rs/cdbe), Derek Sivers describes an initially ordinary automated notification. He later felt it did not fit his wish to make people smile, so he rewrote it as an exaggerated, playful message that went out with every order.

The expression changed; the task remained telling customers that their orders had shipped. Sivers says some customers enjoyed the email enough to post and share it. He also credits it with bringing in new customers. But this founder's retrospective provides neither a comparison nor a method for measuring customer acquisition, so we cannot calculate the additional customers attributable to the email.

For me, the useful reminder is that recurring content does not always need to be created again. Sometimes the version already written should stay in use long enough to reveal how people respond. Asking AI to generate a new email every time might keep the content changing while making it harder to understand what people are responding to.

Keeping a version in use does not make it untouchable. If customers cannot understand it, information becomes outdated, or feedback reveals an omission, revision makes sense. What matters is having a reason for the change. A revision should inherit what is already known about the problem. A more appealing new version is insufficient reason to discard the old one together with everything learned from its use.

This also changes how I think about daily habits. In a [study published in 2010](https://transformationweightcontrol.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Lally-2010-How-Habits-are-Formed.pdf), Lally and colleagues asked 96 volunteers to choose a health behavior and repeat it daily in the same context over 84 days. They used self-report questionnaires to track how far the action became automatic, requiring less deliberate thought. The 96 were enrolled participants: 82 provided enough data, the researchers could describe 62 people's records with a curve showing change over time, and that curve matched 39 people's records well. From those curves, the researchers estimated gradual change and substantial differences in the time people might need; some estimates extended beyond the 84 days observed and were not directly observed results.

This small study concerned behaviors such as eating, drinking, and physical activity. It did not test the success of AI projects. Keeping the circumstances for starting work consistent is my suggestion to try, not a work method tested by this study. Whether a particular solution is correct still requires feedback of its own.

You can keep a regular occasion for working on a problem while allowing the method to change from day to day. Becoming more practiced at starting work does not require treating a particular answer as permanently correct. A stable routine can support changes in judgment.

That asks more of us than either starting over every day or repeating the same process every day: remember what you are trying to find out. AI can help propose approaches and organize records, but you need to be able to explain which piece of feedback changed your understanding. Where you cannot, leave the question open.

Files can be copied unchanged; understanding needs conditions attached before it can be reused. Some things learned from building stores for Viaweb's merchants would need checking again when the task was for merchants to operate the software themselves. Changes in the user, task, or conditions of participation should be marked. Otherwise, even within the same project, you can end up treating a changed problem as an old one.

## Leave a record you can use again today

New projects keep appearing. Before the current question has had a chance to receive an answer, your attention may already have moved on. In a [short essay from 2009](https://sive.rs/hellyeah), Derek Sivers recommends that people who are already overcommitted or scattered turn down new commitments they do not feel strongly drawn to. This is a personal decision method for a stated audience, rather than an experimental finding.

I would use it before accepting a new task, without using it to judge every action in an existing one. A problem you have chosen to address can involve boredom, frustration, and waiting. Rechecking your excitement at every step makes it hard to give that problem a stable place in your work.

Nor does this require waiting indefinitely. If resources can no longer bear the cost, the task carries an unacceptable consequence, or feedback indicates that the problem itself is not worth solving, reduce the scope or stop. Saving patience for a question includes allowing a well-founded negative answer. Carry that answer into your next choice, and the work has still left something useful.

Today, try a half-hour exercise. It does not require a new project or a system for taking notes. Take a piece of work already in progress and use an ordinary, single-page document for the following actions. Half an hour is my suggested limit to make starting manageable, not a research-established optimum.

1. Write down whom the work should help and what task it should help them complete. Choose one thing you can currently examine, setting distant ambitions aside. Remove the name of your current tool from the sentence and see whether the problem still holds.
2. Look at the most recent revision and write what you intended to improve. If you left no record of that intention, write that you do not know. Do not turn a reason you have thought of today into something you supposedly knew at the time.
3. Use what remains unclear to choose what you want to observe next, then decide what to change. You can ask AI for possible approaches, but select an action that addresses the current uncertainty and can be advanced within the half-hour limit.
4. If an appropriate user is available, ask for consent and have them carry out the actual task. Record observable actions. If nobody is using the work yet, record the results of technical checks and leave the question of demand open. Your own sense that it is convenient does not substitute for someone else's use.
5. Append what you actually observed to the original question. State what to retain, what to change, and what remains unanswered. If you cannot yet judge, identify the missing information and the conditions for checking again. There is no need to rush to declare either success or failure.

The page's neatness is not the test. When you return to the work, can you say where the next attempt should begin without having to guess the entire direction again? If you can, the record is starting to help. The end of one attempt has become a usable starting point for another.

If writing reveals that you have been holding onto a tool without clarifying whom it should help do what, that is useful too. You have found where to stop polishing. Next, you can investigate the task or honestly end the project. Generating a few more pages is unnecessary to maintain a feeling of progress.

I plan to begin holding myself to this record. The next time I want to change my approach, I will carry the unanswered question with me. The next time I decide to stop, I will also keep the observations that led to that decision.

Open that piece of work now and write the first sentence: whom should it help, and what task should it help them complete? Leave out the tool's name for the moment.
