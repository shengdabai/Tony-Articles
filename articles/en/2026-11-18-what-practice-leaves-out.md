# What Your Practice Leaves Out

> 发布日期：2026-11-18 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-18-题目没问到的事.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-18-what-practice-leaves-out.md)

---

Getting fluent at something and learning everything important about it are different achievements. The first means an activity is becoming easier. The second quietly makes a much larger claim: that we have encountered the important questions surrounding it.

I believe in practicing thoroughly. But I want to add a requirement to my own learning: the more effective a set of exercises becomes, the more reason I have to check occasionally what it never asks. We can get better at answering selected questions while overlooking matters that have never made it into a question. The answers may be perfectly fine.

A clipped summary about AI recommendations and increasingly similar choices raises a question closer to learning: when we ask AI to organize our practice, do we also hand its practice plan the power to decide what deserves our attention? The clipped summary prompts the question; it does not establish that AI-organized practice necessarily narrows our choices.

## A question draws a boundary around attention

Start with a shipping confirmation. The order has shipped, the order number is included, and the customer gets a thank-you. The information has been delivered. This is something we can do correctly, and correctness should come first.

In a [2025 interview](https://sive.rs/2025-06-frank), CD Baby founder Derek Sivers recalled replacing a dry shipping email with a deliberately extravagant little story: the CD rests on a satin pillow and departs on a private jet. He said he wanted to make the recipient smile, and that people subsequently shared the email. This is a founder looking back, without a controlled comparison establishing how much business the email generated.

The interesting part is how differently we could practice with this material. We could work on making the email shorter, clearer, or more polite. We could also ask what additional purpose an email might serve once it has successfully communicated that an order has shipped.

Change the question and you change the activity. The first kind of practice directs attention toward missing information. The second directs attention toward an experience that might be created within an ordinary interaction. Both can be worthwhile.

The trouble begins when completing the first exercise feels like mastering the whole task. The sentences flow, the information is complete, and there appears to be nothing left to think about. Meeting the requirements becomes the end of thought.

That possible early closure concerns me. Someone may have plenty of imagination but keep answering questions that never require them to use it. Conversely, requiring humor in every notification would overlook accuracy, the recipient's mood, and the occasion.

A set of exercises works like a flashlight. It can make one part of a room very clear without illuminating the whole room. Making the beam brighter and pointing it somewhere else are different actions. Learning likewise needs to distinguish reinforcing selected material from reconsidering what was selected.

Rooms have walls, and learning needs boundaries too. I do not expect to learn everything. I want to avoid treating the clarity of an illuminated area as evidence that nothing remains elsewhere.

## Remembering more securely is different from encountering more

Specific research supports the value of practice for memory. In 2008, Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger had university students learn foreign-language word pairs. They compared continued study and continued testing after an initial correct recall. [Their experiment](https://educationgroup.mit.edu/HHMIEducationGroup/wp-content/uploads/2011/04/14-Karpicke-Roediger-2008.pdf) found that continuing to produce answers from memory substantially helped recall a week later; simply continuing to look at successfully recalled pairs did not produce the same benefit. This was a vocabulary-memory task, not a measure of creative work.

The practical lesson for me is clear: finding something familiar while looking at it and being able to recall it without the material deserve separate checks. Repeated answering also has a prerequisite: the exercises need to contain the things you need to remember.

In [research published in 2010](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf), Andrew Butler had university students read passages, then compared repeated testing with repeated reading. A week later, benefits from testing extended to new inference questions: students had to use a learned fact or concept to work out an answer they had not practiced directly. These were tasks with defined materials and scoring criteria, rather than a test of all real-world skills.

The paper also contains a result that is easy to miss. Pooling the first three experiments, the researcher examined control questions—material covered in the passages but not specifically targeted during practice. The proportion answered correctly was 35% after rereading the passages and 23% after repeated testing.

What could account for this difference? The rereading condition encountered the whole passage again; the testing condition mainly worked with selected facts and concepts. This comparison includes a difference in exposure. It does not establish that testing damages other memories, or that everything left unpracticed will deteriorate.

It reminds me to consider the benefits of a learning method together with its coverage. For selected material, repeatedly producing an answer may beat rereading. For material that was not selected, rereading at least provides another encounter.

Both can be true. We do not need to credit a useful method with solving problems it was never designed to address.

Return to the shipping email. Repeatedly practicing concision may help someone write clearer, shorter notifications. But repeating that exercise does not necessarily add another requirement: considering whether the interaction could be enjoyable for the customer. Proficiency addresses the question the exercise asks. Someone still needs to notice questions the exercise leaves out.

## A successful example contains places to ask a different question

The theatrical details of Sivers' email are memorable. Pillows and private jets are easier to remember than an order number. But if the lesson becomes adding a little exaggeration to a notification, we have acquired another ready-made answer.

I find it more useful to return to the moment before the change. The original email already performed its basic function. Why change it at all? Sivers explained in the interview that the interaction was an opportunity to make someone happy.

That reason changes the question. Clarity still matters. Whether an interaction makes someone feel thoughtfully treated also enters consideration. Only then do we choose the language, without needing to start by imitating a particular style.

What I want to learn from this example is how to notice a useful purpose that the task did not explicitly require. That is harder than remembering a writing technique. Nobody has drawn a blank on the page telling us to insert creativity here.

This places a condition on AI-generated practice. If a tool merely breaks an existing outline into more questions, the number of exercises can increase without broadening their scope. A purpose missing from the outline may remain missing. A different tone or a few additional examples may leave that unchanged.

Counting more headings will not tell us whether coverage has expanded. We need to examine whether a new question calls for a different judgment: are we refining details within the existing requirements, or reconsidering whom the task serves and what else it could accomplish?

Of course, entertainment does not belong in every notification. Sivers himself noted that this was a music store where a lighter touch was appropriate. Carrying the same joke into a situation requiring a serious explanation could frustrate a recipient who just wants to understand the information quickly.

The useful exercise is therefore to recognize when an additional purpose has value and when to let it go. Memorizing what worked in a successful case remains different from knowing when to avoid copying it.

## Focused practice is useful when its scope is explicit

Someone might object that beginners cannot even perform the basic actions yet. How are they supposed to audit omissions? I agree with the concern. When the basics are unstable, narrowing a task and developing reliable actions can be more useful than continually seeking fresh angles.

If the goal is an exam with a defined syllabus, or an operation with fixed requirements, practicing those requirements intensively makes sense. Every session need not become an open-ended exploration. Exploration consumes time too.

We need to be clear about the promise being made. Learning a specified set of actions is a testable goal. Independently handling the important issues surrounding a kind of work is a much broader one. The broader goal calls for an additional check of whether the exercises cover the essential parts of that work.

AI assistance deserves the same distinction. Bastani and colleagues conducted a [mathematics-learning experiment](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf) at a high school in Turkey in fall 2023. They compared a standard chat-style tool, a tutor incorporating teacher-designed guidance, and no AI access. The teacher-designed prompts told the tutor to offer hints so students could continue solving a problem, instead of giving the full solution. The study measured short-term mathematics performance in a particular learning environment with particular tool designs.

The standard version improved performance during assisted practice, but students subsequently performed worse than the no-AI group on an unaided exam. The tutor with those prompts and other safeguards largely removed that disadvantage, yet the study did not find better unaided exam performance than in the no-AI group.

That makes me take tool design seriously while keeping expectations precise. Reducing a particular loss and developing every capability we want require different evidence. Whether students became better at identifying a worthwhile new question was not answered by this experiment.

For an ordinary learner, this does not require building an elaborate new learning system. It is already useful to state what the current practice is supposed to accomplish, then watch for anything essential that still needs attention beyond that stated goal.

Checking coverage is not a demand for completeness. Someone writing an email need not study every school of writing. They may, however, need to notice whether they have practiced sentences while neglecting what the recipient needs. We are looking for relevant omissions, rather than pursuing everything novel.

## Let yourself add a question when practice ends

Here is a question I want to leave at the end of practice: in a recent task that went smoothly, was there an important purpose that never entered the exercise requirements?

Important is the operative word. Missing a rhetorical technique may not affect the work. Missing what the recipient needs to do with the information could matter a great deal. Whether a question deserves adding depends on the task, rather than how original it sounds.

I plan to try the following sequence:

1. Choose a learning resource I have just used and write down what its existing exercises actually require me to do. Describe those requirements in my own words, then check against the material. For a shipping email, completeness and clarity are both requirements we can examine.
2. Return to the complete material, identify one question absent from the exercises, and explain its connection to the task. For this public example, we could ask what purpose the interaction might usefully serve beyond announcing shipment. AI can suggest candidate questions, but those candidates need checking against the material for support and relevance to the task at hand.
3. Carry the added question into the next task of the same kind and record whether it changes how I handle the work. If it merely produces another vague sentence, revise the question. If it changes a substantive choice about the content, decide whether to include it in future practice.

I am not presenting this as a validated learning program. The research above provides separate evidence about memory, inference in specified tasks, and the importance of tool design. Connecting those findings into a coverage check is a method I intend to try.

It does not require finding an omission every time. Returning to the material may show that the current exercises are sufficient. Then keep practicing. There is no need to manufacture a new question to appear thoughtful.

But when an important omission does emerge, we do not have to turn it immediately into another fixed answer. We can preserve it as a question that requires judgment in context. A different recipient or purpose may rightly call for a different answer.

Next time you finish practicing, leave a little time for what the questions never asked.
