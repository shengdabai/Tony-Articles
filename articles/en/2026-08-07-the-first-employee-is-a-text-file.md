# The First Employee Is a Text File

> 发布日期:2026-08-07 · [中文](../zh/2026-08-07-第一个员工是一份文本文件.md) | [English](../en/2026-08-07-the-first-employee-is-a-text-file.md)

---

The most important AI employee most people can create today does not have a face, a salary, a calendar, or a seat in a meeting.

It is a text file.

That sounds too small, almost disappointing. After all the noise around agents, automation, tool use, and "AI teams," a plain file feels like the least futuristic object in the room. But that smallness is exactly the point. The revolution does not begin when a machine becomes dramatic. It begins when part of your judgment can leave your head, enter a stable form, and improve after every use.

For a long time, personal ability lived mostly inside the person. You had taste because you had seen enough examples. You knew how to write because you had suffered through enough sentences. You knew how to solve problems because your nervous system had absorbed patterns that were hard to explain. That kind of ability is real, but it is also fragile. It travels poorly. It does not scale well. It disappears when you are tired. It becomes vague the moment someone asks, "How exactly do you do that?"

AI changes the bargain.

Not because AI magically understands you. Not because a better model will read your soul and become your perfect assistant. The change is more practical: more of your working method can now be written down, loaded, tested, criticized, edited, and reused.

A text file can say: when I ask for an article, do not flatter the idea too early. First find the tension. Separate the private source from the public argument. Check whether the theme has already been used. If the piece relies on a private detail, throw it away. If the ending only repeats the opening, rewrite it. If the output looks smooth but has no risk, push harder.

That file is not "a prompt" in the shallow sense. It is a small piece of operational memory. It is a reusable fragment of judgment.

## The Old Way Was Exhausting

Most people still use AI as if every conversation starts from zero.

They open a chat box, explain the background again, describe their taste again, correct the same mistake again, and then wonder why AI feels powerful but strangely tiring. The machine can produce a lot, but the human has to keep rebuilding the invisible workplace around it.

This is why many people feel the paradox: AI saves time, yet their work does not get meaningfully better.

The reason is simple. They are using AI to generate output, but not using AI to preserve learning.

If every correction dies inside one chat, the system never gets stronger. If every preference must be re-explained, the human remains the bottleneck. If every task depends on today's mood, energy, patience, and memory, the workflow still belongs to the old world. The only difference is that the old world now has faster autocomplete.

The first real upgrade is not a more clever prompt. It is the moment you ask:

What do I keep repeating that should become part of the system?

That question sounds boring. It is not. It is the doorway from using a tool to building a capability.

## A Good Agent Is a Boundary, Not a Personality

The word "agent" tempts people to imagine a little person inside the computer. That image is useful for five minutes and dangerous after that. Once we imagine personality too strongly, we start caring about the wrong things: names, avatars, clever greetings, human-like banter.

But a useful agent is not primarily a personality. It is a boundary.

It knows what job it is responsible for. It knows what it should refuse. It knows which tools it may use. It knows what evidence counts. It knows when to stop. It knows what kind of output is acceptable.

This is not just metaphor. In the OpenAI Agents SDK documentation, an agent is described through concrete parts such as instructions, tools, and handoffs: instructions shape behavior, tools let the agent take action, and handoffs let one agent pass work to another specialized agent.[^1][^2][^3] Different frameworks use different names, but the deeper pattern is stable: capability becomes useful when it has a role, context, allowed actions, and a feedback loop.

That is why the text file matters.

It is the place where the boundary becomes explicit. Before the file exists, your standard is only a feeling. After the file exists, your standard has a surface. You can inspect it. You can argue with it. You can version it. You can notice that one rule is too vague, another is too strict, and a third is missing the real failure mode.

This is also why "just tell AI what you want" is not enough. Most people do not know what they want at the level of execution. They know the emotional result: make this better, clearer, more useful, more human. But they have not decomposed their standard into repeatable moves.

The hard work is not writing a magical instruction.

The hard work is discovering your own operating system.

## Text Is Cheap Enough to Iterate

A strange advantage of text is that it is almost embarrassingly easy to change.

If a human team member misunderstands a task, correction is expensive. You need a conversation, emotional calibration, patience, context, perhaps several rounds of alignment. If an organization changes its workflow, there are meetings, training, politics, and inertia.

A text file has no ego.

You can delete a bad rule. You can add an example. You can tighten a refusal condition. You can split one overloaded role into two smaller roles. You can tell the system to copy the final result somewhere, check a source before claiming a fact, or stop when a privacy boundary appears. Then you can run the workflow again and see whether the behavior improved.

This does not make the system perfect. It makes improvement cheap.

That distinction matters. A lot of AI discourse is trapped between two lazy extremes. One side imagines autonomous agents that will soon do everything. The other side dismisses the whole thing because today's agents still fail in obvious ways. Both miss the ordinary engineering path: make the loop smaller, make the failure visible, change the instruction, test again.

The first text-file employee is not impressive because it is brilliant. It is impressive because it is editable.

Human habits are hard to edit. Human memory is hard to diff. Human intention is hard to reproduce. But a file can be compared against yesterday's version. A rule can be removed. A checklist can be tightened. A bad assumption can be replaced by a better one.

This is how personal growth becomes less mystical.

Instead of saying "I need to become more disciplined," you can ask: which decision should no longer depend on my mood? Instead of saying "I need to write better," you can ask: which recurring mistake should my writing system catch before publication? Instead of saying "I need to learn AI," you can ask: what repeated judgment can I externalize, run, and improve this week?

That is engineering past the boundary of the self.

## The Objection Is Real

There is a serious objection here: if you externalize too much, do you become weaker?

Yes, if you use AI as a dumping ground for responsibility.

If the machine thinks, decides, writes, edits, remembers, and judges while you only click approve, your ability will atrophy. You may produce more artifacts, but you will own less of the capability. The danger is not that AI will replace you in one dramatic moment. The danger is that you will slowly stop practicing the parts of yourself that made your work worth doing.

So the test is not whether AI makes a task easier.

The test is whether AI makes your next task larger.

Used badly, AI reduces friction by removing thought. Used well, AI reduces friction around the lower layers so you can climb into harder work. You stop spending energy retyping the same context, but you spend more energy sharpening standards. You stop doing the repetitive format cleanup, but you become more responsible for the argument. You stop relying on memory, but you become stricter about what deserves to be remembered.

This is the difference between outsourcing ability and compounding ability.

Outsourcing says: do this instead of me.

Compounding says: capture how I want this done, help me test it, and make the next version stronger.

The surface may look similar. Both involve AI. Both may produce a document, a plan, a script, or an email. But internally they move in opposite directions. One makes the human thinner. The other gives the human a larger working body.

## The Zero Class Starts With Its Own Rules

The real divide in the AI era will not be between people who use AI and people who do not. That line is already too crude. Most people will use AI somehow.

The deeper divide will be between people who borrow generic intelligence and people who build situated intelligence.

Generic intelligence is what everyone gets when they open a model and ask a question. It is powerful, but it does not know your work, your standards, your history, your taste, your constraints, your refusal lines, or the strange connections that make your thinking yours.

Situated intelligence is different. It is AI placed inside a working context. It carries your notes, your rules, your examples, your failures, your vocabulary, your checks, your preferred forms of evidence. It is not merely smarter. It is closer to the work.

That is why the first employee is a text file.

Not because files are glamorous, but because they are the most accessible bridge between human judgment and machine execution. A file can carry a role. A file can carry a memory. A file can carry a standard. A file can teach the next run what the previous run learned.

The people who understand this will build small systems around themselves. At first those systems will look humble: a few instructions, a few scripts, a few saved examples, a few checks that prevent familiar mistakes. Then the pieces will start to connect. One file becomes a role. One role becomes a workflow. One workflow becomes a personal operating system. The person does not disappear. The person gets a larger surface area for action.

This is what I mean when I say AI should make people stronger, not lazier.

The goal is not to have a machine that writes like you while you slowly vanish. The goal is to make your judgment more explicit, more testable, more portable, and more alive.

The future does not arrive first as a robot colleague with a perfect voice.

It arrives as a sentence you write down because you are tired of making the same correction twice.

Then you run it.

Then you improve it.

Then one day you realize the file is no longer just a file. It is a piece of you that learned how to work without waiting for you to remember.
