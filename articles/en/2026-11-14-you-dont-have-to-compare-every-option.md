# You Don't Have to Compare Every Option

> 发布日期:2026-11-14 · [中文](../zh/2026-11-14-你不必比较所有方案.md) | [English](../en/2026-11-14-you-dont-have-to-compare-every-option.md)

---

AI has already given you a pile of options. Why are you still unable to choose?

My short answer: some decisions don't require you to compare every option. For an affordable, reversible attempt, it is worth trying a different approach: define what would count as good enough, check one option at a time, and act when one meets those conditions. Getting faster at reading every answer may not be the improvement you need.

This is a practical problem. AI can keep generating, but your work doesn't end when generation stops. Every proposal still needs to be understood, checked, and weighed. There may be fewer people doing the production while more things wait for a decision. The desk looks busy. The work hasn't moved.

## Extra options can keep you occupied, too

Among today's notes is a saved summary of an entrepreneurship article. It places the bottleneck of working with AI in human decision making: machines can produce plenty, but someone still has to choose and bear the consequences. I agree with that concern. I am less willing to jump from it to the conclusion that our capacity to decide is fixed and we can only grit our teeth and carry more.

Something is missing: how the work is handed to us.

Consider those neatly arranged comparison tables. Each option has strengths and weaknesses, and AI can even score them across several dimensions. But the basis for those scores may need its own investigation. If the scores still rest on the same unchecked descriptions, the table has organized judgments without supplying evidence. After reading it, you still have to ask what justifies it.

One proposal asks whether it will work. A set of proposals also asks you to explain why you chose this one over the others. Sometimes that is necessary. Sometimes it quietly changes the task: you began by looking for something workable and ended up feeling obliged to prove it was the best. Proving that requires knowing much more.

Suppose you are buying a chair for your desk. Comfortable, the right size, and within budget might be enough. But as the shopping page keeps recommending similar models, you start comparing armrest angles, fabrics, and long customer reviews. You have added choices without necessarily learning how the chair will feel to sit in.

Your requirements for the chair correspond to the conditions an approach must meet. Product pages correspond to candidate proposals; sitting down in the chair corresponds to testing one. The shared mechanism is that descriptions can multiply while decisive information requires a different kind of action. Of course, being able to try a chair doesn't mean every task permits inexpensive mistakes. Commitments that cannot be undone need different treatment.

That is why I see part of the burden of deciding as a consequence of how work is delivered. Does AI hand you a batch of candidates to rank, or a recommendation with clear conditions that you can immediately test? The answer changes how much work remains. This is my inference, to be checked against real tasks, not a new law about the capacity of the human brain.

## A good-enough answer can go first to the test

There is serious research behind the idea of proceeding without comparing every option.

In their [joint paper published in 2009](https://edbatista.com/wp-content/uploads/files/conditions-for-intuitive-expertise-kahneman-klein.pdf), Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein discuss research on decisions made by professionals such as fireground commanders. Commanders often identify a plausible course of action and mentally simulate whether it will work. If they find a problem, they modify it; if they cannot readily modify it, they move to another option. This describes judgment in particular professional settings. The authors synthesize existing research here; they did not run a new experiment testing whether everyone should compare less.

What interests me is where the effort goes: into checking an action. Relevant consequences are still considered, but there is no requirement to rank every alternative first. The question narrows from which option is absolutely best to whether this one can work under the present conditions.

But why trust the first recommendation? The conditions in the same paper matter: the environment must contain learnable regularities, and the person must have enough practice and feedback. Feeling certain is unreliable. An experienced person recognizing a workable response in a familiar task is very different from a beginner approving an AI answer because it reads smoothly.

My practical takeaway is to establish how an option will be checked before deciding whether comparisons can be reduced. A recommendation is not ready for testing if it cannot explain the conditions it depends on, where it is most likely to fail, and whether a failure can be reversed. It may sound attractive. That doesn't make it good enough yet.

The conditions themselves need care. Looking reasonable is insufficient; they should specify observable results and acceptable costs. Without that constraint, finding something good enough can become an exercise in justifying the first appealing answer. Write the conditions before reviewing candidates, rather than lowering them once you have a favorite.

Good enough doesn't mean steadily reducing your expectations, either. Some requirements must hold at the same time. A dazzling strength in one area cannot compensate for a serious failure in another. Checking conditions that would disqualify an action before considering its extra benefits can prevent you from spending a great deal of effort praising something you cannot use. That order follows from the task's constraints, not from the confidence of AI's recommendation.

## Order the unknowns before ranking the options

In his [2011 account of starting Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/), founder Joel Gascoigne describes a small beginning. He wanted to make queuing tweets for publication work well. First, he used a simple two-page entry point to see whether people would leave their email addresses. He then inserted a pricing page to see which plans people clicked, before building a working version. He reports getting his first paying customer within four days of launch. This is a founder's retrospective: it illustrates a sequence of exploration, not the probability that a typical product will succeed.

The most useful part isn't the small number of pages.

Leaving an email address answers whether someone wants to learn more. Clicking a paid plan adds a clue about willingness to accept a price. Actually paying goes a step further. These forms of evidence cannot stand in for one another. A promising signal at one stage doesn't automatically settle the questions that follow.

More interestingly, a pricing page can challenge an optimistic interpretation: people willing to leave an email address may not remain interested after seeing the price. The test can distinguish behaviors that were previously lumped together. It doesn't make every decision for the founder, but it can make the next one less dependent on imagination.

Before development, Gascoigne did not need to compare every possible combination of features. The immediate unknown was smaller: would anyone approach the idea, and would they continue after seeing a price? A test matters when it can change the investment that follows. Making a complete product more polished might not replace that check.

There is an easily missed distinction here. An action worth taking first is not necessarily an action worth immediately scaling up. A small test only needs to answer the question in front of it. Requiring it to establish long-term retention, reliable revenue, and everyone's needs all at once stuffs a small decision back into an enormous comparison.

Ordering unknowns has conditions, too. Testing a shared assumption first may reduce work on several candidates when they all depend on that same unconfirmed assumption. If they address entirely different people and require different investments, each needs its own basis. Forcing different directions into the same small test can erase the differences that matter to the choice.

In his [2013 essay](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html), Paul Graham discusses doing work manually before automating it. His rationale is that actually doing things for users reveals details about what needs to be built. This is advice based on startup cases, limited by a perspective that includes successful companies; it cannot establish that intensive manual service necessarily causes success.

Applied to the Buffer question, that advice adds a step. AI can do more than write another complete proposal: it can help identify the missing information and the small action that could supply it. The decision maker may then receive less material, with a clearer next step. What should shrink is the collection of candidates awaiting comparison, not our knowledge of the facts.

## Some decisions deserve more comparison

Someone might object: if we only check the first good-enough option, won't we always choose something familiar and miss a better path?

We might. That is particularly true when the conditions for acceptance are wrong, or when different directions have fundamentally different consequences. Sequential checking is affected by sequence. If AI always recommends the same approach first and the same standards always let it through, we may simply have hidden a preference inside a procedure.

For choices that are difficult to reverse or create long-term commitments, I would retain the requirement for careful comparison. Find at least one alternative that differs in how it works, and explain what each direction gives up. Consult someone with relevant experience when necessary. Three versions of the wording of one proposal do not amount to three choices.

The depth of comparison should not be determined solely by the money involved today. A seemingly convenient choice may require continuing accommodations later; a somewhat harder attempt may leave you with something easy to replace. Include maintenance and switching burdens. Being able to start now and being able to leave later are separate checks.

In his [2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders), Jeff Bezos advocates lighter processes for reversible decisions and emphasizes quickly recognizing and correcting mistakes. The letter was published the following year. It reflects his management experience, rather than an experimental threshold applicable to everyone. What matters to me is adapting the process to the nature of the decision, instead of assigning every decision the same weight.

Reversibility still requires someone who can make the correction, a way to detect mistakes promptly, and costs you can afford. Deleting an option from a document doesn't undo a promise made to someone else. Without the ability to discover and correct errors, reversibility on paper is insufficient.

Conditions for acceptance can also change, but the reason should be recorded. New evidence may show that an original requirement was unsuitable. That is different from relaxing a requirement merely because the first proposal failed it: that lets the recommendation determine its own standard. The simplest way to distinguish the two is to preserve the original conditions, then identify the new fact that changed them.

There is another case: your purpose is to learn. In the [2025 version of their paper on learning to code](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09047v2), Matthias Lehmann, Philipp B. Cornelius, and Fabian J. Sting report no statistically significant overall learning effect from AI access in two experiments with university students: the observed differences were not sufficient to rule out random variation as an explanation. Their research plans had been registered before the experiments. Subsequent exploratory analyses suggest that asking for solutions and asking for explanations may have different implications for progress through material and understanding. The experiments took place in 2024 using the models available then; differences in usage found in exploratory analyses should not be treated as equivalent to causal results from random assignment.

The reminder is specific. When the immediate goal is completion, finding an acceptable action early may reduce effort. When the goal is developing your judgment, comparing two directions may itself be part of the work. Those goals need different standards. Before saving yourself some thinking, check whether you are also removing the learning you wanted.

I don't want to describe comparison as waste. It can expose a tradeoff: what a benefit costs, and when that cost becomes unacceptable. The work to reduce is ranking that has no bearing on the present action. When a difference could change a commitment, a capability, or the range of choices available later, slowing down to understand it is still a responsible thing to do.

## Leave less unresolved in your next request

You can try this today without building an elaborate process. Choose an affordable, reversible task whose purpose is completion rather than systematic training. Replace a request for a pile of proposals with a small, ordered test.

1. Write the conditions for acceptance first. Record the result you need, the cost you can afford, and the consequences you cannot accept. If you cannot yet state the conditions, get more information.
2. Ask AI to recommend one approach worth checking first, together with its assumptions, likely failure points, and the finding that would make it recommend something else.
3. Before increasing your investment, take the smallest action that can test the key unknown. Compare what happens with what you expected. Continue if it meets the conditions; if a problem appears, see whether it can be repaired, and change approaches if it cannot.
4. Keep a brief record. Note what you read and asked in order to decide, how many things remain unresolved, and whether rework followed. If later costs rise, revise the conditions or return to fuller comparison.

I cannot promise that this will help you. It is worth trying because it puts the prospect of reducing effort in a working method you can change, while preserving a chance to discover that you were wrong. A different kind of task could produce the opposite result.

Count the whole cost when you check. Faster approval followed by more rework merely shifts the burden downstream. Reading fewer pages while leaving a critical condition unchecked doesn't lighten the task, either. To compare the approaches, include reading before the decision, corrections during execution, and maintenance afterward. Only then can you see what was saved.

I plan to watch one question: what new information about the candidates on my desk could still change the choice? When I cannot answer that, more generation often just adds reading. When I can, the next move is to obtain that information. To me, that looks more like a skill I can practice than a demand to read everything faster.

Next time you receive a pile of proposals, leave yourself time to test the one that matters most. Don't rush to finish reading them all.
