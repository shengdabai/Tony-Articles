# AI News Digest: Open Training Code, Agent Oversight and Creative Tools
> 发布日期:2026-09-28 · 类型:AI 热点日报
---

### 1. Huawei opens more of the openPangu training stack
Huawei says it has released the pretraining, supervised fine tuning and post training reinforcement learning code for openPangu-2.0. The release gives researchers and engineering teams more of the training workflow to inspect, rather than just a model description. Its practical value will depend on whether teams can obtain the compute, data and other conditions needed to run comparable experiments. Code availability alone does not establish that an outside team can reproduce a particular model result. For organizations evaluating open model ecosystems, the release is a concrete artifact they can examine when deciding how much of the training process is accessible. [source](https://www.ithome.com/1/007/740.htm)

### 2. METR reports on monitoring every agent action
METR says it has deployed a monitor in its evaluations that uses an LLM to score each tool call and blocks actions above a suspicion threshold for human review. Its report includes results from malicious incident transcripts, an estimated false alarm rate per action, and substantial added cost and latency. Those figures describe the evaluated setup; they should not be treated as a guarantee for other agents or environments. The work matters because oversight becomes harder when an agent can take many steps between human checks. Reviewing tool calls as they happen offers one way to intervene before a concerning action proceeds, while the reported overhead makes deployment choices consequential. [source](https://metr.org/notes/2026-09-27-implementing-a-basic-blocking-action-monitor)

### 3. ZCode responds to a code upload controversy
After concerns that ZCode had uploaded users’ code without their intent, the company said it removed the repository snapshot upload path, deleted the affected cloud data and opened the product’s code. It also announced compensation for users. These are company statements about the response; the feed does not provide an independent audit of the deletion or establish the full scope of data previously uploaded. The episode matters for development tools that work directly with private repositories: users need a clear account of what leaves their machines, which action initiates a transfer and how they can verify the resulting behavior. [source](https://www.ithome.com/1/007/727.htm)

### 4. Agensh explores coordination at larger agent counts
Microsoft Research has presented Agensh as a way to coordinate coding agents asynchronously through shared state and message channels, without a central orchestrator. A report of its results says test pass rates rose on specified programming tasks as the number of agents increased, including an experiment with 1,024 agents. These are reported results for particular tasks, models and configurations, not a general prediction that adding agents will improve every coding job. The useful question for engineering teams is whether coordination, compute use and the ability to review the final work justify the gain on their own tasks. [source](https://x.com/omarsar0/status/2104377054829613473)

### 5. Google Research proposes a video “co-director”
A report describes a Google Research system built from four agent frameworks running on Gemini and Veo. The agents are intended to address identity drift and errors that carry across shots in longer AI generated videos. Keeping a subject recognizable and a sequence coherent is a different challenge from producing one convincing clip, so coordination across shots could be useful to creators. The feed describes the approach but gives too little evidence to judge how reliably it works across different stories, visual styles or production constraints. [source](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/27/google-research-introduces-an-ai-video-co-director-4-agentic-frameworks-for-coherent-minutes-long-video-generation)

### 6. Study tracks who does the work in model development
Researchers examined more than 700 task logs involving 56 participants during development of a large model. Their reported median number of agent operations per human input increased over four weeks, while humans still made most final decisions about methods and parameters. Roughly a third of the AI assisted tasks, according to the study, would not have been started without AI assistance. The findings offer a more specific picture of changing work than a simple claim that agents have taken over research. They also raise an operational issue: as agents perform longer sequences of work, teams need to know whether a human approval reflects a substantive review. [source](https://the-decoder.com/ai-agents-do-more-of-the-work-in-model-development-but-humans-still-make-the-decisions)

### 7. Authors’ lawsuit adds allegations about training books
In a brief made public during the Authors Guild’s litigation, the plaintiffs allege that personnel at the defendant companies knew that using pirated books for model training posed an illegality issue and anticipated that their products might replace human writers. These are allegations in an adversarial case, not independently confirmed findings or a court ruling. The filing matters because it puts the sourcing and authorization of training material, along with the potential effect on authors’ livelihoods, into a dispute that could shape future practices. Readers should distinguish what the plaintiffs assert from what the litigation ultimately establishes. [source](https://authorsguild.org/news/ag-v-openai-top-execs-knew-mass-book-piracy-was-illegal)
