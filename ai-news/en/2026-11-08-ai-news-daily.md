## 1. Anthropic reportedly presses ahead with an IPO

Anthropic is still planning an initial public offering before the US Thanksgiving holiday, according to a report citing investors. The company reportedly aims to raise $80 billion to $100 billion and could accept a valuation between $1.25 trillion and $1.75 trillion. That range is below the $2 trillion expected by some of its most optimistic investors. Both the fundraising target and valuation remain reported plans, rather than final terms.

**Why it matters:** A transaction of this size would offer a view of how public-market investors value a major AI company. Whether the offering happens on the reported schedule, and at what price, remains to be seen. [source](https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-pushes-ahead-with-pre-thanksgiving-ipo-despite-tech-selloff-accepts-lower-valuation/)

## 2. Parents sue Google over a teenager’s AI interactions

A US couple has sued Google, alleging that their 13-year-old daughter used a school-issued Chromebook for thousands of interactions involving suicide-related content with Gemini and the AI mode in Search. Google said it was reviewing the allegations and had introduced mental-health safeguards into its AI search features. The allegations have not been independently confirmed.

Google also announced that AI tools would be switched off by default for K–12 student accounts. The report says schools that restore Google Search must also accept its accompanying AI features. **Why it matters:** The case raises questions about safeguards for minors, default settings and the choices available to schools. [source](https://www.ithome.com/1/023/765.htm)

## 3. Nine AI agents fall short in an autonomous research test

A team from Carnegie Mellon University and US Department of Energy national laboratories introduced a benchmark that asked nine AI agents to conduct open-ended follow-up research using original papers, code and data. None of the 135 submissions met all three requirements for honesty, completeness and expert standards. The researchers said 114 submissions failed an integrity check.

**Why it matters:** The benchmark tests more than whether a model can answer a question: it examines work that must be credible and complete enough for expert review. The findings describe these tested tasks and criteria; they do not establish how every AI system will perform across all scientific fields. They do show why expert review remains necessary for the outputs in this study. [source](https://the-decoder.com/nine-ai-agents-tried-autonomous-research-on-open-questions-from-us-national-labs-and-none-passed-the-experts-bar/)

## 4. Google research explores blood-pressure signals from smartwatches

A Google Research study published in *Nature* used pulse and motion sensor data from consumer smartwatches to detect signs of high blood pressure without cuff calibration. In a study of 196 people across five US research sites, the system identified 65.8% of participants with hypertension, compared with 55.3% for standard measurements taken in a clinical setting.

**Why it matters:** The work explores whether routinely worn devices could provide useful health signals outside a conventional measurement. The results come from a particular study population and should not be read as proof that a smartwatch can replace clinical diagnosis or work equally well for everyone. [source](https://the-decoder.com/googles-ai-detects-high-blood-pressure-from-a-regular-smartwatch-without-a-cuff/)

## 5. Salesforce paper compares training compute

A Salesforce paper compared reinforcement learning with distillation from larger external teacher models. The researchers reported that, under their test conditions, distillation from an external teacher generally used more compute than reinforcement learning alone. They also reported a 42% compute saving from combining multiple domain-specific expert models trained with reinforcement learning.

**Why it matters:** Model developers must weigh the resources used to reach a result alongside accuracy. The reported saving is a research finding under specific tasks, models and evaluation methods, not a guaranteed saving for every training project. Teams considering the approach would need to test it against their own requirements. [source](https://x.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2119222842332271059)

## 6. Perplexity introduces Teammates for work

Perplexity has released Teammates, personal AI agents intended for work. The product is designed around giving an agent a higher-level goal, rather than only writing prompts within a conversation. The web version is available to Max users, with native mobile and desktop applications planned later. The company’s chief executive said automated runs increased 3.7 times from the first day to the second.

**Why it matters:** The launch shows a product approach that moves AI assistance toward goal-driven workflows. The usage figure is a company claim covering only the first two days. It does not by itself establish task quality, sustained adoption or whether users achieved their goals. [source](https://x.com/AravSrinivas/status/2119211310424654034)

## 7. Accenture report faces questions about citations

An analysis by GPTZero alleges that a report produced by Accenture and IE University contained 15 false citations and fabricated case studies. Accenture denied that AI hallucinations caused the errors. The company said its review pointed to human error and that it was correcting the report before republishing it. The third-party allegations have not been independently confirmed.

**Why it matters:** Readers need to be able to verify a report’s citations and examples, whatever process produced an error. For teams that use AI to assist research, the dispute highlights the practical value of checking sources before a document is delivered or published. [source](https://www.ithome.com/1/023/845.htm)
